<h4>Five questions. Any business.</h4>
<p>Most people can tell you what a company sells. Far fewer can tell you who actually pays for it.</p>
<p>This book breaks any business into five questions:</p>
<ul>
<li><b>Customer</b> &mdash; Who actually pays?</li>
<li><b>Value</b> &mdash; What are they paying for?</li>
<li><b>Payment</b> &mdash; How, and when, does the money arrive?</li>
<li><b>Moat</b> &mdash; Why hasn't anyone taken it yet?</li>
<li><b>Fault Line</b> &mdash; Where does it break?</li>
</ul>
<p>Part One builds the five boxes one at a time. Part Two runs eighteen businesses across nine fields through all five: Spotify, Arm, Domino's, Starbucks, Visa, Berkshire Hathaway, Airbnb, Marriott, Red Bull, Patagonia, the NFL, Formula One, Singapore, Dubai, the Netherlands, Israel, Norwegian salmon farming and Iceland's fishing quotas.</p>
<p>A coffee chain and an insurance company turn out to collect money the same way. A semiconductor designer that owns no factory and a hotel group that owns no hotels turn out to be the same structure. The frame does not care what industry you are in.</p>
<p><b>Every figure carries a year and a source.</b> Where a number could not be confirmed in a public source, it was left out rather than estimated, and the book says where those gaps are.</p>
<p>By the end you will be able to read companies that are not in this book. That is the only thing this book is for.</p>

Description

<h4>Why do some companies keep earning, and others stop?</h4>
<p>Book One asked how a business earns. This one asks whether it gets to keep earning.</p>
<p>It opens by grading its own predecessor. Eighteen predictions from Book One, checked against what actually happened: three wrong, three half right, eight right, three too early to call, one that could not be confirmed. The three wrong ones turned out to be wrong in the same place, and that finding shaped the rest of the book.</p>
<p>Then it digs into the two boxes made of time:</p>
<ul>
<li><b>The five types of moat</b> &mdash; economies of scale, network effects, switching costs, intangible assets, cost advantage</li>
<li><b>The five paths a moat breaks along</b> &mdash; regulation changes, technology substitutes, customers move, suppliers revolt, and growth breaking it from the inside</li>
</ul>
<p>Thirty-six businesses, grouped by structure rather than by industry. Shein sits next to Peruvian fishmeal, John Deere next to Adobe, Estonia next to Grameen Bank, Maine lobster next to Manchester United.</p>
<p>Part Three asks why the largest companies rarely last fifty years while shops nobody has heard of pass two hundred. Part Four turns the diagnostic tool around and asks what you would build with it, including the two moats that cost time and decisions rather than capital, and a ninety-day plan with dates on it.</p>
<p><b>The book states its own limits.</b> How far the fact-checking went, where it stopped, and what was never confirmed are all written down inside.</p>
