<h4>The shop with the queue last year is gone. The old one at the end of the same street is still there.</h4>
<p>Which was better at business? That the question is wrong is where this book starts.</p>
<p>A dessert franchise opened its five hundredth store two years after it began. Seven months later there were four hundred and ninety. On the approach to a shrine in Kyoto, a shop has sold one grilled rice cake for a thousand years and is now run by the twenty-fifth generation. Its stated policy is not to make the business bigger.</p>
<p><b>They were not better and worse at the same work. They were doing different work.</b> One sold what people were talking about. The other sold what people keep needing. Talk ends. Need doesn't.</p>
<p>This book measures books, clothes, appliances, food, film and music with the same ruler &mdash; a ruler where thermal underwear and a book on raising a child land in the same column. It builds three sets of words and nothing more:</p>
<ul>
<li><b>Three clocks</b> &mdash; fashion, generation, need. Where a thing is now</li>
<li><b>Four bridges</b> &mdash; repeat, shape, patina, handoff. Where it can go</li>
<li><b>Four paths</b> &mdash; what has happened to things like it before</li>
</ul>
<p>Then it does the part most books skip. <b>It examines what died first</b>, so that the traits the dead ones also had can be struck off the list of reasons the others lived. Good product, people loved it, luck held &mdash; all true of the vanished ones too.</p>
<p>What it arrives at is uncomfortable and useful. <b>Most things that lasted were not planned. They just never disappeared.</b> Which makes this a game that favours whoever started late, because staying is counted from today and whoever started first also goes to zero if they quit.</p>
<p>Twenty chapters. Each one ends with a single line to write down today.</p>
