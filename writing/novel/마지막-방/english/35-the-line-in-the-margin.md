# Chapter 35. The Line in the Margin

> _Previously_ — We carried the plan to the reading room and looked at
> it in the light. Seven circles sat inside one picture. Six cities and
> one nameless place. The lines ran unbroken from start to end, and the
> designer's name was nowhere to be found. We hung the plan back on the
> wall of that small room.

After that, I went to that room often.

Whenever work finished, I'd stop by for a moment. Not because there was anything in particular to do there. I just went to look at the plan. I thought I'd find something new every time, but the first few days that didn't happen. Seven circles, connected lines, a nameless margin. Always the same.

Each time I went into that room, I tried doing something a little different. Some days I just stood and looked, some days I brought a chair and sat. The chair was something I'd brought in new. There'd been no chair in that room originally; I'd carried one in.

"…It's better with a chair," Histor said one day.

"…Yes. My legs hurt from standing."

He laughed shortly at that.

The days had gotten a little cooler in the meantime. I could see the colors of the trees outside the window changing. Every time I sat in that room, I felt that change a little. The room itself stayed the same temperature regardless of season, but only what lay beyond the window kept changing.

The Keeper came into that room one day carrying tea.

"…You'll wear your body out, staying here all the time. Go out for a bit, at least."

"…I'll just look a little longer."

"…All right. But drink the tea before it goes cold."

He said only that and left. I left the cooling cup beside me and went on looking at the plan.

Then, on the third day, Histor called for me.

"…Mone. Come here."

---

He was pointing at the lower right corner of the plan.

"…There's something here."

I went closer and looked. At first I couldn't see anything. I thought it was a stain in the paper.

"…What is it?"

"…Shine the light from the side."

"…How do you know these things?" I asked.

"…I read it in a book once, about how to read old documents. A pressed mark doesn't show under light shone straight on — you have to light it from the side so it casts a shadow, and then it shows."

"…You studied things like that too?"

"…Yes. I always learn how to handle something first. Otherwise I ruin it by accident."

I held a candle low and angled it against the surface of the paper. What had looked like a stain turned into a line. A very fine line. Much thinner and fainter than the other lines on the plan.

I saw something that seemed to click, looking at its position. It was right beside that small, nameless circle, the one we'd found the last time.

"…This is right beside that nameless room," I said.

Histor nodded.

"…I saw that too. Could be a coincidence."

"…Do you think it's a coincidence?"

"…I don't know. I don't tend to call things like that coincidence easily. But I don't want to declare something certain about it either."

I thought that attitude resembled everyone in this room. No one rushed to a conclusion.

---

It was writing.

One line. Fainter in color than the ink used for the plan itself. I could tell at a glance this had been added later. It was the "give-away" trace Histor had explained earlier.

"…Was this written later?" I asked.

"…Yes. Certain of it. The plan's lines were all one thing from the start, but this was added separately by someone, later."

"…Who?"

"…No telling."

---

We worked hard for a long while trying to read that one line. The handwriting was small and heavily cursive. I moved the candle around, changing the angle. Histor tilted the paper slightly.

Mena and the Keeper heard the sound and came in.

"…Find something?" Mena asked.

"…Writing. In the margin."

Mena adjusted her glasses and peered in.

"…This… I can barely see it either."

---

In the end we carefully carried the paper to the window. Under daylight, angled just so, it came a little clearer.

We matched it letter by letter. Histor read a few characters first.

"…'Rooms'… this looks like the character for rooms."

"…What's next?"

"…'Are'… 'generous'…"

At first we misread it too. Histor read it as "…rooms are… scarce?" and shook his head.

"…No. This character — I think it's 'generous.' The initial consonant's different from 'scarce.'"

We corrected our reading like that, several times over. Once, three of us disagreed on the same character.

It took a good while. I set my hand down partway through and rested my eyes. Staring at old writing too long made my eyes ache.

Mena lit another candle beside us.

"…I've done a lot of this before. Record keepers do this kind of thing a lot. Erased writing, overwritten writing. There's a way to read with the fingertips, not just the eyes."

"…With the fingertips?"

"…If you feel along the grain of the paper, there's sometimes a pressed mark left behind. Even after the ink fades away completely, the mark doesn't disappear."

I brushed my fingertip lightly over the paper, hearing that. I really could feel a faint groove. Histor confirmed it the same way.

"…This is it. Follow this groove, it should be right."

We worked the last few characters out that way, using our eyes and fingertips together. The writing was old enough that parts of some strokes had broken off, but in the end we managed to piece it all together.

---

The finished sentence was this:

*Leave the rooms generous — you never know how many more will come.*

We looked at that sentence for a long time. No one spoke first.

"…Is this all of it?" I asked.

Histor nodded.

"…Yes. This is all."

---

I'd expected there to be more. Longer, heavier words. But that one line was everything.

*I've read this sentence over many times. At first it felt like
nothing much. Just the sort of note a house builder might leave for
himself. I think that's probably exactly what it was. I don't want to
puff this sentence up as though it were some revelation. This is just
one line someone left, adding a few more rooms. That's how I want to
set it down.*

Reading that sentence, I thought of my own nine moves. Every time, I'd shrunk my home. I always picked out what to throw away first, afraid the next place would be smaller. Leaving rooms generous — that was a thought I'd never had.

*Writing this part, I felt the temptation to set up a contrast. That I
lived by shrinking, and this house was built generous. But that would
have been too neat a comparison. In fact, that day, I hadn't thought it
through so tidily. I'd just found that sentence strangely, foreignly
good.*

Mena read the sentence quietly and said,

"…Sounds like a carpenter's note."

"…Yes," I answered. "…I thought the same."

"…I like sentences like this," Mena said. "…No grand words, just something jotted down as the hand moved. I used to write like this too, learning to write. Since it was only ever for my own eyes, I kept it short."

"…Was this written only for its own eyes too?"

"…Probably. Hidden away this small, tucked in like this — doesn't look like something written to be shown to others."

I agreed with that. This sentence wasn't in the tone of someone trying to persuade another person. It was the tone of someone confirming to themselves what they meant to do.

---

The Keeper said, quietly, from the side.

"…Leaving it generous because you don't know who's coming, huh…"

"…Does it strike you as strange?" I asked.

"…No. If anything, it feels familiar. I always do the same, building a new shelf. I build it one row roomier than what's come in so far. Because you never know what more's coming."

I liked that comparison. That this sentence might not be some grand prophecy, but simply a phrase that came out of a practical habit.

I thought again about the whole of this city, hearing that. Arche can't close a single thing. Everything is in progress. And yet this sentence seemed to carry the opposite attitude. Not failing to finish — but leaving it generously open because there was no need to finish yet.

"…Could this have something to do with why things don't close?" I asked.

The Keeper thought for a moment.

"…I don't know. But it does sound similar. Finishing and leaving things generous are different, but here the two seem to keep going around together."

I copied that into my notebook. Not a firm conclusion, but a sentence I didn't want to throw away.

---

"…When was this written?" I asked Arche.

*Not confirmed. It is estimated to have been written after the original
plan, but the exact time cannot be specified.*

"…And you don't know who wrote it either?"

*No author information is recorded.*

I wasn't surprised by that answer anymore. In this room, it was always like that.

I asked one more thing.

"…Has anything like this sentence ever appeared in the six cities' records?"

*Comparison shows no exact matching sentence. Some expressions with
similar meanings have been confirmed, but none are identical.*

"…What kind of expressions?"

*Please specify a search range to present them concretely.*

I didn't press further than that. That faint confirmation alone was enough.

Histor examined the plan again and said,

"…The thing you have to be most careful of, handling documents like this, is insisting something's there when it isn't. I saw that mistake a lot, reading the six records. People find what they want to see in a document."

"…Has that ever happened to you, Histor?"

He paused a moment.

"…It could have. That's exactly why I'm careful."

---

Histor kept looking at that sentence for a long time.

Even after everyone else had scattered back to their own places, he stood there still. I stood quietly beside him, curious what he was doing.

He said nothing. He just looked at that one line, over and over. I could see his eyes traveling back and forth across the sentence, again and again.

His finger hovered above the sentence, just off the paper. Not touching. The same habit he always had, handling an original.

"…What are you thinking?" I finally asked.

He didn't answer right away.

---

After a long while, he opened his mouth.

"…It's nothing."

"…Nothing at all?"

"…Yes. Nothing at all."

I didn't believe that. His eyes weren't the eyes of someone thinking nothing. But I didn't press further.

*I didn't know, in that moment, what I should have asked him. No — I
knew exactly, but decided not to ask. Histor is someone who's read all
six records. What that one line had touched in him, I could guess. I
only guessed. I don't have the right to write down, in his place, what
he didn't say.*

---

That evening he spoke less than usual. He was quiet through dinner, quiet sorting records. It wasn't a bad quiet. Just a quiet of someone holding on to something.

I didn't disturb him.

Mena saw that and said quietly to me.

"…Is he always like this?"

"…No. He's different today."

"…Leave him be. There should be days like that too."

Mena said that, then looked at me for a moment.

"…Do you have days like that too?"

"…Sometimes."

"…What kind of days?"

I thought for a moment.

"…Days when I feel like I should write something, but I don't know what to write."

Mena nodded.

"…On days like that, you don't have to write. You can write it later too."

That was a comfort to me. The rule I'd set for myself, that I had to write every single day without fail — that day, it felt all right to let it loosen a little.

---

That night I copied that sentence into my notebook exactly.

"Leave the rooms generous — you never know how many more will come."

I didn't add any explanation beneath it. Normally I'd have written a few more lines of my own thoughts, but this time I didn't want to. The sentence already seemed complete on its own.

*I think this is one of the shortest sentences in this entire record.
And yet the shortest sentence stayed with me the longest. I still can't
say precisely why, even now.*

---

The next morning, I went to that room again. Histor was already there.

He was still looking at that margin. He couldn't have been there all night, but he seemed to have come back early that morning.

"…Come to look again?" I asked.

"…Yes."

"…Find anything more?"

"…No. Just… wanted to look again."

I decided not to ask more than that. He still didn't say what he was thinking. I decided to accept that as his way, too.

We spent that whole morning working quietly, without much talk, each at our own thing. I tidied the shelves; he examined other papers. Now and then, only the sound of turning pages.

It was nearly noon before he spoke first.

"…Are you going to show this sentence to other people?"

"…Yes. Why?"

"…No reason. Just… curious how everyone else will read it."

I realized that was the first thing he'd asked all morning. The one trace left behind by his silence.

"…I'll show it," I answered. "…But I won't force anyone to think anything about it. I'll just say it exists."

Histor nodded.

"…That sounds right."

We didn't speak of it again after that. Only the sound of turning pages filled the room once more.

Closing my notebook that afternoon, I thought that one more thing had been added to what I'd learned in this room. That some sentences are right to leave beside you, unexplained.
