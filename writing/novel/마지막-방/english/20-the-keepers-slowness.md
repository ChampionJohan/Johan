# Chapter 20. The Keeper's Slowness

> _Previously_ — It was confirmed that four of the five cities had
> never read one another's records. Histor admitted the fact but did
> not change his position. He said other possibilities still remained.
> Mone learned, for the first time, that facts don't move a person.

After that day, I began to see the Keeper differently.

It was a strange trigger. Watching Histor not change his position, I
suddenly thought of the Keeper. That slow walk, that slow way of
speaking. Up to then I'd just taken it for personality.

*In this chapter I write down the day I learned that slowness wasn't
personality.*

The trigger was small. When Histor admitted that four had never read
each other and still didn't change his position, I couldn't, in that
moment, think who it reminded me of. But that night, thinking it over
at home, I thought of the Keeper. He was similar. Twenty-two years
with not one single case closed, and knowing that, still coming every
day. I kept feeling those two were alike somehow.

---

That afternoon, while Histor was out of the room, I asked the Keeper.

"…Why do you do everything so slowly?"

The Keeper stopped what he was doing and looked at me.

"…What am I doing slowly?"

"…Everything. Your walk, your speech. You were like that from the
first day. You asked me, 'What changes if you do it fast?'"

The Keeper laughed a little.

"…You remembered that."

"…Yes. I thought it was just a joke at the time."

"…It wasn't a joke."

I looked at the paper still in the Keeper's hand. Holding the page
he'd been turning, frozen mid-motion. I kept wondering why he was this
slow.

"…Seeing Histor made me think of you."

"…Why?"

"…Histor doesn't change either. No matter what you show him. But he
keeps coming. So do you."

The Keeper heard that and studied me for a moment. A surprised look.

"…I didn't expect you to set those two side by side."

"…Aren't they alike?"

"…They're alike. But there's a difference too. I'll tell you that
later."

I was curious about that "later," but I didn't ask more.

---

The Keeper set down what he was working on and pulled up a chair. I
sat down in front of him too.

"…Do you know how many years I've done this?"

"…I don't know."

"…Twenty-two years."

I was startled by that number. Twenty-two years was before I was even
born.

"…Do you know how many cases have closed in twenty-two years?"

"…Isn't that something you could ask Arche?"

"…I did. You know what the answer was?"

I had a rough guess. But I wanted to hear the exact answer.

"…What did it say?"

"…Zero. Not one."

---

I couldn't say anything, hearing that number. Not one in twenty-two
years. I thought of the "in progress" markers I'd seen the first time
I came to the repository. Some had been in progress ten years, they'd
told me. I'd assumed at the time that meant a handful of cases.

"…All of them? None at all?"

"…Not one. Arche gives an answer to everything you ask. But it never
gives an end."

"…Isn't that hard?"

The Keeper looked thoughtful for a moment.

"…It was hard. The first few years. I waited every day thinking
something would finish. It never did."

---

"…Is that why you slowed down?" I asked.

"…More or less. At first I thought doing things fast would finish
something sooner. The more I hurried, the more anxious I got. Then one
day it hit me. Fast or slow, nothing finishes either way. So it's
better to go slow."

"…How is that better?"

"…Going slow, at least, means seeing what you're doing right now
properly. Going fast, you're already moving on to the next thing, so
you never properly see what's in front of you."

I turned that over. *Writing this line down again now, I think this is
one of the quietest sentences in the whole book. He hadn't given up on
finishing. He'd just changed the way he waited for it.*

"…So how did you spend those first few years?" I asked.

"…Ran myself ragged. Came in early every morning, worked late every
night. Thought that if I could process even one thing fast, something
would get better."

"…Did Arche fail to close anything back then too?"

"…Same as always. Only I wore myself out. I remember one night,
sitting here alone, crying. I didn't even know why I was crying. Just
that it was hard."

I heard that story and found it hard to picture — that the person sitting
in front of me now, so unhurried, had once run himself that ragged.

"…What changed that night?"

"…Nothing changed. I just walked one step slower than usual the next
morning. That's all. It wasn't a great resolution."

*I thought of my first day, hearing that. I'd started putting things in
order without any great resolution either. Both of us, without any
grand reason, had each found, a little at a time, a way of enduring.*

"…Did that one slow step keep going?" I asked.

"…No. Fast again the next day. And the day after that. More days
going back than forward. But some days I walked slow again. Kept it up
for a few months, and the slow days came to outnumber the fast ones."

"…So it didn't change all at once?"

"…Nothing changes all at once. That's how I think of it. If there's
such a thing, it's something I don't know about."

Hearing that answer made me think of my own days — I didn't put
things in order every day either. Some days I was impatient, some days
I put things in the wrong slot. I'd thought that was shameful. That
day, for the first time, I thought maybe it was just how it was.

---

"…Do you think it will end someday, then?" I asked.

The Keeper thought about that question for a long time.

"…I don't know. Really don't know. But I don't think it's mine to
know."

"…Whose is it, then?"

"…Whoever finishes it would know, I'd think. I'm not the one who
finishes it."

"…Is there someone like that?"

"…I don't know if there is or not. But if there is, it isn't me.
That much I'm sure of."

I found that certainty strange. About everything else he said he
didn't know, and yet he stated one thing with certainty — that he
wasn't the one who finishes it.

"…How can you be so sure?"

"…Twenty-two years, that's how. If I were the one to finish it,
something would have finished by now. Nothing has. So I'm not the
one."

I thought that logic resembled Histor's way. Confirming your own
position by evidence. Only the direction was different. Histor stacked
evidence into reasons not to believe; the Keeper stacked evidence to
confirm his own place.

"…Does that make it easier?" I asked. "…Knowing you're not the one who
finishes it."

"…Not easier so much as lighter. There's no weight of having to finish
it. I just put today's things in their place today."

"…That sounds easy the way you say it, but it doesn't look easy to
me."

"…It isn't easy. But not-easy and heavy are different things."

I turned that short distinction over many times that day. Not-easy and
heavy. I didn't fully understand the difference then. I understand a
little now. There are things that aren't easy but are still bearable,
and things that look easy but still crush you.

That distinction felt unfamiliar. He'd worked here for twenty-two
years. And yet he said he wasn't the one who finished it.

"…Then what is your job, exactly?"

"…To set things down. In order. Same as you."

---

Something in me warmed a little at that. I'd always thought putting
things in order was only my job. Hearing the Keeper call it his job
too made this work suddenly feel bigger.

"…When did you realize finishing wasn't our job?"

The Keeper looked out the window for a moment.

"…About five years in. Someone came through here then, another one
full of questions like you. That person asked me, 'Why do you keep
working if nothing ever finishes?' I couldn't answer it at the time. It
took me days of thinking to find the answer."

"…What did you say?"

"…I said, 'I've come to think finishing was never our job to begin
with.'"

"…What did that person say back?" I asked.

"…Nothing at all. Just nodded. But after that, their face looked more
at ease. That's when I first knew it too — that I only knew I believed
it once I'd said it out loud."

"…Did you not know before? What you thought?"

"…I didn't. Before someone asked me, I was just coming and going every
day, that's all. Some things only become known once they're put into
words."

I copied that sentence down exactly on my paper. Something only
becomes known once it's put into words. I liked that sentence enough
to underline it.

---

That line stayed with me a long time. There was no resentment in it,
no despair. Just a voice accepting a fact.

"…Aren't you angry at Arche?" I asked. "…It's the one that can't
close anything."

"…It's not Arche's fault. Arche can't finish because the closing
condition was never defined. Whoever it is that didn't define it,
it isn't Arche."

"…Then who is it?"

The Keeper shrugged.

"…I don't know either. That's probably not mine to know either."

*I held onto this exchange for a long time. The Keeper didn't resent
Arche. Didn't pity his own situation either. He just came every day.
Doing a job that never finished, unfinished, with real care, every
day.*

"…Tell me more about that old story," I said. "…The one who asked why
you keep working when nothing finishes."

The Keeper laughed a little.

"…That one didn't stay long. About half a year, then went somewhere
else."

"…Where?"

"…Don't know. A letter came once, later. Just said they were doing
well."

"…Did that person not believe either?"

"…No. That one believed. Just never found a reason to keep doing this
work. Believing and keeping at this work are two different problems."

I was struck again by that distinction. Everyone in this repository
said something similar. Knowing and believing were different, and
believing and keeping on were different too. I tried to sort those
three layers out in my head, but it didn't come together well.

"…Then why do you keep on, Keeper? Even while believing?"

"…That's the first time anyone's asked me that."

The Keeper looked out the window for a moment, then looked back at me.

"…I like it here. Even without an ending. I like watching something
get set in its place, in order, every day. That's enough for me."

*This is my favorite answer in this chapter. He didn't work for the
sake of waiting for an ending. He just came because he liked today's
work. That simplicity stayed with me a long time.*

I thought once more about the distance between that impatience and
this slowness. When I first came to this repository, I'd thought
placing paper quickly and accurately was the most important thing.
Fast seemed like doing it well. But the Keeper had let go of fast over
twenty-two years. I hadn't reached that place yet. I still tended to
hurry — afraid the order would be wrong, afraid I'd miss something.

"…Will I turn into someone like you someday?" I asked.

"…I don't know. That's for you to decide."

"…Decide what?"

"…Whether to go fast or slow. Both are ways of keeping at this work. I
can't say which is right."

I liked that answer. He hadn't told me to become like him. He'd just
told me I could choose.

---

That evening, Histor came back and asked what we'd talked about. I
told him roughly. The Keeper's twenty-two years, zero closed cases, and
that line.

"…The Keeper said, '…I've come to think finishing was never our job
to begin with,'" I told him.

Histor was quiet a long time, hearing it.

"…That's a good line," he finally said.

"…A good line?"

"…Yes. An honest one. Not pretending to be able to do something you
can't."

I was surprised by that response. I'd expected he'd raise some
objection. Instead he just accepted it, exactly as it was.

*I set this moment down at the end of this chapter. Histor might have
seen something in himself, in what the Keeper said. Speaking
precisely, not pretending to do what you can't do. The two resembled
each other in that.*

"…But there's a difference too, between the two of you," I said. What
the Keeper had said earlier in the day came back to me.

"…What's different?"

"…The Keeper said he'd tell me later. I don't know yet either."

Histor thought about this for a moment.

"…I'm curious too."

I found it a little unexpected that he'd say that. He usually didn't
show much curiosity about what he didn't know. But that day was a
little different. *Looking back on this scene now, I believe he really
was curious — about what the difference was between himself and the
Keeper. That curiosity never took him anywhere, and that's one of the
sorrows of this book, but it isn't true that he wasn't curious.*

On the way home I passed the water channel and ran through what I'd
heard that day. Twenty-two years, zero, and that line. I thought about
how far apart that number and that line stood. Twenty-two years with
nothing finished — for an ordinary person, that would be a number to
despair over. And yet what was left at the end of that number wasn't
despair.

*Writing this chapter, I sort through the Keeper as a person once
more. He was different again from the six officials in the earlier
records. The overseer trusted the ledger; the Adjudicator knew no
alternative; the manager wasn't ashamed; the caretaker grieved; the
secretary believed she was nothing at all. The Keeper wasn't like any
of them. He knew his own work never finished, and came every day all
the same. Without resentment, without despair. I still can't put a
name to that manner. I'll leave it here, unnamed.*

On the way home I wrote in my paper. Twenty-two years and nothing
finished. And still, every day, he comes. No resentment. "I've come to
think finishing was never our job to begin with." I read that line
over several times.

*At the very bottom of the paper I added one short question. "Then
when does the one who finishes it come?" That day I thought I'd
written it out of simple curiosity. Setting this record in order now, I
see that I'd already begun waiting for something. Without even knowing
what.*
