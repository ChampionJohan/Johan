# Chapter 22. Histor's Way

> _Previously_ — Mone asked Arche about the man with no name. It
> quoted all six records precisely and answered, ***That person cannot
> be identified.*** No lie, nothing hidden. There simply was no name.
> Mone felt Arche and Histor resembled each other.

The days that followed seemed no different from before, on the
surface.

We kept cross-checking, and Histor kept being precise. On the surface,
nothing at all happened. But the way I looked at him was slowly
changing. *This chapter is about that change.*

I should start with why I was changing. Up to then I'd only seen him
two ways. A person who knew everything, and a person who didn't
believe. Both were true. But around this time I began to feel there was
something more between the two. The way his hands touched paper, the
speed at which he chose his answers, the way he fell silent. I began to
notice these things.

---

One afternoon, a bundle of Haven's record came in soaked. Retrieved
from the water channel. I began the work of drying it, as I always
did.

Histor sat beside me and helped. But the way his hands handled the
paper was more careful than usual. Sheet by sheet, unfolding each one
very slowly to dry it.

"…First time you've seen it this wet?" I asked.

"…No, I've seen it a few times. But it's nerve-racking every time."

"…Why?"

"…When it gets wet, the ink runs. Once it runs, you can't undo it."

I listened, watching the way he handled each sheet of paper.

He laid the dripping bundle flat on the floor and peeled the sheets
apart one by one, carefully. Where the paper had stuck together, he
never pulled it apart by force — he lifted from the edges, very
slowly. I watched his hands and wondered how many times he'd done
this.

"…Have you done a lot of this?" I asked.

"…Yes. I saw wet paper often as a child. Our village was by the water
too. I learned how to dry wet paper back then."

"…Who taught you?"

"…No one. I learned it myself, after ruining a few."

I found myself picturing his childhood again from that answer. Picking
up paper, straining to save what had gotten wet. I wondered why he'd
gone to such lengths, but I didn't ask that day.

Instead I laid it beside my own childhood. I learned how to pack a bag
only after I'd broken plates. He probably learned how to dry paper only
after he'd ruined some. Both of us learned by losing first. That common
thread bothered me. We were both people who'd started out from
losing something. Only I'd fled into order, and he'd fled into
checking.

"…Do you remember the first paper you ruined?" I asked.

He stopped his hands and thought for a moment.

"…Yes. A letter. Someone had pulled it out of the water channel, and
I opened it too fast and tore it in half. I only got to read half of
what it said."

"…And the other half?"

"…I don't know. Never will know. That's what I learned that day.
If you aren't careful, something stays unknown forever."

I heard that and thought again of my own moving. Of the eleven things
I'd lost, four of them. I too had stood in that same "will never know"
place he described. We'd each lost something different, but what was
left behind afterward had a similar shape.

---

He truly did cherish the paper. He let only his fingertips touch the
edges. He never forced a fold flat trying to smooth it out. If
something looked like it might tear, he simply left it be.

"…Aren't there going to be parts that never get unfolded?" I asked.

"…That's fine. Better to leave it folded than force it and tear it.
There'll be a way to deal with it later."

I felt something strange, hearing that. He really did cherish these
records. That much was certain. Every motion of his hands proved it.

---

Once the work was done, I asked what I'd been wondering.

"…Histor, you handle these records so preciously."

"…Of course. If these are gone, nothing's left at all."

"…So why cherish them this much, if you don't believe?"

He stopped his hands for a moment and looked at me. A thoughtful face.

"…Not believing and cherishing are different things. I respect the
people written down in this paper. Elpis really did wait for her
brother beside the flower bed. Pheugon really did run away. All of it
really happened. I don't want to handle that carelessly."

I felt that answer was genuine. And yet something else felt strange
alongside it too.

I watched the way he worked a while longer that day. Once he finished
going through one bundle, he always washed his hands before touching
the next one. At first I'd just thought that was tidiness.

"…You wash your hands a lot," I said.

"…Whatever's on one bundle shouldn't pass over to the next. Dust,
moisture, anything."

"…You're that careful about it?"

"…Yes. They mustn't mix. Each has to stay its own record."

I liked hearing that. Each had to stay its own record. Even as he laid
all six cities' records side by side and cross-checked them, he washed
his hands every single time to keep from mixing them. I thought that
was, of all his principles, the most deeply ingrained one.

I looked down at my own hands that day. I didn't wash them nearly that
often. I'd thought as long as the order was right, that was enough. He
cared about the mixing, not just the order. I had to admit I was less
meticulous than him. And strangely, that didn't feel bad. We were each
guarding something different.

---

"…And that person? The man with no name."

"…He must really have existed too. At least someone was seen at least
six times. I respect that he existed."

"…You respect that he existed — so why won't you believe he might
still exist now?"

He fell silent for a long time at that question.

"…Mone, I respect this paper. That's all I can do. I don't yet know
how to respect what's beyond this paper."

That was the first time I clearly felt something from him. *He'd set a
firm wall between cherishing a record and believing what a record
points to. That wall was very solid. Paper could be touched, could be
checked. What lay beyond it could not.*

I wanted to know where that wall had begun, so I asked carefully.

"…Histor, did you never learn how to respect what's beyond the paper?"

"…I tried to learn."

"…How?"

"…When I was little, I heard grown-ups in my village speak of things
in records as though they were real, true things. I found that
fascinating and tried to do the same."

"…And?"

"…It didn't work. I kept wanting to check. Where the story came from,
whether it really happened. Checking never took me toward believing —
it only ever took me toward knowing."

I heard this explanation and felt my chest grow heavy. It wasn't that
he'd chosen not to believe. His own way of doing things had carried
him there on its own.

"…Doesn't that feel unfair?" I asked.

"…It's not unfair. I've accepted this is simply my way. Only, I do
sometimes wonder how other people manage to go on without checking."

Listening to him, I felt I was watching him envy people outside
himself for the first time.

---

I wanted to press this difference a little further that day.

"…Then what is this paper, to you, Histor? If it disappeared."

"…That would be a disaster. There'd be no way to find it again."

"…But wouldn't the people inside the paper disappear too?"

"…No. Even if the paper disappears, the fact that those people existed
doesn't disappear. We'd just lose the way to know it."

I admired that distinction. It was truly a precise distinction. *At the
same time, I noticed for the first time that day how that precision
kept him from going anywhere at all. He drew a hard line between what
could be known and what could not. And about what could not be known,
he neither said it existed nor that it didn't. He just stopped there.*

Watching that stop, I thought of the Keeper again. The Keeper was
someone who stopped before what couldn't be known too. Not knowing
whether he was the one who finishes it or not, only certain of one
thing — that he wasn't the one who finishes it. But the Keeper's stop
and Histor's stop felt somehow different. The Keeper kept coming, even
while stopped. Every day. Histor also kept coming, even while stopped.
Every day. The surface looked the same. But something inside that stop
was different. I couldn't put that difference into words at the time.

---

Late that afternoon, the Keeper stopped by and watched Histor handling
the paper for a while. After watching silently for a long time, the
Keeper said quietly,

"…Good hands."

"…Thank you," Histor answered.

"…But good hands alone aren't enough for this work."

Histor looked up at that.

"…What do you mean?"

"…Hands can be precise. But this work needs something more than
hands. You'll find out eventually."

The Keeper left it there and went back to work. Histor looked to be
turning that over for a long while. I'd never seen him sink that deep
into thought before.

"…What do you think is needed more," he asked me later.

"…I don't know either."

"…What do you think, Mone?"

I was a little surprised by the question. He wasn't someone who asked
others much.

"…Well — I just think, maybe something that takes longer than hands."

"…Longer?"

"…Yes. Hands can get skilled fast. But the Keeper's been here
twenty-two years. That's not something hands alone can do."

He said nothing to that. But I saw him tuck the words away somewhere.
That was always his expression whenever he heard something — before
arguing back, he put it away first.

---

A few days later, I had another chance to see his way confirmed once
more. An old bundle had started crumbling at its corners. It turned to
dust at a touch.

"…What do we do about this?" I asked.

"…We have to move it as little as possible. Even the air has to be
careful."

He laid a thin sheet of paper beneath the crumbling record to support
it and moved it, one careful step at a time. I watched him and thought,
if only this person were this careful with people too, how good would
that be.

*And I soon realized. He really was. He was always careful with
people too. He never judged carelessly, never added a word he didn't
mean. Only that carefulness showed itself, before paper, as cherishing,
and before belief, as reserve. The same nature, producing different
results.*

I spent a long time that day debating how to write down this
discovery. I didn't want to portray him badly. He wasn't a bad person.
If anything, he was the most careful and the most honest person I'd
met in this repository. Only, that carefulness and honesty stopped him
at a certain point. I wanted to set that point down exactly in this
chapter. Not as blame — as a record.

Carrying the crumbling paper, he said one more thing to me.

"…Mone, you learn this doing this work. However careful you are, some
things can't be saved. When that happens, the right thing isn't to
force it back to life. You just protect what's left."

I felt that he wasn't only talking about paper. But I didn't have the
courage that day to check.

---

Finishing the work, I asked him something a little different for the
first time.

"…Histor, is it possible you don't believe because you're afraid of
losing something you cherish?"

He looked greatly startled by that question. An expression I hadn't
seen before.

"…What do you mean?"

"…Believing means you have to stake something, don't you? But if you
don't believe, you don't have to stake anything. And if you don't
stake anything, maybe you can't lose it either."

He was quiet for a long time. I regretted having gone too far.

"…I don't know," he finally said. "…I've never thought about it."

*I think this was the most honest thing he said all day. He was
someone who checked and organized everything. But before this
question, there was no organized answer. I still think, even now, this
was the first moment I saw inside his wall.*

He picked up, a moment later, a sheet of paper he still hadn't been
able to unfold, the crease still stubbornly set in it.

"…Still can't unfold this one today?" I asked.

"…No, not today. I'll look at it again tomorrow."

"…Isn't it just going to keep getting put off?"

"…It's not being put off. If I force it open now, it'll tear. I'd
rather leave it folded a long time than have it tear."

I listened to that answer, and this time I was certain it wasn't only
about paper. But this time too, I didn't say it out loud.

On the way home I passed the water channel and went back over the
day. The hands drying the wet paper, the habit of washing them, the
face that couldn't answer my question — "Believing means you have to
stake something, doesn't it?" I laid all of it together, one over the
other. He was a person who knew how to cherish. He just didn't know
how to stake. I think the difference between the two was the biggest
thing I learned this chapter.

*Writing this chapter again now, I have to admit that my feelings
toward him started to shift a little from that day. I still envied his
certainty. But now I'd seen the carefulness behind that certainty too.
He wasn't a coward. It wasn't that he feared losing something — it was
that he was a person for whom gaining anything without checking it
first was simply impossible. I distinguished those two things for the
first time that day.*

That evening I wrote in my paper. Histor truly cherishes these
records. But only as material. Today, for the first time, I felt
clearly what that difference was. And for the first time, I asked him a
question with no answer. He couldn't answer it. I don't think that's a
bad sign.
