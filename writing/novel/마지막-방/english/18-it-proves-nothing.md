# Chapter 18. It Proves Nothing

> _Previously_ — They discovered that a man with no name appears in
> every one of the six cities' records. The flower bed, the end of the
> line, the field, the valley, the water's edge, the front gate. Histor
> admitted it was true, but said it wasn't proof. He said other
> possibilities remained.

The next day, Histor came in with a different look on his face than
the day before.

Not an excited look. If anything he looked heavier. I could tell right
away he'd spent the night thinking about something.

"…What were you doing all night?" I asked.

"…Went back over what we found yesterday."

"…Find anything?"

He looked at me for a moment, then sat down.

"…Mone, I said yesterday there were other possibilities. Can I lay
that out in more detail now?"

I felt myself tense a little. It sounded like something wasn't going to
be good news.

---

"…The first is," he began, folding down a finger, "traveling stories
naturally resemble each other. Stories about treating a stranger
kindly, about a person who doesn't get angry — that kind of story turns
up everywhere. Because people tend to like similar types of stories."

"…Isn't that a stretch? You're saying six people just happened to
imagine such a similar person, purely by coincidence?"

"…It isn't imagination, it's records. But records are written through
memory too. People tend to fit the unfamiliar into familiar patterns
when remembering. A stranger who doesn't get angry, a person who
quietly stays close by — impressions like that can end up looking
similar even when different people go through different things in
different places."

I tried to find a way to argue back against that explanation, but I
couldn't. It was plausible. Too plausible.

"…For example," he added, "there's a merchant's record in the stacks
that has nothing to do with this city. It has a story like this too — a
stranger traveler being put up for the night, with no questions asked.
No name. It isn't a city connected to our six by the water channel at
all. It's a completely different place."

He brought that record out from the shelf. A short story, two
paragraphs. A traveler, a strange village, a name no one asked. I read
it and felt something go cold inside.

"…Is this the same person too?"

"…No, it's the opposite, actually. This is a record with no connection
at all to our six books. And yet a similar story appears in it. Which
means this kind of story itself could arise anywhere."

I couldn't help but nod along with that logic. What he'd brought as
evidence turned out not to argue against us, but to shake what had
looked like it was on our side.

---

"…Second," he went on, "the recorders might have read each other's
records."

"…What does that mean?"

"…Remember that Mena's record was misquoted in the Reckoning's record.
That means someone on Pheugon's side had been exposed to Mena's story,
at least by rumor. Pheugon might then have shaped the character in his
own record to resemble that story unconsciously, even without copying
it directly."

That part hurt the most, because it was evidence we'd found ourselves,
the day before. That Mena's record had drifted over into the
Reckoning's. Now it was being used against us.

"…So what we found yesterday actually counts as a counterargument?"

"…Not quite a counterargument. It just gives us one more explanation.
It could be the same person, but it could also be the same story being
passed along. Both possibilities remain open."

"…Is there a third one?" I asked. Only two of his fingers had folded
down.

He hesitated for a moment.

"…The third one is weaker. I'll say it anyway. The water channel.
Records flow along the water channel. So something someone experienced
in one city could travel as rumor to another city, and there be written
down again as though it happened to them. Not reading the record
directly — just hearing the story."

"…Isn't that similar to the second one?"

"…Similar, but different. The second is reading a record. The third is
just something passed along by word of mouth. The two are confirmed
differently."

I was struck by how finely he split apart these possibilities. *At the
time I thought this was persistence. Now I think it might not have
been persistence but fear. Saying something uncertain as though it were
certain was, for him, the thing he most wanted to avoid.*

---

Quietly straightening the six sheets, I suddenly noticed this method
felt familiar. It resembled the way I used to pack when I moved.
Fragile things in the middle, heavy things at the bottom. Histor was
stacking possibilities the same way. From the most certain to the
weakest, in order. I was a person who set objects in order; he was a
person who set possibilities in order. The methods resembled each
other, but we used them for opposite ends. I kept order so as not to
lose things. He kept order so as not to jump to conclusions.

I felt a kind of grievance rising in me.

"…Histor, are you trying to convince me right now, or convince
yourself?"

He looked a little startled by the question.

"…Neither. I'm saying what I know."

"…Then why do you keep raising objections? You were curious about this
together with me at first."

"…Being curious and drawing a conclusion are different problems. I am
curious. Even now. But I can't conclude anything yet."

I couldn't respond to that. *Writing this scene again now, I admit I
was angry that day. Not because he was wrong. I was angry because he
was right.*

---

"…Tell me honestly," I asked. "…Do you really think this is
coincidence?"

He closed his eyes for a moment, then opened them.

"…I don't know. I'm not certain it's coincidence, and I'm not certain
it isn't. What I have are two facts. That a similar figure appears in
all six records, and that there are at least two more explanations for
that. I don't know which of them is right."

"…That seems too well organized to say you don't know."

"…I can organize it. Organizing and knowing are different."

That distinction felt unfamiliar to me again. *He kept splitting words
that way over and over. Knowing and believing, organizing and knowing.
At the time it felt like wordplay to me. I know now. It wasn't
wordplay. It was how he lived.*

I wanted to ask Arche about it that day too.

"…Arche, have any of the six cities' recorders ever read each other's
records?"

*Some items are confirmed and some are unconfirmed. Would you like to
check?*

"…Please check."

*There is no evidence that Haven's recorder and the Reckoning's
recorder ever read each other's records. The reference to Haven found
in the Reckoning's record is presumed to have come through word of
mouth. The remaining combinations are unconfirmed.*

I heard that answer and looked at Histor.

"…Did you hear?"

"…Yes. But there are a lot of 'unconfirmed.' It just means the answer
hasn't come in yet."

"…Then we can just confirm them."

"…That takes time. We'd have to check every combination. With six
people, that's fifteen possible pairs."

I gave a small laugh, realizing he'd already done the math. *He was
always several steps ahead of me in moments like these. I never found
it hard to keep up with him. I actually found it fun. I just didn't yet
know that being several steps ahead never led anywhere.*

---

I looked at the six sheets again. Papers I'd thought, the day before,
were a great discovery. Today they looked a little different. Still
astonishing, but my confidence had shaken a little.

"…So does this mean nothing?" I asked.

"…No, I never said it meant nothing. I said it's not proven. Meaning
and proof are two different problems again."

"…Different again, is it?"

"…Yes. There's a lot in the world that isn't proven but is still
meaningful."

I found that a little comforting. But at the same time, frustrated.
*Why he'd say that and still not believe, I didn't understand at the
time. He kept saying it wasn't proven but was meaningful, and yet not
take one step toward that meaning.*

---

That afternoon we went back over the six rows again. Unlike the day
before, Histor started looking this time for the opposite evidence.
Checking each of the fifteen combinations for whether any of the six
recorders might really have read another's.

"…What happens if we check this?" I asked.

"…If it turns out all six never read one another at all, then the
second possibility I described disappears. What's left is only the
first — that similar stories resemble each other by coincidence."

"…What if they all read each other?"

"…Then my objection is right."

The Keeper stopped by the room that afternoon and looked at the chart
we were drawing.

"…What are you doing?"

"…Checking whether the recorders read each other," I answered.

The Keeper looked at the chart for a long while.

"…Interesting thing to be doing."

"…Histor's trying to use it as a counterargument, and I'm just
curious."

"…A counterargument, is it." The Keeper turned the word over. "…That's
fine too. Better than believing without checking."

I was a little surprised by that. I'd assumed the Keeper wasn't on
Histor's side.

"…Do you think Histor's right?" I asked.

"…Right or wrong aside, checking isn't a bad thing. I'd rather have
someone who checks and can't believe than someone who believes without
checking."

"…Why is that?"

"…Someone who checks and can't believe is at least looking at
something. Someone who believes without checking might not be looking
at anything at all."

I fell blank for a moment, hearing that. *I'd come back to this line
often later in this book. The Keeper never saw Histor as bad. If
anything, the Keeper understood him. I wouldn't learn where that
understanding came from until much later.*

I had to admit that this work was, for Histor, a fair way of working.
He wasn't only looking for evidence in his own favor. He looked equally
hard for evidence that could prove his own position wrong.

*I liked that fairness that day. I still think it was good. Only I
know now that fairness wasn't a conclusion. It was something he could
stand still on, and stay fair, indefinitely.*

Drawing that chart, the six scenes kept coming back to me all the same.
The hand by the flower bed, the face at the end of the line, the back
in the field, the footsteps in the valley, the silence by the water,
the slow walk near the gate. Even after hearing everything Histor
explained, I strangely couldn't believe those six scenes were six
separate coincidences. I knew that not being able to believe wasn't the
same as proof. But my heart still moved that way regardless.

I helped with the work, drawing fifteen combinations onto paper in a
chart. Haven-the Reckoning, Haven-the Harvest, Haven-the Preserve,
Haven-the Equity, Haven-Loop. The Reckoning-the Harvest, the
Reckoning-the Preserve, and on down the line. I liked drawing charts.
It meant filling in boxes.

"…Won't things get clear once this is all filled in?" I said.

"…Some things will get clearer, some won't. Even if 'never read'
gets confirmed, that doesn't mean 'never knew at all.' They could have
heard it by word of mouth."

"…So in the end, whatever we know, we never know completely."

"…Right. That's how it usually is. Nearly nothing is known
completely."

I thought that sounded strangely like him. Someone who says almost
nothing is fully known, and yet speaks about himself in the most
complete terms of all. I know, I don't believe. Neither sentence had a
"perhaps" in it.

*I only caught this contradiction that night. He was careful about
nearly everything in the world. Except for one thing. The one thing he
was always certain of was the fact of his own not believing.*

---

Toward evening we began marking, one by one, whether each city's
recorder had ever met or exchanged letters with another. It was slow
work. We didn't finish that day.

"…Let's finish tomorrow," Histor said.

"…Will we have an answer by tomorrow?"

"…Might. Might not."

I laughed at that answer. A sad laugh.

"…Histor, do you know that habit of yours? Whatever you're asked,
you're always half one way, half the other."

He heard that and laughed a little too.

"…Trying to be precise tends to come out that way."

On the way home I stopped a moment by the water channel. Listening to
the water, I went back over the day's conversation. Histor had offered
three possibilities. That stories naturally resemble each other, that
recorders could have read each other, that it could have traveled by
word of mouth. All three were plausible. I couldn't fully refute any of
them.

*That day I took this as losing. Now I don't. Losing and being
refuted are different. I was refuted, but I didn't lose. I just didn't
know the difference then.*

On the way home I wrote in my paper. Histor made his counterarguments.
The counterarguments were mostly right. I couldn't beat them.
Tomorrow, we check whether the recorders read each other.

*I added one more line beside that. "The reason he can't believe isn't
because he's foolish. It's the opposite, actually." I held onto that
sentence for a long time. It might be a strange sentence for a
sixteen-year-old to write. But I thought, that day, it was the most
accurate one.*

That night I thought again of the merchant's record. A stranger
traveler, a name no one asked. Those two paragraphs had nothing to do
with our six books. And yet they resembled it. I thought that was
evidence Histor was right, and at the same time something felt off. A
resembling story being common in the world, and the six we'd found all
falling on that common side — those were two different questions. I
tried to write that difference down on paper that night, but it didn't
come out well.

*I look back at myself that night now. I could follow Histor's logic.
I couldn't say he was wrong. But being able to follow it, and following
it all the way to the conclusion it pointed to, were different things.
I was standing somewhere between the two.*
