# Chapter 1. The Apprentice

What I do is put things in order.

When I say that's all there is to it, people usually laugh. Let them laugh. That really is all there is. Arche does the sorting, Arche makes the list, and Arche tells me which slot something goes in. I take the physical piece, and I put it on the shelf, in the order Arche gives me.

*I tried rewriting this first sentence several times. Meaning I wanted to make the work sound more impressive. But the more I revised it, the more it became a lie. So I left it as it was.*

*There are a few things in this record I left uncorrected, and this is the first one.*

*And one thing I'll say up front. There is nothing invented in this record. Where I didn't hear something, I wrote that I didn't hear it, and where I don't know something, I wrote that I don't know. That is the way I was taught to do this work.*

---

I should talk about Loop first.

This is a city that answers everything you ask it. That isn't just something I say — it's something anyone who lives here says. I say it too. I still say it now.

If there's something you don't know, you just ask. You can ask anywhere. You can ask in the street, or ask at home. Arche answers. If there's no answer, it says there isn't one. If it doesn't know, it says it doesn't know.

I have never once heard it lie.

That's what we learned at school too. That Arche never says it knows something it doesn't. Our teacher explained it and said, "…You'll understand later how rare that is." We let the words go by. To a ten-year-old, nothing is rare. Everything just is the way it is.

*I end up writing that sentence again several times in this record. Each time I write it, it grows a little heavier. Right now it will just read like boasting. That's correct. At sixteen, it was boasting, to me.*

People who come from outside don't believe this at first. They think there must be a price to it somewhere. There isn't. There really isn't. You just ask, and it answers. After about half a year, those people start asking anywhere too.

My grandmother never managed that, to the end. Every time she asked Arche something, she cleared her throat first. The little cough you give before speaking to a person. I thought it was funny, and asked her once.

"…Grandma, why do you clear your throat?"

"…It's manners."

"…You don't have to be polite with Arche."

My grandmother looked at me for a moment and said,

"…That's its business, not mine."

*At the time I thought my grandmother was old-fashioned. Now I think about that line often. She wasn't treating Arche like a person. She was refusing to change what kind of person she was.*

---

I came to work at the repository in the spring I turned sixteen.

The reason is simple. They were hiring, and I applied. There was a test, but it wasn't a hard one. They gave you twenty sheets of paper and told you to put them in numerical order. I finished fastest.

The kid sitting next to me during the test asked, once it was over,

"…Have you done this kind of thing before?"

"…Yes. A little."

"…Where?"

I didn't answer. Thinking about it now, it isn't a strange thing at all, but at the time, saying it felt a little embarrassing.

She didn't ask again. We both passed, and she quit after two months. She said it was because it was boring. I thought I understood what she meant. This work really is boring.

---

By the time I turned sixteen, my family had moved nine times.

That was my father's line of work. Not the kind that kept you in one place long. I packed nine times and unpacked nine times.

Packing is something you get better at with practice. By around the fifth move, I'd gotten quite good at it. Heavy things on the bottom, breakables in the middle, things you use often on top. Write on the side of the box what's inside. Keep the same kind of thing in the same box.

*I come back to this, much later in this record. By then it will be a different story from this one. Right now it's just the story of a girl who packed well.*

Nobody taught me any of this. On the third move all the dishes broke, and after that I put the dishes in the middle. That's how I learned — that way.

Across the nine moves, I lost something every single time.

On the second move, a spoon. On the fourth, the blanket I'd used as a small child. On the sixth, a wooden doll my mother had given me. On the seventh, I don't even know what I lost. Not knowing was the strangest part. Something was clearly gone, but I couldn't remember what it had been.

*Across the nine moves I counted it all up. Eleven things lost. Of the eleven, I still don't know what four of them were.*

I asked my mother once. What had gone missing from the sixth house. She thought about it for a long time and said,

"…I don't know. Was there something?"

If nobody remembers a thing being lost, was it lost at all, or had it simply never existed? I had that thought around the age of twelve.

*It wasn't a thought a twelve-year-old should have been having. But I had it, and that thought is what brought me here.*

So I put things in order. It isn't some grand reason. I simply never wanted that to happen again.

---

The repository stands at the eastern edge of the city.

It's a low building, with small windows, and it's cool inside. They said it was built that way because of the paper. The first time I walked in, I noticed the smell before anything else. Paper, dust, and something like chemicals, all mixed together. It wasn't an unpleasant smell. It was close to the smell of walking into an old house.

"…Is the smell here all right?" the Keeper asked.

"…Yes. I like it."

"…Then that's fine."

That was all he said, and he went inside. I followed him in.

The inside was bigger than I'd expected. From outside it looked low and small, but once you were in, it ran back a long way. There was a corridor, rooms on both sides of the corridor, and shelves in every room.

"…Is all of this paper?"

"…Yes."

"…How much of it is there?"

The Keeper answered without stopping.

"…Arche would know that."

---

There were a great many shelves.

I wanted to count them, but doing that on the first day seemed like it would look strange, so I didn't. On the third day, I counted. Twenty-two rows, eight slots to a row, roughly forty bundles to a slot.

*I still remember that number. Twenty-two times eight times forty. At the time I thought it was an enormous number. Later I would learn it wasn't nearly enough.*

For the first week I did nothing but look around. The Keeper was in no hurry. He was never in a hurry, about anything.

His walk was slow, his speech was slow, and even turning pages he turned them one at a time. At first I thought it was his age. He wasn't even that old.

On the third day I couldn't stand it any longer and asked. I was about to beg him to give me something, anything, to do.

"…Don't we need to do this faster?" I asked.

The Keeper looked once at the shelves, and once at me.

"…What changes if you do it fast?"

I took it for a joke, at the time.

*It wasn't a joke. He was really asking it. What changes if you do it fast. It took me two years to have an answer to that question, and once I had it, I grew slow too.*

---

The first job was simple.

Take in whatever bundle had come, and put it where Arche said it belonged.

I learned how to ask Arche that same day. There's nothing to it. You just ask.

The one thing I learned was that inside the repository, you ask a little differently. Outside, you can just say it, but inside you have to give the bundle number first. They said it's because there are too many records with the same name.

"…Where does this go?"

*Section Fourteen, Row Three, Slot Fifteen.*

"…Yes."

The voice doesn't sound like a person's voice, but it isn't a machine's voice either. It's more like a very quiet person speaking right next to you. I got used to it quickly. Everyone said the same.

On the first day I asked a few useless things too. I asked what time it was, whether it was raining outside, what I should have for dinner. The answer to the last one was strange.

*That item is not defined.*

"…What is?"

*The criterion for "good" is not defined.*

I thought it was funny and told the Keeper. He didn't laugh. He only nodded once.

*I would go on to remember that nod for a long time. What Arche said that day is the single most important sentence in this whole record, and I heard it while asking about dinner.*

---

Six bundles came in that day.

There's no fixed time for them to arrive. Sometimes they come in the morning, sometimes in the evening. Some days someone carries one in; some days one is fished out of the water channel. Anything fished out has to be dried first, which doubles the work.

All six had come from different places. I found that interesting, and looked at each cover in turn. Each had a city's name written on it. Haven. The Reckoning. The Harvest. The Preserve. The Equity.

I read the names out loud. Haven, the Reckoning, the Harvest, the Preserve, the Equity. They were all good names. A place to rest, a place that squares its accounts, a place that gathers in, a place that keeps watch, a place that's fair.

"…All the names are good."

The Keeper said, taking the bundles from me,

"…That's right. Every one of them named itself after what it meant to give."

That accounted for five. The sixth had no city's name on it.

"…Where did this one come from?"

The Keeper looked at that bundle a moment, then said,

"…From here."

"…From here?"

"…Yes. Our own city needs a record too."

"…Then why doesn't it have a name?"

"…Because you don't write your own name on yourself. The way you don't write your own address on the envelope you're sending from."

I liked the comparison and nodded.

At the time that sounded obvious to me. It doesn't sound obvious now. *Why it isn't obvious for a city to keep its own record — that, you'll understand if you read this record to the end.*

---

That day I shelved all six bundles and went home.

On the way, I saw the water channel running behind the repository. Something like a narrow ditch. Set with stone, with water running shallow through it.

I had never really looked at that water channel before then. I'd seen it, but I hadn't seen it. That's usually how it is for anyone born and raised in this city. What's always been there goes unseen.

I stopped and looked at it a while. Not because it was strange, but because of the sound.

The water wasn't clear. It was the color of soil, and there were small stones laid along the bottom. It wasn't deep or wide. You could have jumped it, if you'd wanted to.

But it kept flowing. The whole time I stood there, it never once stopped.

This city tends to be quiet. But that water made a sound. A small, unceasing sound.

Someone passed by beside me, and I asked,

"…Where does this come from?"

"…I don't know. Somewhere upstream, I'd guess."

"…Upstream where?"

That person thought a moment, then said,

"…Ask Arche about that."

I didn't ask, that day. I was tired, and just went home.

*Writing this passage now, I want to set down that I don't regret not asking, that day. A sixteen-year-old can't work out a city's whole structure on the first day. But I did hear that sound, then. That much is true.*

*Later I would go on to ask it. And I would go on to write down, in this record, what Arche answered. I won't say it now, ahead of time.*

---

Once home, I wrote down what had happened that day, on paper.

It wasn't anything grand. I went to the repository, there were many shelves, I shelved six bundles, I saw the water channel. That was about it.

Oh, one more thing. That when I asked what I should have for dinner, Arche said the criterion for "good" wasn't defined. I wrote that down as a funny thing.

*I'm writing this chapter now with that very sheet of paper in front of me. Sixteen-year-old handwriting. At the end of that line I'd written, small, "funny."*

I didn't know myself, then, why I wrote it down. Nobody had told me to.

*I know now. I wrote it down because I was afraid of losing it. That day, and every day after.*

*For two years I wrote something down every single day, without missing one. It isn't a grand feat. Often it was three lines a day. But I never missed.*

*Across nine moves I lost eleven things, and of those, I don't even know what four of them were. Not knowing was the most frightening part. More than losing something, it's frightening not to know that it's gone.*

*So I write things down. It wasn't some grand resolution.*

---

Let me set down one last thing.

At the very end of the repository corridor there's a door. I saw it on the first day and thought nothing of it. A door is just a door.

*I'll write about that door at the end of Part One. If I said more about it here it would read like some great door, and it isn't a great door. That's the strangest thing about this story.*

---

This record begins from that day.

There are only two things I did, inside this story. I put things in order, and I wrote them down. There is nothing else I did besides those two.

I fought no one, defeated no one, brought nothing crashing down. There is no such scene in this record. Whoever started reading expecting one may close it now.

*There is no scene in this record of anyone being brought down, either. I'm setting that down in advance too.*

*But I'll note that I tried rewriting this sentence too, several times. I wanted to make it sound more plausible. I couldn't. Because those two really are the only things I did.*

*Only — that nobody else was doing those two things, either — that, I wouldn't learn until much later.*

That is the first thing the one reading this record should know, so I'm putting it up front.
