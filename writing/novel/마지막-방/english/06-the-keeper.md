# Chapter 6. The Keeper

> _Previously_ — Mone was good at shelving records where Arche told her to. What she'd learned packing for nine moves carried straight over into this work. About half a year in, she began, for the first time, to wonder what became of these records.

Now I need to write properly about the Keeper.

*I've kept putting off this person's story. There's a reason. Writing him accurately took some time. For the first few months he just looked like a kind, slow adult. This chapter I'm writing now includes what I came to know afterward too. So the front half of this chapter and the back half may read a little differently. That's correct. That's how I came to know him too.*

---

The Keeper wasn't so old. The first time I saw him I thought he was, but I learned later he was younger than my father. His steps were slow and his speech was slow, and that made him look older than he was.

He wasn't tall, wasn't short. He wore the same color, always. A dark gray vest — I never once saw it any other color. His hands were unusually large. Handling paper, he turned pages with those big hands very carefully.

*I don't quite know why I'm setting down these small details. But when I think of him, that's the first thing that comes to mind — those hands. The sight of him turning thin sheets of paper, one at a time, with those big hands.*

---

I've already told about the first day. Asking, "Is the smell here all right?" and then, hearing my answer, saying "Then that's fine" and walking inside. I learned later that this was simply his way. Ask short, confirm short, move on with anything.

He wasn't a man of many words. At first that made me a little uneasy. Whatever I asked, only a short answer came back. As time passed I came to see that everything necessary was there, inside that short answer. There was simply nothing extra in it.

---

The Keeper never gave long explanations, teaching me. Instead he did it himself, and had me follow. If I got it wrong, he just told me to try again.

"…Is this how it's done?" I asked, the first time I carried a bundle.

"…Try it."

I tried. The bundle sat a little crooked.

"…Is this right?"

The Keeper came over, looked, and adjusted the angle slightly.

"…Right, now."

That was all. No long explanation of what had gone wrong, or why it had to be that way. I learned it through my body. Thinking about it now, that was actually the faster way to learn.

---

I've already said several times that the Keeper was slow. Let me set it down more precisely here.

Slow didn't mean lazy. The Keeper never took a day off. As far as I know, he never once called in sick. He came at the same time every day, worked in the same order every day. It was only that each single thing was slow.

Turning a page, checking a bundle, taking a single step. He never once hurried.

I found that pace frustrating. I believed working fast was the good thing to do. The Keeper never stopped me from thinking that. He just did his own work at his own pace.

---

Once I couldn't help asking.

"…Why do you do everything so slowly?"

The Keeper set down the paper in his hand and looked at me. It was the first time he'd looked at me that long.

"…Do you think it'll be over sooner if you do it fast?"

I was struck speechless by that question. I hadn't been thinking exactly that, but I couldn't say I hadn't been, either.

"…No…it's just, you can get more done if you do it fast."

"…You can get more done. But that isn't the same as it being over."

I didn't know, then, what that meant.

*I've come back to this exchange very often since. I know now what that line meant. Or — I think I know.*

---

The Keeper never once got angry. Not once, at least, while I was there.

Once, another apprentice accidentally dropped a bundle into the water channel. An old record, one that shouldn't have gotten wet. She cried, apologizing.

The Keeper didn't scold her. Instead he went with her to the drying room and calmly showed her how the paper could be saved.

"…It's all right. Not everything can be undone, but this can."

I heard that from beside them, and felt a strange relief. This man didn't press people over their mistakes. Instead, he looked first at what could be done next.

---

The Keeper and I didn't talk much, outside of work. Even eating lunch together, we were quiet. It felt awkward at first, and came to feel comfortable later.

Now and then the Keeper spoke first. Usually a short question.

"…Is everything all right at home?"

"…Yes."

"…And your grandmother?"

"…She's doing well."

That was all. And yet I didn't mind those short questions. It felt like someone was paying attention to me.

*Anyone who has read the other cities' books will know the Keeper is different from the six officials before him. The Administrator, the Adjudicator, the administrator, the Curator, the Secretary — each of them faced their own work in their own way. Of all of them, the Keeper was the quietest. And yet he was also the one who stayed with me the longest.*

---

Once the Keeper told me a little about his past. It wasn't a common thing.

"…Were you an apprentice too?" I asked.

"…Yes. Right at your age, too."

"…Were you this slow back then?"

The Keeper gave a small smile. "…No. I was fast, back then. Like you."

"…Then why are you slow now?"

The Keeper paused for a moment, then went back to sorting his papers.

"…Let's talk about that some other time."

I didn't ask further. His expression was telling me not to ask.

---

Before closing this chapter, I should set down one thing I didn't know, at the time.

The Keeper's slowness wasn't laziness. It wasn't listlessness either. I didn't know what it was, back then. But I could vaguely sense it. This man knew something. Something I didn't know yet.

*Further into this record, that feeling turns out to have been right. For now I'll only set down this much. The Keeper was slow. And there was a reason for that slowness. I wouldn't learn the reason until much later.*

---

The Keeper's day followed a set order. First, in the morning, he checked the water channel; then he confirmed whatever bundles had arrived that day; then lunch; then in the afternoon he made his rounds of the shelves, checking the index. In the evening he wrote a few lines in his own notebook, about what had happened that day.

I watched that order for a few months and then asked.

"…Do you always do exactly the same thing?"

"…Yes."

"…Isn't it tiresome?"

"…Tiresome and necessary aren't the same thing."

I took that answer at face value. Thinking about it now, there was more in that line than I understood, then. I didn't know it. I just assumed adults' words were always short.

---

Once, checking the index, I saw the Keeper standing in one spot for a long while. I went over, thinking something must be wrong.

"…Something wrong?"

The Keeper shook his head. "…No. It's just old, that's all."

I looked at the paper he was holding. The date on it was very old. Before I was even born.

"…Is this still not processed?"

The Keeper set the paper back in its place and said,

"…No. Still."

I let that go by lightly, at the time. Just thought — an old record, it can happen.

*I would come to remember this moment again, later. That this scene was a tiny foreshadowing of something I'd discover two chapters from now — I had no idea of that, then.*

---

One morning, sweeping the water channel together, I asked him something else.

"…Why do you keep staying here, Keeper?"

He answered, pole still in the water,

"…It isn't that I've nowhere else to go. It's just — I think this is my place."

"…Your place?"

"…Yes. Someone has to keep being here. Records keep coming."

I found it striking, how plainly he said that. It wasn't spoken with any tone of duty or pride. He said it the way you'd state a fact.

---

There was exactly one time the Keeper got angry with me. Not angry, precisely — his voice turned stern. It was when I dropped an old bundle, rushing.

"…Careful." His voice was different from usual.

I was startled and picked it up at once. Thankfully it hadn't torn.

The Keeper came over and examined the paper I was holding, then his voice went back to normal.

"…It's fine. Thank goodness."

I wanted to ask him why he'd been so startled, that day, but didn't. Looking at his face, it seemed better not to ask.

*I only learned later which bundle that paper had been. I'll set that down separately. For now I'll only note that there was this one moment — the only time the Keeper ever raised his voice.*

---

Sometimes I wanted to sneak a look at the Keeper's notebook. The one he wrote a few lines in every evening. I asked him about it once.

"…What do you write in there?"

"…What happened that day."

"…Can I see it?"

The Keeper smiled faintly. "…Later."

I didn't ask when "later" was. I just took it to mean he'd show me someday.

*When that "later" came, I'll say much further into this record. For now, I'll set down only this much. There were things the Keeper hadn't shown me. Not hidden things — things he thought the time hadn't come for yet.*

---

The way the Keeper treated Arche was also worth watching closely. Most people only called on Arche when they needed something. The Keeper asked, at a fixed time, a fixed question, every single day.

"…Arche, any change to today's index?"

*No change.*

"…And the unresolved cases from yesterday's check?"

*Two hundred and thirty-two cases, no change.*

I heard that exchange every morning. At first I thought it was just a work check. But listening to it for a few months, something felt off. The number of unresolved cases never went down. If anything, it grew, little by little.

I thought about mentioning it to the Keeper, then decided against it. I was sure he already knew.

---

The Keeper never spoke a word of resentment toward Arche. I thought that was unusual. Other people, whenever something frustrated them, would sometimes grumble, "Couldn't Arche do a bit more?" I never once saw the Keeper say anything like that.

"…Have you never felt let down by Arche?" I asked him once.

"…No."

"…Not once?"

"…Arche does everything it's able to do. That's not something to feel let down about."

Curious whether that was really true, I asked again.

"…But there are things Arche can't do."

The Keeper looked at me for a moment and said,

"…There are. But that isn't Arche's fault."

I wrote that line down in my diary that day. *It isn't Arche's fault.* I put a question mark after it. I didn't fully understand what it meant, at the time.

---

The repository had old tools. A knife for cutting paper, a brush for dusting, string for tying bundles. All of them things the Keeper prized. I once suggested using new ones.

"…It'd be faster if we swapped these for new ones."

The Keeper studied the worn brush and said,

"…Faster isn't always better."

"…But this is all worn out."

"…Worn to fit my hand, this much. A new one, I'd have to get used to again."

I didn't argue with that. Instead I began to see those old tools a little differently. Not worn from long use, but bearing the mark of a long companionship.

---

One winter, there was a day the repository was especially cold. The heater had broken, and my hands were cold the whole day. I could hardly keep my mind on the work that day.

The Keeper worked exactly as he always did. Hands red with cold, he never changed his pace.

"…Aren't you cold?" I asked.

"…I am."

"…Then why keep going?"

"…Being cold and not being able to work are different things."

I thought that was a bit much, at the time. But that evening, until the heater was fixed, the Keeper truly never stopped his hands. I stayed beside him, clumsily following along the whole time.

*That day I felt, for the first time in my body, what this man's endurance was like. It wasn't some grand resolve or determination. It was just continuing to do what he did every day. That was all there was to it.*

---

Closing this chapter, I feel the temptation to sum up this man, the Keeper, in a single sentence. But I can't. This man doesn't fit inside one sentence.

He was kind. He was slow. He never got angry. He never resented Arche. He worked in the same order every day. He treasured his worn tools. He never stopped, even on a cold day.

*Even having written all these sentences, I still don't feel I've written the whole of him. That's probably how it will be even by the time I finish this record. I'll keep writing anyway. As much as I know, as much as I've seen, as much as I've heard.*

Through all the time I worked as an apprentice, the Keeper was always there. He was already there when I arrived in the morning, and still there when I left. I thought, at the time, that this was simply how things were.

I know now how rare a thing that was. That someone, every day, without any show of it, kept holding the same place. That was never an easy thing to do.

Even now, writing this record, if I close my eyes I see him from behind. Turning pages one at a time under the low light. A back that never hurried, and yet never stopped, either.
