# Chapter 4. What Comes In

> _Previously_ — Arche doesn't lie and doesn't hide. When it doesn't know, it says so. But the Keeper left a line hanging — "Arche isn't everything" — and broke off the conversation there. Mone still doesn't know what he meant.

Records keep coming in.

I've already told how six bundles came in on the first day. That wasn't because it was some special day. More came the next day, and the day after that. Some days nothing came at all; some days more than ten came. On average, it worked out to three or four a day.

*I started counting this number in my third month as an apprentice. It's a habit. Counting things gives me a sense that something's in order. It's the same habit from the nine moves, showing up here too.*

---

Records arrive by two routes. A person carries one in, or one drifts down the water channel.

When a person carries one in, it usually goes like this. A courier from another city knocks on the repository door, sets down a bundle, leaves a few short words, and goes. "…This is from the Reckoning." "…This is the Harvest's, please confirm it." And they usually don't stay long.

At first I wanted to talk with those people a little. Which city they'd come from, what it was like there. But they all looked busy. That's just what a courier's work is like, the Keeper told me.

"…Those people make the rounds of several places in a day. Here isn't their last stop. It's just one stop."

---

What comes by the water channel is a little different.

That channel running behind the repository — the ditch I saw on my first day. Bundles drift down it now and then. Sometimes wrapped in oilcloth; sometimes it's just a wet clump of paper.

Fishing things out of the water channel happens at set times. Once in the morning, once in the evening. The Keeper walks along it, sweeping it with something like a long pole. I went along a few times.

"…Who sends these?" I asked, the first time I went along.

"…I don't know either," the Keeper answered, dipping the pole into the water.

"…Doesn't Arche know?"

"…Ask Arche."

I asked, right there. "…Arche, who sends the records that come through this water channel?"

*Sender information cannot be confirmed. Inflows through the water channel arrive with no record of a sender.*

I found that a little strange. "…How does it get here if nobody sent it?"

*An answer to that question cannot be confirmed.*

---

Whatever's fished out has to be dried. That's a feature of records that come by the water channel. Wet paper spoils if you leave it be. There's a small drying room in the back of the repository, and that's where it goes. Sometimes it's hung on a line like laundry; sometimes it's laid flat to dry.

I liked going into that room, at first. It smelled of paper drying. Damp, but warm.

"…How long does it take to dry?" I asked.

"…Depends on the bundle. Two days, usually."

"…And in the meantime we just leave it?"

"…In the meantime there's nothing to do. Waiting for it to dry is work too."

At the time, calling waiting "work" sounded a little strange to me. Now I think it's exactly right.

---

Looking at the bundles with their cities' names on them, I came to know, little by little, what kind of place each city was.

Records from Haven usually took the form of a ledger. Densely written, who had gone through what and when. The Reckoning's had a lot of tables split left and right, like scales. The Harvest's were rolls — sheets with names and qualifications listed one after another. The Preserve's had an especially strong smell of long-aged paper. The Equity's were mostly things like minutes of meetings. Who had said what, how many hands had gone up.

*I didn't think deeply, at the time, about why these forms differed. I just let it pass as each city having its own way. Now I know that each of those forms, on its own, shows what that city held to be important. I'll come back to this, much later, when I lay those records out and read them directly.*

---

Sometimes I wondered why so many records came here at all. One day I asked the Keeper.

"…Why does everything come to us?"

The Keeper sorted bundles and thought for a moment.

"…I don't know. It's always been that way. I don't know the reason either."

"…Who decided they should be sent here?"

"…I don't know that either. It was already that way before I came."

I wasn't satisfied with that answer, and asked Arche too.

"…Arche, why do all six cities' records come here?"

*No confirmed rule or directive record exists. The inflow is an observable fact; its basis cannot be confirmed.*

I felt strangely relieved, hearing that. That Arche didn't know either — that meant I wasn't the only one who didn't.

---

Once I asked a courier directly. A young man who said he was from the Reckoning.

"…Who told you to bring these here?"

"…It's just custom. In my city, once records build up enough, we send them to Loop. Everybody does it that way."

"…Why?"

He shrugged. "…I don't know either. That's just how I was taught."

Hearing that, I felt a little like laughing. Nobody knew the reason, and yet everyone was doing it. It was a strange thing, but no one else seemed to feel that strangeness the way I did.

*Looking back now, that was the strangest part of all. Nobody had ever asked for it to be sent, and yet everyone took sending it for granted. A thing taken for granted that no one could explain. I would go on to meet a few more things like that, after this one.*

---

Receiving whatever came in was the start of my job. Take it in, check it, ask Arche where it goes, put it there. That repeated several times a day.

I didn't mind the repetition. I actually liked it. Something arriving, and putting it in its place. It was work I was good at, and I knew it was work that mattered.

*In this chapter I've only written how records come in. What becomes of them afterward, I'll write in the next chapter. I'll say this much ahead of time: there's a great difference between what comes in and what goes out. What comes in was always there. What goes out — up to that point, I had never once seen it.*

---

There were a few couriers whose faces I came to know well. One of them was a middle-aged man who said he was from the Harvest, and every time he came, he set his bundle down with great care.

"…Is this one not supposed to get hurt?" I asked him once.

"…It shouldn't get hurt. This is somebody's name."

That line stayed with me. It was just a stack of paper, and yet he treated it as though it were a person.

"…What about the name?"

"…If you're careless with something a name is written on, isn't that the same as being careless with the person who carries that name?"

I copied that line straight into my diary that day. *You must not be careless with something a name is written on.* From then on I thought of it every time I moved a bundle.

---

Records from the Preserve tended to be especially old. The paper was yellowed, and some of them were thin enough at the edges to crumble. Handling those made me especially careful.

"…How old is this?" I asked Arche the first time I took in one of those bundles.

*The writing is estimated to date from roughly fifty years ago.*

"…Fifty years old, and it's only getting here now?"

*An explanation for the timing of arrival cannot be confirmed. It's presumed to be an internal matter of the sending city, but there's no basis for that.*

That answer left me with a strange feeling. Fifty-year-old paper, suddenly riding the water channel all the way here one day. Where had that paper been, those fifty years? I couldn't ask anyone. I didn't think there'd be an answer even if I did.

---

The minutes that came from the Equity were especially thick. One bundle held about twice as much as any other city's.

"…Why do you write so much here?" I asked a courier.

"…We write everything down. Who said what, how many hands went up. All of it. So no one can say different, later."

I laughed a little at that. "…Do a lot of people say different?"

"…Can't say there aren't any."

He said that, gave a short laugh, and turned to leave. Shelving that thick bundle, I thought — writing everything down like this must be a comfort, to someone.

---

The Reckoning's records always had a lot of numbers in them. Tables split left and right like scales, what came in and what went out. Every time I looked at one of those tables, I got a feeling of something being neat. Right and wrong looked clear.

"…This one's easy to look at," I once said to Arche.

*The Reckoning's method of record-keeping is designed to make comparison easy. However, a balance in the table does not always mean a balance in the actual circumstance.*

That answer startled me a little. It was the first time Arche had ever said something in quite that way. I let it pass by, at the time. Now I think I understand a little what that one line meant.

---

Haven's records had names in them more than most. Who had come to see whom, who had stayed by whose side. I liked reading records like that. I think it's because they were stories about people.

I asked the Keeper once.

"…I want to read all of these cities' records."

The Keeper stopped his hands and looked at me.

"…You can, but it'll take a while. There are six cities."

"…That's fine. I have plenty of time."

The Keeper gave a small smile. "…Thought you might say that."

*I started reading the records a little at a time, from that day. In the gaps between work, in no particular order, whatever came to hand. I didn't know then how important a habit that would become.*

---

There were days records piled up. Once, twelve bundles came in a single day. Two from each of the six cities, almost all at once.

I spent that day frantically taking them in, checking them, shelving them. By evening my arms ached. Even on a day like that, the Keeper didn't hurry.

"…Why is there so much today?" I asked.

"…Some days are like that. There are times it piles up, and times nothing comes."

"…Is there a reason?"

"…There must be one. We just don't know it."

I was growing used to that answer. There was quite a lot at this repository that had no known reason. And yet no one felt uneasy about it. They just accepted it and did what needed doing.

*I didn't think that was strange, at the time. Looking back now, it seems a little strange. Repeating the same work every day without ever knowing the reason for it. And yet that strangeness never felt like a bad thing. If anything, it was reassuring. Even not knowing the reason, what needed doing was clear.*

---

Among the records drifting down the water channel, sometimes there wasn't even a city's name on one. Sometimes the cover had gotten wet and the writing washed away; sometimes nothing had been written on it at all, from the start.

When I got a bundle like that, I asked Arche.

"…Which city's is this?"

*Cannot be confirmed. No identifiable marking is present.*

"…Then how do I shelve it?"

*Hold it in temporary storage in the unidentified section; reclassify once the contents can be confirmed.*

I came to know a certain shelf set aside for bundles like that. Not a very large section, but it filled in, little by little, as time passed. Every time I walked past it, I found myself wondering, quietly. Where had everything in there come from.

*I'll come back to this unidentified section, much further into this record. For now I'll just note that such a section exists.*

---

Taking in records, I began, little by little, to feel the weight of this work. This wasn't just paper. It was something someone had gone through, something someone had written down, something someone had sent all the way here.

Every time I took in a bundle, I thought of the names inside it. People I didn't know, people I'd never met. And yet those people's days, one after another, were here, inside this paper.

*I already held this feeling, from that point on. But what that feeling was really directed toward, I wouldn't come to understand until further into this record. I was putting people in order. Not paper.*

---

Every evening I counted how many records had come in that day. And I wrote only the date and the number, in a small notebook. At first I didn't even know why. It was just a habit.

About a month in, I opened that notebook again. The number kept climbing. There was never a day it went down. I thought that was only natural. Records would keep being made, so it was only natural that they'd keep coming.

*I didn't stop, at the time, to think about what it meant for that number to grow in only one direction. That things came in, and never went out. That was one of the most important facts in this whole record, and it would be several more months before I noticed it.*

---

Closing this chapter, I'll set down one more thing. I liked knowing that something arrived every day. I didn't know the reason, but something continuing to arrive felt like it meant this city was still connected to the others.

*That thought wasn't wrong. But being connected, and what became of the things that connection carried, were two different matters. I learn that difference, little by little, starting from the next chapter.*

Every time I closed the repository door and stepped out, I heard the sound of the water channel. The same sound, day or night. It sounded like proof that something was always still arriving. I didn't mind that sound. If anything, a day when I couldn't hear it would have troubled me more.

I walked home every day listening to that sound. Not once did I skip that road.
