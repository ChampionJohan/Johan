# Chapter 5. Putting Things in Order

> _Previously_ — Records arrive daily from six cities, by hand and by water channel alike. Nobody knows exactly why. The number coming in only ever grew, and Mone did not yet know what that meant.

Now I should set down exactly what it is I do.

Arche does the sorting. Which city something came from, what date it's from, what kind it is — Arche grasps all of it the instant it receives a bundle. Exactly how it works that out, I don't know either. I did ask, once.

"…Arche, how do you sort this bundle?"

*By weighing together the notation on the cover, the style of the content, the material of the paper, and the composition of the ink.*

I didn't really understand that answer, even hearing it. But Arche had never once been wrong. I decided that was enough for me.

---

What I do comes after. When Arche tells me "Section Fourteen, Row Three, Slot Fifteen," I walk to that spot and set the bundle down.

It sounds simple, and it isn't. The repository is large, the shelves are many, and there are several slots that look alike. At first I went back and forth more than once, having found the wrong spot.

*In my first week as an apprentice I mistook the spot three times. All three times, even after Arche had told me. It was a person's mistake, not Arche being wrong.*

As time passed I memorized the sections. Now I could find my way with my eyes closed. This is the one thing in this work I can genuinely take pride in.

---

"Why doesn't Arche just put it there itself?" I asked, early in my apprenticeship. I was truly curious.

The Keeper answered.

"…Arche doesn't have hands."

I laughed at that. It was true. Arche is a voice whose exact location no one even knows. It has no hands to move a physical thing with.

"…Then couldn't some other machine move it?"

"…Some places have that, in other cities. Not here."

"…Why not?"

The Keeper thought a moment, then said,

"…I don't know. It's just set up so a person does it."

I found that answer a little unsatisfying, at the time. Now I know that unsatisfying answer was exactly the important part. What Arche couldn't do had been left to a person, and a person did it. That was all there was to it. There wasn't some special reason — it had simply been left that way.

---

I should explain a little more what "putting things in order" means.

Records from the same city come in more than once, at different times. All of them get arranged and placed in order of arrival, or in the order of the date Arche gives. Stack it all up in a jumble, and later you can't find it.

I was good at this work. Not a boast — a fact. What I'd learned about packing, moving nine times, was put to direct use here. Heavy things on the bottom, things you'll reach for often on top. The same kind in the same slot. Never disturb the order of dates.

*I wrote earlier that I'd never learned this skill. To be precise, I didn't learn it at school — I learned it from life. And this skill learned from life turned out to be exactly the skill this repository formally needed. I found that remarkable.*

---

The Keeper marveled at my work a few times.

"…You're fast."

"…I'm just used to it."

"…No, you really are fast. I'm slow, myself."

I thought that was modesty, but I found out later it was true. The Keeper really was slow. At first I thought it was his age, or his temperament.

*I won't go deep into that in this chapter. I'll write properly about the Keeper's slowness in the next chapter. Here I'll only set down the fact — that I was fast, and the Keeper was slow.*

---

One day I mistakenly swapped two bundles' spots. I mixed up one from Haven with one from the Reckoning. I only found out when I asked Arche to confirm something the next day.

*The item in Section Fourteen, Row Three, Slot Fifteen does not match its registration information.*

I checked at once, startled. It really had been switched. I hurried to fix it, a cold sweat breaking on my back.

"…Arche, why didn't you say something sooner?"

*As no confirmation request was made, it was not separately reported. I will confirm it for you whenever you ask.*

That answer frightened me a little. It meant that unless I asked first, Arche wouldn't step in on its own to tell me. Not catching a mistake, but answering exactly when asked. That was Arche's way. It wasn't a bad way. It was only a little different from what I'd assumed.

*From that day I got into the habit of checking far more often. It wasn't a bad habit to have.*

---

I've thought about why I like this work.

It isn't because it's fun. Honestly, it's dull more often than not. The same motions repeated, the same road walked, the same slots opened and closed.

And yet I loved this work. I loved the certainty that something was in its place. That there wasn't a single thing whose location I didn't know. That if I looked, or asked Arche, it would surely come.

*I know where that certainty came from. From the eleven things I lost, moving nine times. Of those, I don't even know what four of them were. Here, I was doing the opposite of that. Not losing anything. That's the real reason I loved this work.*

I told no one this, at the time. Not out of embarrassment — I just didn't think it needed saying. It was a reason it was enough for me alone to know.

---

The Keeper and I worked differently. I moved as fast as I could; the Keeper moved one slow step at a time. And yet, oddly, by the end of the day the same amount had gotten done.

"…How does that work?" I asked.

"…Because I do something else. You place things, and I check."

"…Check what?"

The Keeper turned a page and said,

"…Whether the index is right. Arche can't be wrong, but you still have to check."

I found that a little strange. If Arche couldn't be wrong, I didn't understand why he checked at all. The Keeper didn't explain further.

*What that checking was really for, I wouldn't understand until much further into this record.*

---

I always ended the day's work in the same order. Confirm that every bundle that came in that day had been shelved, confirm there was nothing whose spot I couldn't find, write the number in the notebook, lock the door.

I never once changed that order. There was no reason to. Keeping the order was the first principle I learned in this work.

*Keeping an order and bringing something to a close are different things. I thought, for a long time, that they were the same. It took a while for me to learn that I was wrong.*

---

Once another apprentice helped me for a few days. A kid borrowed from a neighboring department because they were short-handed. That kid tried to do the sorting herself, too.

"…Arche does the sorting," I told her.

"…I could do it too."

"…Being able to and doing it are different things. That isn't our job."

She looked crestfallen. I felt a little bad, at the time. Thinking about it now, I'd said exactly the right thing. What Arche did and what a person did were kept apart. Whenever a person tried to cross that line, something always got tangled.

In the end, she only did the placing, following me. She went back to her own department three days later. Leaving, she said to me,

"…This job really is boring."

I said nothing back to that. It wasn't a wrong thing to say.

---

Sometimes I'd take out the bundles I'd shelved and look at them again. Not for work — just out of curiosity. I once opened a bundle from Haven and read it, and it told the story of someone who'd lost a child and found her again.

I sat there a long while after reading that story. It wasn't a stack of paper — it was a person's story. I felt again, right then, that what I handled and moved every day was people's days, one after another.

*I go through this moment many times. Stopping short in the middle of work to read, and my heart moving over someone whose name I don't even know. That kid's line about this work being boring wasn't wrong — but moments like this were also unmistakably there, alongside it.*

---

Doing this work, I felt, for the first time, the weight of the word "organize." Organizing wasn't just stacking things neatly. It was making something findable, making it so nothing gets lost.

The Keeper said something to me, once.

"…A record that isn't organized might as well not exist."

"…It exists, though. Why call it nothing?"

"…If you can't find it, it's nothing. If it can't be pulled out when someone needs it, you can't say you have it."

That line stayed with me a long time. From then on, I decided to call what I did not "moving paper" but "keeping things from being lost." I told no one, but I set that in my own mind. Giving it a new name made the same work feel a little different.

---

There were things I learned through the body, doing this work too. How to bend your knees instead of your back carrying a heavy bundle, how to unfold wet paper without tearing it, that anyone with a dust allergy should wear a mask. Nobody taught me any of this. I watched the Keeper do it and followed along.

*I want to set down even these small things. It isn't a grand story, but this is what I actually did, every day. I'm writing it here because I'm afraid that if this record only tells the big things, what I did with my own hands, day after day, will be forgotten.*

---

One day my parents asked about my work. My father asked, over dinner,

"…Are you learning anything, there?"

I thought a moment and answered, "…Putting things in order."

"…That's all?"

"…Yes."

My father didn't look particularly satisfied. I started to explain further, then stopped myself. Explaining it wouldn't make it sound grander. It really was all there was to it.

My mother spoke up instead. "…It's fine. Doing one thing properly is no small thing."

I was grateful for that. Doing it properly. That was everything I was holding on to, in this work.

---

As time passed, I came to feel something like pride in my work. Not pride in doing something grand — pride in doing something small without getting it wrong.

Once I felt that the spot Arche had given me seemed off. There should already have been another bundle in that slot, and it was empty.

"…Arche, what's supposed to be here isn't here," I asked.

*I will confirm this. …That item may have been mistakenly moved to a different section during a previous apprenticeship period.*

I spent the whole afternoon that day searching for its true spot. I found it, in the end. An apprentice three years earlier had shelved it wrong. For three years, no one had known.

*I got a little chill, that day. For three years that bundle had sat quietly in the wrong spot. Unless someone went looking, it would have stayed there, exactly like that, forever. Organizing was the work of stopping mistakes like that.*

---

The day I fixed that mistake, the Keeper heard about it and said,

"…Well done."

It was the first time he'd praised me that way. I felt a little bashful.

"…It's nothing. I just found it."

"…It's not nothing. Nobody found it for three years."

I remember that praise for a long time. It was the first moment I had confirmed that there was something I was good at, and that the thing was useful. I told my parents about it too, going home that day. They were glad for me.

*There's a reason I'm setting this moment down. Further into this record, I go on to write about what the thing I'm good at cannot do. But before that, I wanted to make it clear, first, that I was genuinely good at this work. Setting down that I did it well, first, feels like the only way I can write the part about what I couldn't do, later, without being a coward about it.*

---

About half a year into this work, I had this thought for the first time. What becomes of all these bundles I've been shelving. Would they just stay here like this, forever?

At the time it was only a passing thought. I didn't even try to find an answer. I just took in the next bundle, asked where it went, and placed it. That day, and the next, and on.

*That passing thought carries forward into the next chapter, and the one after, growing larger. Looking back now, I think that small question that arose in my sixth month was the beginning of this whole record.*

---

Closing this chapter, I want to set down, briefly, what I learned from this work. How not to lose things. How to keep a place. How to find a mistake. And above all, how to repeat the same order, every day.

Of these four, I held on longest to the last. Repeating the order. I believed that was safe. I believed that as long as I kept the order, I would never lose anything.

*That belief was largely right. But not entirely right — I would come to learn that, working longer at this repository.*

Walking home, I would sometimes look down at my hands. Hands that had touched paper all day long. A little dust on them, the fingertips grown a little rough. I didn't mind those hands. If anything, I was proud that these hands were keeping something from being lost.

A day like this repeated itself, identical, every day. I didn't grow tired of the repetition. Before I could even feel weary of it, a new bundle would already be waiting the next day. And that bundle, too, had to be placed in order, just the same.
