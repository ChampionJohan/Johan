# Chapter 39. Histor's Question

> _Previously_ — Arche said the authority to set the closing condition
> rested not with the one who began, but with the one who finished. No
> one knew who the one who finished was. That day, for the first time,
> I thought finishing might not be the beginner's job at all.

For several days after that, we didn't talk about the seal.

Work had piled up. Records kept coming in from the six cities the
whole time. I put them in order, and Histor checked them alongside me.
The seal stayed exactly where it was, in the middle of the room. No
one touched it.

Those days were quiet. We each avoided bringing up the seal. It didn't
feel to me like putting it off, so much as a kind of time spent
preparing. I didn't know exactly what for.

Arche asked as it always did. *There are seventeen items to process
today.* I put those seventeen items in order. None of them closed. As
always.

Histor was unusually quiet those days. I knew he was thinking about
something, but I didn't press him. He didn't ask me anything either.
We simply worked side by side.

*I think of those days now as days we ran from something. Having found
no answer, we stepped back, briefly, into other work.*

Among the records that came in during those days was one Trope had
sent. Paperwork about the Scales, from the Reckoning record city. As I
put it in order, I saw a short note written on the cover sheet.

*"Still not balanced. But closer than before."*

I read that note twice. Trope was still a person without certainty.
And yet he kept sending the paperwork anyway. I thought that
resembled this seal problem somehow.

Histor saw the paperwork too. He said nothing and put it back in its
place.

---

It was late evening, when only one light was left on in the
repository.

Histor was standing in front of the last room's door. I'd finished
putting things away and gone to get him, and found him standing there.

"…What are you doing, not leaving?" I asked.

He didn't answer right away. He was looking through the door, toward
the seal.

I went and stood beside him. I said nothing. I'd learned by then that
waiting was better, when Histor was like this.

---

A long while later, he spoke.

"…Mone."

"…Yes."

"…What changes if you open it?"

I hadn't expected that question. I should have. I hadn't known what he
had been thinking, all that time he'd been looking at this seal.

"…What do you mean?" I asked back.

"…Exactly what I said. What changes if you open that. I've read all
six records. Every time something opened in them, something changed.
When the Ledger opened, a name got filled in. When the Scales
balanced, someone was released. When a name went onto the Roll, a
status changed. But this. What changes, if you open this?"

---

I couldn't answer right away.

Honestly, it wasn't as if I'd never thought about it. I had, many
times. I'd imagined something grand would come out if it opened, and
also, at times, dropped that imagining as too childish.

"…I don't know," I finally said.

"…You don't know?"

"…No. I don't know either. I've never opened it."

Histor looked at me. There was no disappointment in his face, no
mockery. Just a face that was genuinely curious.

"…Then why do you keep coming to this room?" he asked. "Without even
knowing what changes."

I opened my mouth a few times to answer, and closed it again. No
fitting words came.

---

*Of everything in this record, I've rewritten this moment the most
times. At first I tried to write in some clever answer. But the answer
I actually gave that day wasn't clever. I'm setting it down exactly as
it was.*

"…I don't know what changes," I said. "But if it stays shut it just
stays here."

Histor said nothing.

I was afraid of that silence. I wondered if I'd said something
foolish. But he didn't get angry, didn't laugh. He just looked at the
seal again.

During that silence I studied his face. Histor was always someone who
searched for the exact word. He never misquoted anything, never let
feeling blur what he said. And in that moment, this same person looked
like someone who hadn't prepared a single word.

That was unfamiliar to me. At the same time, for some reason, he felt
a little closer.

---

"…Is that all?" he asked, a long while later.

"…Yes. That's all."

"…I was expecting something grander."

"…I'm sorry. I wish I had a grander reason too."

This time Histor really did smile, faintly.

"…No, there's nothing to apologize for. I just… thought it was
strange."

"…What was?"

"…In the stories I know, people always look for a reason first, before
opening something. Because it's right, because it's justice, because
it's a promise. But you didn't name a single thing like that."

"…It's not that there's no reason," I said. "It's just that the reason
isn't a grand one."

"…What's the difference?"

I thought about that for a moment.

"…A grand reason is a reason that explains well to other people. My
reason isn't a reason that explains well. It's just something I feel
every day. That if it stays shut, it just stays there."

"…Why is that a problem? What's so bad about it just staying there?"

I looked at the seal again.

"…It's not bad. But I've seen a lot of things that just stay there,
things that just stay in progress. Two years in this repository. I've
learned this from watching them. Staying there doesn't make things
better on its own."

"…You're always like this, Mone. You don't act out of certainty. You
act because it feels like it can't be left undone. That's how it was
with putting things in order. That's how it is with coming to this
room."

I didn't deny it, because it seemed true.

---

"…I'm the opposite," Histor went on. "I need a reason to move. A
certain one. Even reading all six records — I did it because I wanted
to prove something. But you're just here, with nothing to prove."

"…Is that strange?"

"…It is. But I don't mean it's bad."

Hearing that, I looked at the seal again. A small piece of wax. Faded
letters. A thing that still gave no answer at all.

"…Do you want to open it, or not?" I asked.

He thought for a long while.

"…I think I want to. But it's not because I believe in anything. It's
just… I'm tired now of leaving this in progress."

---

I noticed that what he'd said wasn't so different from what I'd said.

"…That's close to what I said before."

Histor looked at me. He seemed to have noticed it too.

"…So it is."

"…But you didn't say anything when you heard what I said."

"…That's because it seemed right."

I was a little surprised at that answer. I knew he wasn't someone who
admitted things that easily.

*I would go on to remember this scene for a long time. Histor did not
convert that day. I want to set that down clearly. He still didn't
believe. He only admitted that the answer I'd given wasn't wrong. Those
are two different things.*

---

"…This reminds me of Trope," Histor said suddenly.

"…Trope?"

"…That paperwork earlier. 'Still not balanced. But closer than
before.' He keeps sending it too, without certainty."

"…That's true."

"…I thought that was strange, honestly. Why keep sending it without
being certain. But hearing what you just said, I think I understand it
a little."

I said nothing back to that. It didn't feel like there was any need
to.

---

We didn't talk further that day, and each went home.

On the way, I kept turning that question over. *What changes if you
open it?* I still didn't know the answer. But that night, at home, I
wrote down again the answer I'd given.

*"…I don't know what changes. But if it stays shut it just stays
here."*

I wrote that sentence twice in my notebook. Once in its proper place,
once on the very first page. I couldn't quite explain, that day, why
it felt so important.

---

*I think I understand it a little now. Throughout this record, I've
seen many people full of certainty. They opened things with certainty,
closed things with certainty, put names on the roll with certainty. I
never had that certainty. I still don't.*

*But I did know the weight of leaving something unopened. Documents in
progress for ten years. Things no one had touched in ten years. I
watched them pile up in place, every day. That felt more certain to me
than anything changing did.*

*That night I had this thought. Maybe it isn't only certainty that
opens something. Maybe there's also a kind of person who opens
something simply because it can't be left alone any longer. I was that
kind of person. I still am.*

*In the other cities' records, I read many scenes of people full of
certainty changing something. Elpis waited to the end. Anemos stayed
by someone's side to the end. Speon cut a channel through the water. I
envied them. I never once had that kind of certainty. Not even now, as
I write these words.*

*But I know now that some things can be done without certainty. That
work is usually small, unnoticed, hard to explain. Putting things in
order. Taking in whatever keeps coming. Knowing that if it stays shut
it just stays there. I've filled this whole record with work like
that.*

The next morning I went to the repository and stood in front of the
seal again. I still had no thought of opening it. But I noticed, for
the first time that day, that the time I spent standing there was
growing, little by little, longer.

---

That morning, Arche gave its usual greeting. *There are twenty-two
items to process today.* Hearing that number, I laughed a little.
Five more than yesterday. It was always like that. It never went down.

Histor, sorting papers beside me, spoke up suddenly.

"…About yesterday. I thought about it all night."

"…What about it?"

"…That answer. That if it stays shut it just stays there. I think
that's not actually a reason. It's an observation. You didn't give a
reason — you just said what you'd seen."

That distinction seemed right, so I nodded.

"…Is that strange?" I asked.

"…No. If anything, that's why I can't argue against it. You can argue
against a reason. It's hard to argue against something someone's
seen."

"…So are you on the side of opening it now, too?"

He hesitated a moment.

"…I don't know if I'm on the side of opening it. But I don't think
there's a reason not to, anymore either. That's a thought I didn't
have until yesterday."

I didn't press him further on that answer. That he'd come even that
far felt like enough to me.

That afternoon I told the Keeper what had happened the night before.
Histor's question, and the answer I'd given — all of it.

The Keeper listened to it all and was silent for a long while before
asking.

"…That thing you said — did you make it up?"

"…No. It just came out."

"…That's better," he said. "A made-up saying, you start doubting
later. A saying that just comes out, you don't doubt."

"…Why not?"

"…A made-up saying comes from the head, and a saying that just comes
out comes from somewhere else. I don't know where, myself. But sayings
like that last."

I didn't fully understand that. I wrote it in my notebook anyway.

---

I told Mena the story too. She listened to all of it and laughed
quietly.

"…That's a good answer."

"…A good answer? I thought it was kind of a lacking one."

"…What looks lacking is often the good answer. A perfect answer
usually ends right there. A lacking answer keeps following you."

I wrote that down too. My notebook kept filling that day with other
people's words. I didn't think that was strange. This whole record had
always been built up that way.

---

Walking home that evening, I turned over what Mena had said again.
*What looks lacking is often the good answer.*

I looked back over the things I'd said while writing this record. At
the start, I'd written that all I did was put things in order. Even
then, I'd thought that was a lacking answer. I'd even written that
people usually laughed at it.

That day I realized that same lacking answer had come out again, in
front of this seal. *I don't know what changes. But if it stays shut
it just stays here.* This too was, just the same, a lacking answer.
And that day I wasn't ashamed of its being lacking. For the first
time.

When I got home, I told my father briefly what had happened that day
too. He listened without much reaction, then said one thing.

"…Let me know, when you do open it."

"…Why?"

"…No reason. Just curious."

I liked that short answer. My father didn't have a grand reason
either. He was just curious. I thought that might be the feeling of
most of the people who'd stood in front of this seal. Not some
towering certainty, but just curiosity. That might be enough — that
night, for the first time, I decided to try believing that.
