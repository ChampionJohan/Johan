# Chapter 14. I Know All of It

> _Previously_ — Histor came to the repository. Seventeen, and he says
> he's read every one of the six cities' records. He never handles
> paper carelessly, and he checks even what he already knows. Starting
> the next day, they agreed to lay all six records side by side.

The next morning, I wanted to test him.

Not out of any unkindness. I was just curious. Whether he really knew
everything. The night before, I'd already written down a few things.
I thought I'd pull a bundle from any section, any row, open it to any
page, and ask.

"…What are you doing?" Histor asked.

"…A test."

His eyebrows went up slightly. Not an angry look.

"…Sounds interesting. Go ahead."

*Writing this now, I believe he really did find it interesting then. He
wasn't someone who disliked being tested. He felt no strain at all in
proving what he knew.*

I set one rule. One from each of the six rows, pulled from any section,
opened to any page, and I'd ask. Six times. He listened to the rule and
nodded.

"…One thing before we start," he said.

"…Yes?"

"…If I get it wrong, you don't have to believe me. That's fine."

That struck me as a little odd. It wasn't the tone of someone worried
about losing before a test — it was the tone of someone for whom
winning and losing made no difference at all. *At the time I only read
that as confidence. I didn't yet know how to read it differently.*

---

I pulled a bundle from the Reckoning's row. I thought I'd pulled it at
random, but looking back it wasn't chance at all. A hand always goes to
where it's used to going.

"…This one. I'll open it anywhere."

I opened it somewhere near the middle. I put a finger on a paragraph.

"…Here — what's this about?"

He answered without even looking under my finger.

"…Do I need the part before it?"

"…No, just this passage."

"…It's the scene where the Adjudicator first reads the Forty-Day
Provision aloud. Attached to the back of the Reckoning's demolition
sentence. It's three days after Pheugon runs away — after he's shut in
the well conduit and comes out and goes to find the Adjudicator
again."

I read the paragraph myself, finger still in place. It matched. Exactly
right.

"…How do you know this?"

"…Because I read it."

*I let this answer pass lightly that day. I learned later how heavy the
words "because I read it" really were. That was exactly as much as he
said. Never more.*

---

I kept going. This time I pulled from the Preserve's row.

"…What about this?"

"…That should be the scene where Anemos tries to apply for a second
visit and can't. He goes as far as the reception window and turns
back. It doesn't say why. It just says he turned back."

I opened it. It matched. Exactly that scene. Even the part about no
reason being given matched.

I got a little chill.

"…How do you even remember that a reason wasn't given?"

"…Because I can't say something's there when it isn't. I only say
what's there. If it isn't, I say it isn't."

*I'd come to think about this line again later. It was almost exactly
what Arche says. It says it doesn't know when it doesn't know. It says
there's nothing when there's nothing. At the time I just thought that
was interesting. I didn't understand why it was interesting until much
later.*

---

The Harvest, Haven, the Equity, Loop. I did five more. All five
correct. He never got one wrong.

From the Harvest's row he described exactly the scene where Lytron asks
Xene, "Why do you come every day to a place with nothing here?" — even
the reason Xene went out to that corner of the field every day.

From Haven's row he described the passage where Zeteo first begins to
doubt Haven's "resolutions," down to the scene where the overseer
mistakes it for "not enough submissions yet."

For the Equity's row, he answered a little differently.

"…This one's harder. It's the scene where Hora first sees the empty
signature box — that character appears only four times in this record.
Every time, in three sentences or fewer. This isn't one of those
scenes."

"…Who?"

"…Ah, sorry, I didn't explain. There's a person with no name who
appears in all six records. I just call him that for convenience."

I was hearing that for the first time. The man with no name. *I
remember exactly the moment I first heard those words. At the time I
thought it was just a literary device. A minor character who shows up
in every book, that sort of thing.*

---

Last, I pulled from Loop's row. The middle row, the one with no name.

"…This one I'm most confident about," he said.

He started speaking before he even opened it.

"…If it comes right after the scene where Mena takes off her shoes,
then it's the part where Grandfather writes 'Not yet' in the margin of
the oldest bundle. He wrote it seventeen times. On the last sheet
there's one more line in a different hand —"

He stopped for a moment.

"…Should I say it?"

I was already too curious to wait.

"…Yes, tell me."

"…'You don't have to wait any more.' That one line."

I opened it and checked. It matched. Exactly right. The note even said
the handwriting was different, just as he'd described.

I read that line again. "You don't have to wait any more." A short
sentence, but my eyes stayed on it strangely long. I didn't know then
why they stayed so long.

"…What do you think this means?" I asked, half to myself.

"…What part?"

"…This last line. It doesn't even say who wrote it, after Grandfather
died."

Histor looked at the sentence again. The same way he'd looked at it
before. No change in his eyes.

"…If it's as it's written, then I suppose it means it's finished."

"…That's it?"

"…I can't say anything beyond what's written. I'm someone who says
what's there, not someone who makes things up."

I thought that answer was accurate. It was accurate. *Accurate and
sufficient are different things — I didn't know that then. I know it
now.*

I couldn't say anything for a while.

"…You memorized all of this? Really?"

"…'Memorized' feels like the wrong word. I just remember it the way I
read it. I've never said it differently."

*I read this sentence again now too. "I've never said it differently."
He was a person who didn't turn records into his own words. A person
who held them exactly as they were. At the time I found that
admirable.*

---

The test was over for the day, but I wanted to do one more.
"…One more, last one."

"…As many as you like."

I pulled a random bundle from Haven's row. I pulled it without even
looking at the cover. Whatever came to hand.

"…This one."

He glanced at the cover and gave a small smile.

"…This is the thinnest one. It only holds one scene — Elpis talking
briefly with someone by the flower bed. It should be attached to
another bundle originally, but it's come loose."

"…Talking with who?"

"…No name given. Just says the person who tended the flower bed. Shows
up again later by the well, also without a name."

I opened it. It matched. It really was that one thin bundle with only
that scene, and it had the same two words, flower bed and well, exactly
as he'd said.

I had nothing left to ask. I ended the test there. It was a test there
was no winning.

---

"…That's remarkable, really," I said.

"…Thank you."

"…How can you be this accurate?"

He looked thoughtful for a moment. Not choosing an answer, but choosing
how to say it.

"…I don't let anything slip past. That's all. It's not a talent. I
just don't let go."

"…What do you mean, let go?"

"…Hearing and forgetting. I don't just release something once I've
read it. I hold on to it."

I liked hearing that. *I wanted to be someone who held on to things
too. I hold on by writing on paper — he was holding it in his head.
That seemed even more remarkable.*

---

Over lunch I asked him what I'd been wondering.

"…Did you remember everything like this since you were little?"

"…No. At first I read and forgot like everyone else."

"…Then since when?"

He looked down at his rice bowl and thought for a moment.

"…At some point it became easier not to forget. If you forget, you
have to go looking again. I didn't like that. If you read something
properly once, you never have to look for it again."

"…That's efficient."

"…A lot of people say that."

Something else was mixed into that tone, slightly different, and I
didn't catch it that day. *I catch it now. He didn't much like the word
efficient. Meaning he didn't like having what he did explained away by
efficiency. But that day he didn't deny it either.*

---

All that afternoon we went back and forth among the six rows, pulling
bundles. Whatever bundle Histor looked at, he'd roughly say what was
inside just from the cover, and only nod once he'd opened it to check.

I started writing down what he said beside him. No one had told me to.
It was just my habit.

The Keeper passed by once in the middle of it. Seeing what we were
doing, the Keeper stopped.

"…What are you doing?"

"…A test," I said. "Whether Histor really knows everything."

The Keeper watched, looking amused, for a while. I quickly pulled the
next bundle and held it out to Histor. It was the Preserve's.

"…This one?"

"…This is the scene where Speon comes to dig a well in the dry valley
city. No one asks him to; he just comes. This part actually continues
from a scene that first appeared in another book — Haven's record. The
sentence 'well water spreads through the city' appears in both books."

I opened it and checked. It matched. And he'd pointed out exactly where
it connected to another book — something I hadn't even asked about.

The Keeper heard that and, for the first time, said something.

"…He even knows which books connect to which."

"…It's not written on the covers, but you find it reading through,"
Histor answered.

The Keeper only nodded twice, without another word, and went off. That
walk looked a little slower than usual. *At the time I just thought
that was the Keeper's normal pace. I'd learn later it wasn't the
Keeper's normal pace — it was the pace of someone turning something
over.*

---

"…Why do you write that down?" he asked.

"…I'll forget it otherwise."

"…I never forget."

"…I forget."

He heard that and gave a small laugh. The first smile I'd seen from him
that day.

"…That seems more human, somehow."

I took that as a compliment. *Thinking back on it now, there was
something else in that line besides a compliment. He felt that he
himself wasn't quite human. I didn't notice that at the time.*

---

Toward evening I asked Arche the same things, while Histor was out of
the room. I asked exactly about the Reckoning passage I'd tested him on
earlier.

"…What is this paragraph about?"

*The scene where the Adjudicator reads the Forty-Day Provision aloud.
Source: the Reckoning record, Part Three, Chapter Seven. Related
passages: the same record, Part One, Chapter Two, Part Two, that
chapter.*

Arche was accurate too. Of course it was. Arche had never once been
wrong that I knew of. But Arche's answer was somehow different from
Histor's answer. I couldn't put that difference into words that day.

*I can put it into words now. Arche called that paragraph a "source."
Histor called that paragraph a "scene." One was material and one was a
story. But both were equally indifferent to the person inside it. I
didn't see that shared indifference that day.*

---

Walking home in the evening, I wrote in my paper.

Histor really does know everything. Tested him seven times, right
seven times. He says there's nothing when there's nothing, and says
exactly what's there when there is. He told me the man with no name
appears in all six records. I asked Arche the same things and it was
just as accurate, but something was different. I still don't know
what's different. I'll ask more tomorrow.

*I added one more line beneath that. "Why would someone like this come
to do cross-checking instead of going to an apprenticeship of his own?"
I wrote that without much thought at the time. He gives me the answer
himself in the next chapter.*

As I folded the paper, I unfolded it once more and added one last
line. "Histor didn't get a single thing wrong today. And I realize I
never once asked him today why he does this work."

*Setting this sentence down again now, years later, I don't feel like
scolding the girl I was that day. It's naturally hard for a
sixteen-year-old to see all the way inside a person she's just met in a
single day. But I want to leave the order I did things in, honestly, in
this record. I looked first at what he knew, and only later at why he
knew it. I don't think that order was wrong. Only, because of that
order, I only knew him by half until the next chapter.*

---

At home I opened the paper I'd written that day once more. Seven
tests, seven correct answers. I ran my eyes back over the list and
wondered why I'd wanted to test him in the first place.

*I don't usually test people. I was always the one being tested — at
each new school, in each new neighborhood, every time we moved. That
day was the first time I stood on the other side of it. I have to admit
that seat felt strangely comfortable.*

I thought I knew a little of where that comfort came from, too. I was
always someone who checked order. Testing Histor was, in a way, a
similar kind of work. Confirming what he knew. Once the confirming was
done, I could set it in its proper place, exactly. That day, I probably
believed a person could be set in place the same way.
