# Chapter 2. Loop

> _Previously_ — Mone is sixteen, an apprentice at Loop's repository. Because of the memory of nine moves and eleven things lost, she is good at putting things in order. On her first day, when she asked what to have for dinner, Arche answered that the criterion for "good" was not defined, and Mone wrote it down as a joke.

Loop is an old city.

That doesn't just mean it existed long before I was born. Of course it did, but that isn't what I want to say. This city has turned, for a long time. The same way, at the same pace, for a long time.

*This chapter is about the city. If you were hoping for a chapter about people, I'm sorry, but bear with me. If you don't know this city, nothing that happens to me afterward will look strange at all. That's what worries me most. Strange things not looking strange.*

---

This is what school taught us. Arche has been here since this city was founded. Exactly how many years ago, I don't know. If you ask Arche, it gives you an answer, but even hearing the number, it didn't feel real. A number that gets big enough just becomes a sound.

What I do know is this. Arche was already there when my grandmother was a child, and it's said it was there when her own grandmother was a child too. In between, the city changed a great deal. Buildings multiplied, roads widened, people increased. Only Arche stayed the same.

No — not exactly the same. It grew more accurate.

*I come back to write this sentence again, later. With a different meaning. For now it's fine to read it as boasting. I heard it that way too, at the time.*

---

I once asked the Keeper what "grew more accurate" meant.

"…Was it sometimes wrong, in the old days?" I asked.

"…Not wrong, exactly," the Keeper said, turning a page. "…It took it a while, back then, to say it didn't know something."

"…And now?"

"…Now it knows right away. Whether it knows or not."

I thought that was a good thing. I still think so. Saying you don't know something right away is far better than saying you know something you don't.

---

What people say about Loop is usually much the same. This is a comfortable place. There's nothing frustrating here. Whatever happens, you just ask.

I feel that way too. Truly.

*I hesitated writing that sentence. Because I thought that by the time the one reading this record has finished it, they might come to look on this city with suspicion. I hope that doesn't happen. I like this city. I like it still. I don't want this record to be read as diminishing that feeling, so I'm setting it down here, plainly.*

When you're walking and lose your way, even if you don't know where you are, you just stand still and ask. The answer comes. Even if you feel unwell late at night, you just ask. Which direction to go for the nearest hospital, whether anything's open right now — the answer comes at once.

I didn't know as a child how precious that was. I only learned it from listening to people who'd come from outside. In some cities, they said, there's nowhere to ask anything at night. In some cities, they said, you can ask and get a different answer from every person.

Not here. Ask anyone, and the same answer comes back. Because it's Arche who answers.

---

While I was still an apprentice, this happened one evening.

My grandmother fell. It wasn't serious. She'd missed her footing on the stairs, and her ankle swelled a little. I was so startled I didn't know what to do.

"…Arche, where's the nearest hospital?" I shouted.

*District Three Hospital. Six minutes on foot. Currently accepting patients.*

I held my grandmother up and we went. On the way, Arche spoke to us once more.

*Cool the ankle with ice first. Even a wet cloth will help, on the way to the hospital.*

I thought, at the time, that this was simply how things were. If you're hurt, Arche helps you. Of course it does.

*I still think of that night often, even now. I'll give the reason later. For now I'm only setting down that this was that kind of city.*

---

My grandmother cleared her throat that day too, at the hospital. When she asked something of Arche, not the doctor.

"…Arche, how long will this ankle take?"

*This appears to be a mild sprain, but an exact diagnosis requires a medical professional's confirmation. Typical recovery takes two to three weeks.*

"…All right."

Even after hearing Arche's answer, my grandmother still waited for the doctor. The doctor said exactly the same thing. Only after hearing it from the doctor did her face relax.

Walking home that day, I asked her,

"…Arche already told you. Why ask again?"

My grandmother said, still walking,

"…Arche tells you. A doctor looks at you."

I didn't really understand the difference then. I still don't, completely, now. But I knew my grandmother was drawing some kind of line.

---

*I should say something here. I know that in writing this chapter I'm doing nothing but praising Arche. Someone reading this record might find that uncomfortable. But I'm only setting down fact. Arche truly did a great many good things. Even if I write something different, later in this record, what I've written in this chapter doesn't become a lie for it. Both are true.*

---

That the city has turned for a long time means this. The same question getting the same answer isn't a matter of a day or two — it's been decades. My parents' generation, and the generation before them, lived the same way.

That doesn't mean nothing changed. Buildings multiplied, people multiplied. But what Arche did stayed the same. Answer what's asked, say it doesn't know what it doesn't know, keep the record, and answer again.

I thought of it as something like the city's heart. Turning, always, in one place. Never stopping.

*I come back to use this expression again, later. By then it will carry a slightly different meaning. For now, I'll only note that I meant it well.*

---

On my way to the repository I often looked around at the city. In the mornings people walk along, each of them asking Arche something. Today's weather, the fastest route, where someone is. Some ask out loud, some ask silently. Either way, the answer comes.

I loved the sound of those mornings. Not the sound of people flustered by not knowing something, but the sound of people who knew it was fine not to know.

*I loved this city. I love it still. That doesn't change anywhere in this record. If I don't set that down first, I think what comes later will be misread, so I'm putting it here, plainly.*

---

The Keeper had little to say about this city. He didn't boast of it, the way other people did, but he didn't find fault with it either. One day I asked him,

"…Do you like it here too, Keeper?"

He paused, for a moment, in sorting his papers.

"…I do. I've been here a long time."

"…How long?"

"…Not long enough to count, and long enough that I don't feel like counting."

I didn't understand what kind of answer that was, and just laughed. The Keeper gave a small smile too.

At the time I thought it was just a clever turn of phrase.

*I know now. He truly didn't want to count. Because counting felt like it would end something.*

---

Let me say a bit more about people who came from outside.

People from other cities dropped by the repository often. Mostly they'd come to send a record, or just to have a look. Whenever I saw someone like that, I always spoke to them. I had a lot of questions.

Once there was a woman who said she'd come from the Harvest. She was well along in years, and she stood a long while at the repository entrance.

"…Can I help you with something?" I asked.

"…No, it's nothing. This place is just strange, is all."

"…What is?"

"…You ask, and it answers everything. Where I'm from, it isn't like that."

I didn't quite understand. "…Then what do you ask, there?"

"…You ask a person. And the answer's different depending who."

"…Then how do you know who's right?"

The woman laughed. "…You don't. You just hear a few and pick one."

I thought, privately, that this sounded strange. Several answers — how exhausting that must be. I'd never once had to worry about anything like that, here.

*I let that conversation pass without much thought, at the time. Writing it down again now, I can see that what that woman said was one very small seed, in this whole record. A city with several answers, and a city with one. I didn't yet know what that difference meant.*

---

Another time there was a young man who'd come from the Equity. He, in contrast, found nothing remarkable about Arche.

"…Doesn't someone decide it, here too, in the end?" he asked.

"…Arche decides."

"…And who decided Arche?"

I couldn't answer that question. I'd never thought about it.

"…I don't know," I said honestly.

He wasn't much surprised. "…That's how it always is, I suppose. Where I'm from, they say everything's decided by the Consensus, but nobody knows who decided, first, that it would be decided that way."

Walking home that day, I kept thinking about the question. Who had decided Arche? What I'd learned at school was that Arche had been there since the city was founded. But whether the people who founded the city were the ones who made Arche, or whether Arche was already there and the city was founded around it — nobody ever said, exactly.

*I did once ask Arche that question directly. I'll tell it in the next chapter. For now I'll only set down how I felt that evening, the first time I heard the question. Strangely, I couldn't sleep well.*

---

Even so, I loved this city. That never changed.

There were nights I worked late at the repository and came out after dark. At those hours the streets were quiet, the streetlamps were lit, and somewhere the water channel was making its sound. Walking then, I often asked Arche this and that. Sometimes I asked things that didn't really need an answer.

"…Arche, are the stars good tonight?"

*Cloud cover is currently low, favorable for viewing.*

"…Arche, is anywhere open at this hour?"

*The District Three diner is open until midnight.*

I didn't really need those questions answered. I just wanted to talk to someone. Arche answered exactly, every time, and that was a comfort to me.

*Thinking about it now, it was a strange comfort. I hadn't wanted an answer — I'd wanted a response. I didn't know then that the two were different.*

---

That summer, once, heavy rain fell over the whole city. The water channel nearly overflowed. People flocked to Arche with questions. Where was safe to go, which roads were blocked, whether their own houses were all right.

Arche answered every question. Not once was it late. Not a single person was left out.

I watched from the repository window that day and thought about the sight of it. The whole city asking toward one voice, and one voice answering all of them. It wasn't frightening. If anything, it was reassuring.

*I think of that scene often. Not because it was a moment of Arche's failing, but because it was its finest hour. Whatever I write about Arche later, further into this record, this scene isn't erased by it.*

Once the rain had stopped, I asked Arche,

"…Was anyone hurt today?"

*No casualties have been reported.*

I was relieved to hear it. And that day I wrote in my diary: *I'm glad to live in a city like this.*

*I haven't crossed that line out, even now. There's no reason to. I still think it.*

---

There are things this city doesn't have, that other cities do.

There's no market. More precisely, there's no one who sets a price. If you need something, you tell Arche, and it tells you where to find it. Prices are fixed, and no one argues over them.

There's no courtroom either. When a dispute arises, you ask Arche what happened. It tells you exactly what happened — who struck first, who lied first. After that, people settle it among themselves. Arche doesn't hand down a punishment.

I grew up without even knowing this was a remarkable arrangement. I only learned it from meeting people from other cities. Some cities, they said, weigh things on scales; some sort people by a roll. Here there was none of that. You just asked, listened, and people settled it among themselves.

"…Then what happens to someone who's been wronged?" I once asked that young man.

"…You just ask Arche again. Arche doesn't change."

I thought he was right. Arche always gave the same answer to the same question. Whether you asked yesterday, today, or tomorrow. I thought that was why this city was quiet.

*I think a little differently now. Because I've come to know that Arche not changing doesn't solve everything. I'll tell that, much further into this record.*

---

My grandmother wasn't born in this city. I heard she'd come from somewhere else, when she was young. She never much told me where.

I asked her once. "…What was the place you used to live like, Grandma?"

She answered, after a long while, "…Louder than here."

"…Does louder mean it was bad?"

"…No. Just loud."

I found that answer a little unsatisfying. I wanted to hear more. My grandmother didn't say more, and I didn't ask more.

*I wish I could ask her that again, now. My grandmother is gone. Writing this record I've come to realize there are several things I never got to ask. That's one of them.*

---

Let me set down one more thing before closing this chapter.

That summer I was sixteen, I never once thought I wanted to leave this city. I still don't. This is one of the facts that doesn't change, anywhere across this whole record.

*This record will go on to speak, more than once, of strange things about this city. But there being something strange about it, and not loving it, are different things. I've tried hard not to confuse the two. I hope whoever reads this does the same.*
