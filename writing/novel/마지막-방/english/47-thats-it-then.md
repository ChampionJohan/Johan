# Chapter 47. That's It, Then

> _Previously_ — The six sets left, one by one, for their own cities.
> The repository slowly emptied out. Only the seventh set, Loop's own
> share, still wasn't finished by spring.

When spring came, the work picked up speed.

The hand copying the blank pages was still slow, but the pages that
had piled up little by little all winter had grown fairly thick by
now. That spring, I went to the last room almost every day.

---

Around that time, there were usually several people in the room.

Histor, Mena, the Keeper now and then. And one more person.

I don't know exactly when he first appeared in that room. Since some
day in winter, he'd simply been sitting there. He would press the
folded corner of a sheet flat with his fingertip, and smooth the
crease again, the way you'd iron out a mark. No one had ever asked him
to do that work. He was just doing it.

"…Who is that?" I once asked Histor.

Histor glanced over that way and shrugged.

"…I don't know. I thought he'd been here from the start too."

I thought that answer was strange. But the strangeness didn't last.
He was simply someone whose hands were quick, who was quiet, who
never got in the way. A few days later I too had come to take his
being there for granted.

---

Looking back later, no one could say exactly which day he'd first
come.

The Keeper said it was early winter. Mena said a little before that.
Histor said he couldn't remember at all. "…I'm having more trouble
remembering a day he wasn't there," he said, and I thought that was
a strange answer. But turning it over myself, it was much the same. I
too remembered the days he'd been there, instead of the days he
hadn't.

*I've decided not to invent something that isn't in this record. I
don't know when he came. No one does. I'll only set down this one
fact. No one knew, and no one found that strange.*

---

His hands were good at folding paper.

I watched them a few times, beside him. Whenever he picked up a
sheet, he'd smooth it once with his palm first. Then he'd fold it. The
crease was always exact. Never once crooked.

"…How do you fold it so straight?" I asked.

"…I've done it a long time," he answered.

His voice was low, unhurried. Hearing it, I felt something familiar,
though I couldn't place what. I didn't hold onto that feeling for
long. There was a lot of work that day.

"…Where are you from?" I asked again.

He didn't answer that right away. He folded the paper once more, then
said.

"…Here and there."

I didn't feel that answer was careless. If anything, it sounded exact.
I didn't press further.

---

Histor exchanged a few words with him now and then.

Mostly about paper. Which direction to fold so the grain wasn't
ruined, how thin the paste had to be spread. Histor was someone who
always knew things exactly, but in front of this man, he was simply
someone learning.

"…Is this right?" Histor asked once, showing him a folded sheet.

He took it and looked it over.

"…Almost there. Here, once more."

He pressed his fingertip lightly against the crease. Histor watched
and nodded.

"…Thank you."

"…Not at all."

Listening to that short exchange from beside them, I felt there was no
particular tension between the two. Histor was someone who'd read all
six records and prided himself on knowing everything there was to
know. But in front of this man, he was simply a learner. I didn't
think that was strange. Folding paper was something anyone could
learn.

---

He didn't come every day. Some days he came, some he didn't. He never
said beforehand that he'd come, and never apologized for not coming.

"…He's not here today," I said to Mena one day.

"…Seems not."

Mena said it with a face that didn't seem to mind much either way. I
found that a little strange, but soon I stopped minding it too. He
was someone who came and didn't come. That was all.

*Writing this part now, I ask myself why no one found him strange, at
the time. I don't know the answer. I can only write that his being
there felt natural. I decided not to leave a lie in this record, so I
write it exactly that way.*

---

The Keeper crossed paths with him a few times too.

"…You've been lending a hand, lately," the Keeper said to him one
day.

"…Yes. There was work to be done."

"…Thank you."

"…Not at all."

That was all. The Keeper didn't ask any further. Listening to that
exchange from beside them, I felt once more that in this city, no one
asks for qualification when someone offers to help. Arche had been
that way from the start. It only answered what was asked, and never
pressed into what wasn't.

One cold day, the Keeper offered him a cup of tea.

"…Have this. You look like you've walked a long way."

He took the cup.

"…Thank you."

"…Do you come from far?"

"…From nearby. But somehow it always takes a while, walking."

The Keeper nodded at that answer with no particular change of
expression. That short exchange stayed with me, somehow. Saying he'd
come from nearby, and yet it always took a while. I didn't ask what
that meant.

---

Mena, in particular, would watch him quietly.

She didn't speak to him much. But I saw her, a few times, looking for
a long while at his hands. The hands that folded paper. I asked her
once.

"…Mena, do you know him?"

Mena looked at me and smiled faintly.

"…No. I don't."

"…Then why do you look at him like that?"

"…No reason. He just looks familiar. But that feeling is often wrong,
for me. At my age, everyone starts to look a little familiar."

I took that as it was. I didn't press further.

---

Once, a sheet of paper I was copying tore. I must have pressed too
hard. I was flustered right there — should I start a new sheet, or
leave it as it was?

He saw it and held out his hand.

"…Give it here."

I handed him the torn paper. He turned it over, laid a thin sheet over
the tear, and glued it from behind. From the front, it barely showed.

"…This will do it," he said, handing the paper back.

"…Thank you," I said. "But that doesn't erase the flaw that was
already there, does it."

"…No. The flaw stays. It just won't tear anymore."

That answer stayed with me longer than I expected, somehow. I took
the paper back and set it in its place. The tear mark could only be
seen if you held it up to the light.

---

As spring deepened, the work of copying the blank pages began to show
an end in sight.

By then I'd forgotten my hand even ached. So had Histor. We just sat
in that spot every day and moved the pages, and beside us, he folded.

One afternoon I was holding the last remaining page. Truly the final
sheet. I copied it and drew the recipient box exactly as it was. One
line, and beside it, the small letters, *Recipient.* I left that box
empty. The way the original had been.

When I set the copied sheet down, he picked it up. He seemed to know
it was the last page. As he always did, he smoothed it once with his
palm, and folded it.

Everyone in the room watched that hand. Histor, Mena, the Keeper, and
me.

He pressed the last crease once more with his fingertip. Then he set
it on top of the sheaf of papers.

---

"…That's it, then."

That was what he said. Not a loud voice. No particular inflection.
Just the words of someone who'd finished a job.

No one held their breath. No one's face showed surprise at those
words. The Keeper, sorting another bundle beside him, answered
plainly, "…Thank you for your work." Mena was looking out the window.
Histor was wiping the last trace of paste off his hands.

Those words raised no ripple at all in the room.

In that moment I stopped the hand holding my pen. I don't know why,
myself. I just didn't want to lose that sentence.

---

"…Is it all finished now?" Histor asked, wiping his hands. That
question wasn't for him — it was for me.

"…Yes," I answered in his place. "The seventh set. All finished."

"…At last," the Keeper said, briefly.

"…We should decide what to have for dinner," Mena said, turning from
the window. She said it as naturally as if it had nothing at all to do
with what had just happened.

"…Noodles, for me," Histor answered, wiping his hands.

"…We had noodles yesterday too."

"…So, today as well."

Listening to that exchange, I felt strangely at ease. Just moments
before, the last page of the seventh set had been finished in that
very spot. And yet people were talking about what to have for dinner.
I felt there was no distance at all between those two things.

He looked over the bundle he'd folded once more, right there, and
stood up.

"…I'll be going now," he said.

"…Stay for some tea, at least," the Keeper said.

"…Next time."

He said only that and left the room. No one held him back. Neither did
I. I watched his back for a moment as he went out the door. His steps
weren't hurried. No different from any other day.

---

*Writing this part, I feel a pressure to write something more. What
that saying meant, who that man was, why no one was surprised. I've
decided not to give in to that pressure. I'll set down only what
happened that day, exactly as it was. He was helping fold paper.
After folding the last page, he said, "…That's it, then." No one
took it as anything special. That's all of it.*

That evening I went home and opened my notebook. I wrote down what had
happened that day. Briefly, as always.

*The seventh set finished today. He folded the last page. After
folding it he said this. "…That's it, then."*

I looked at that sentence for a long while after writing it. I wanted
to add more. What it meant, why that saying had stayed with me. In the
end I added nothing more.

*I wrote, at the very start of this record, that there are a few
things in it I've left unpolished. This is one of them. I won't refine
this sentence any further.*

Beside it I wrote one more line. *No one took these words as
anything special. Only I wrote them down.* I left that sentence as it
was too. I know both sentences might look childish. Even so, I didn't
erase them.

---

The next day, I thought of bringing the story up again at the
repository. I started to ask Histor, then stopped. He was already
sorting other bundles, like someone who'd already forgotten it.

"…Will that person come back?" I finally asked.

Histor thought a moment before answering.

"…Who knows. He might, he might not."

"…Aren't you curious? Who he is?"

He shrugged at that question.

"…I am curious. But I don't feel like it's something I need to find
out. His hands were good. I think that's enough."

I nodded at that answer. I didn't ask further.

*Over those few days I thought several times about asking him his
name. I didn't. I can't exactly explain the reason, even now. It
wasn't a feeling that I shouldn't ask — it was a feeling that there
was no need to ask. The two look similar, but they're different.*

*I know he passed through these six records more than once. In a
garden, by a well, in a field, in a valley, by the water. I have no
way, this time either, of confirming whether it was the same person. It
isn't that I had no wish to confirm it. But that day, I felt that
confirming it wasn't work that fell to me, in this record.*

---

The seventh set was completed that way.

I set it on the table alongside the place where the other six copies
had sat, and the place where the seal had once been. The first sheet,
with the recipient box, on top.

That box was still empty.

I looked at that box for a long while that night. I even thought about
writing a name in it. My own name, or this city's name. In the end I
wrote nothing.

*I can't exactly explain that reason either. But leaving that box
empty still felt right, at the time.*

The Keeper looked at that paper and said.

"…All that's left now is where to put it."

"…Yes," I answered. "I'll take care of that."

The Keeper nodded without saying much more. I felt, from his face,
that the answer had already been decided somewhere. I didn't ask.

That night, walking home, I stopped for a moment beside the water
channel. I wondered if I might see him again. No one was there. Only
the sound of the water running.

*Closing this chapter, even knowing that this is the chapter where I
bring him out more than in any other, I have to admit there's still
only this much I can write about him. He folded paper. Exactly, and
for a long time. After folding the last page he said, "…That's it,
then." That's all I know. I invent nothing beyond it.*
