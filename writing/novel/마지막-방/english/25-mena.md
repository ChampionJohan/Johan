# Chapter 25. Mena

> _Previously_ — Histor and I lined up the six cities' dates against the
> tide records. We saw that the nameless man had been in two different
> cities on the same day. Histor couldn't explain it. That was the first
> time I ever saw him have nothing to say.

When the woman came, at first I didn't even realize she was a visitor.

She'd stood outside the repository door for a while before she came in. She didn't have much with her. One bag over her shoulder. Her clothes looked worn, but not dirty — washed so many times the color had faded. Her shoes were the same. Shoes that had walked here.

The Keeper recognized her first.

"…Mena."

I didn't place the name right away, even hearing it. I knew who Mena was. First in the index, the first record this repository had ever received belonged to Mena. Back in Part One, when Histor first laid out the six records, he'd said that name many times. Loop's own record keeper. The one who'd taught Elpis how to write. And later, Xene too.

But the person standing in front of me was just a middle-aged woman. Her walk was a little slow, and one shoulder sat lower than the other. The shoulder of someone who'd walked a long way. For a while I couldn't lay the name in the record over the person in front of me.

"…It's been a while," the Keeper said.

"…Yes. It has." Mena answered. Her voice was low and plain.

The two of them didn't take each other's hands, didn't embrace. They just stood facing each other and looked, for a moment. The greeting of people who'd known each other a long time.

---

Mena asked, the moment she sat down.

"…Is all of it here? What I wrote?"

The question struck me as a little odd. Someone coming to check on what they'd written themselves. But Mena was serious about it.

"…Which do you mean?" I asked.

"…All of it. I heard everything I wrote comes here. But I've never once seen how it piles up. I always sent it off somewhere else once I finished writing."

I asked Arche. Arche called up the list at once. Seventeen bundles classified as Mena's writing. The oldest was Loop's own record; the rest were short letters, notes, and copies of slips she'd given people she'd taught, left behind as she moved from city to city.

Mena heard the number and said nothing for a while. Then, finally:

"…More than I thought."

"…You're the one who wrote it."

"…You don't know, while you're writing. It all just scatters."

*At the time I only heard that as modesty. Now I know. It wasn't
modesty.*

---

I brought a few of Mena's records over from the shelf. She took them and flipped through them out of order. Front page, then back, then front again.

I found that hard to watch. If it were me, I'd have looked at the dates and laid them in order.

"…These are a bit out of order," I said, carefully.

"…Yes. I know. I've always been like this."

"…Always?"

Mena picked up one sheet and laughed. Not a laugh at herself, nor one meant to embarrass me.

"…I can write. I can't lay things down. I've been like that since forever. Even when I was teaching Elpis how to write — I couldn't do what she did with her own notebook. She used to ask me, why is yours all mixed up like this."

"…What did you tell her?"

"…That writing comes first. Looking back now, that was half true and half an excuse."

I started to say how inefficient that was, and stopped. Mena already seemed to know.

---

The Keeper brought out tea. Mena took the cup with both hands.

"…It's very quiet here," Mena said, looking around the room.

"…Yes. It's always like this," I said.

"…I came from a loud city. Not this time — but for the last several years, that's how it's been. Going from city to city, teaching people how to write things down, then gathering the written things back up again."

"…Why gather them back?"

"…Because if they scatter, they're gone."

Mena set her cup down and looked at me.

"…But even gathered, they scatter like this. I only ever learned how far as gathering. I never learned to lay them down."

*Writing this scene again, I want to set down exactly what I felt that
day. I was startled. This woman — the one who'd taught Elpis to keep
records, who'd passed the keeping of records to Xene — was sitting
there, willing to be taught by me.*

---

That was when Histor came out from the next room. He must have heard Mena's name. He asked before he'd even said hello.

"…The Reckoning's case law. Do you know Mena's own record was misquoted there?"

Mena nodded.

"…I heard. Twice. Once in the Reckoning, once in the Harvest. What I wrote — 'this person never explained herself to the end' — got changed to 'this person never repented to the end.'"

"…Aren't you angry?"

"…I was, at first. Now…" Mena chose her words for a moment. "…Now I think the trouble was that I let it scatter. If it had stayed together, in order, in one place — it would have been harder to change."

Histor listened quietly, as though taking it down. He didn't seem to want to refute Mena. He just looked like someone who wanted to confirm the exact fact.

"…Will you set it right?"

"…How? It's already gone that way. I'm just trying to set what's still ahead of me down properly, at least."

---

It was after that Mena asked Arche something. Her voice dropped a little.

"…The very first thing I ever wrote. Did it go through?"

*Please specify the item you mean.*

"…Loop. That record, from back then. The very first one."

*That item is in progress.*

Mena's expression said she'd already expected that. She wasn't surprised.

"…How long has it been?"

*In progress for twenty-six years.*

The room went quiet for a moment. Twenty-six years was ten more than my own age. I couldn't even measure how long that was, but Mena just nodded.

"…I thought so," Mena said. "I never thought it would close. I was just curious. How many years it had been."

"…Aren't you angry? Twenty-six years."

Mena looked at me. She seemed to smile a little.

"…I can't see a reason to be angry. What I wrote is what I wrote, and whether it closes or not is a different matter. I didn't write it in order to finish it, from the start."

*I turned that over for a long time. I was someone who laid things in
order, and order needed an end for me to feel settled. Mena wasn't.
Mena carried twenty-six years with a face that said it didn't matter
whether it ever ended.*

Histor was quietly writing the conversation down beside us. He looked like he'd put this answer in his own list. But he offered no comment at all.

---

Before evening, I took Mena out behind the repository. I wanted to show her the water channel. It wasn't something we showed other visitors much, but for some reason I wanted to show Mena.

Mena stopped walking when she heard the sound of the water.

"…This came all the way here?"

"…Yes. I heard Speon opened it. Or — not opened, exactly. Joined it."

"…Joined." Mena repeated the word. "…That sounds more right."

Mena watched the water for a long while. It ran shallow between the stones, the color of soil. She crouched and dipped her hand in.

"…Cold."

"…Yes."

"…I think I've seen this water somewhere before."

"…Where?"

Mena didn't answer. She just looked at the water a while longer, then stood up.

*I held on to this scene for a long time. Mena said nothing more. I
didn't ask. I was beginning, around then, to learn that some things
stay only if you don't ask.*

---

On the way back into the repository, the Keeper saw us coming. He seemed to have guessed we'd been to the water channel.

"…When she first came here," the Keeper said to me, speaking of Mena in the third person though she was standing right there. "…She wasn't even twenty. A couple years older than the apprentice, maybe, right now."

"…Were you keeping records even then?" I asked.

"…Since then, yes. And even then, she couldn't lay things down. Same as now."

Mena laughed.

"…Twenty-some years, and I hear the same thing."

"…Because it's true," the Keeper said. Not laughing, just stating a fact.

*Listening to that exchange, I felt a strange kind of relief. That a
person doesn't much change even after twenty-some years. It should
have been a disappointing thing to hear. That day, oddly, it sounded
warm instead.*

---

I spent the whole afternoon sitting beside Mena, sorting her records.

What I did wasn't much. Look at the date first; if there wasn't one, guess from the contents; sort by city; then order them again within that. I checked a few with Arche. Arche answered precisely, as it always did.

Mena watched from the side. She didn't ask to be taught. She just watched.

"…Why is this one in this order?" Mena asked.

"…Because the date comes first."

"…This one doesn't have a date."

"…Then we look at what's written inside. It says 'after winter ended,' so it's one season past the date on the sheet before it."

Mena looked at that paper again. Even though she'd written it herself, her face was the face of someone seeing it for the first time.

After a while, she asked:

"…How did you learn this?"

"…I never learned it. I moved a lot. Nine times."

"…Nine times."

"…Yes. I packed every time. It just came naturally, from that."

Mena nodded. Then she was quiet for a while.

"…I never moved. I stayed in one place a long time. And still I never learned this. I thought it would be the opposite."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I just kept my hands moving.

After a while Mena asked again.

"…What did you say you lost, when you moved?"

"…A spoon, a blanket, a wooden doll. And four more I don't even know what they were."

"…There are ones you don't know?"

"…Yes. I know something's missing, but I don't remember what it was."

Mena nodded slowly, as if turning the words over.

"…I was the opposite. I never lost anything. Maybe that's why I never learned how to lay things down. I never had reason to miss anything."

"…Do you envy that?"

"…No. Looking back on it now, I think it's the person who's lost something who becomes the one who keeps things. I just went on writing."

I repeated that to myself twice in my head, so I wouldn't forget it.

---

By evening, all seventeen of Mena's bundles sat in order on the shelf. She looked them over once, from end to end.

"…This feels a little better."

"…What does?"

"…Seeing what I wrote all in one place, in order. I didn't know it would feel like this."

I didn't ask Mena why she'd come now, of all times. It didn't feel like the moment to ask.

Instead, she spoke first.

"…Others will come too."

"…Who?"

"…The ones I taught, the ones I didn't. They're all off doing their own work somewhere, but lately I've been hearing that everyone's headed this way. Maybe word of the water channel got around. Or maybe it's just time."

Mena didn't explain further than that. I didn't ask more, either.

*Writing this part, I tried to render Mena's words exactly. "Maybe it's
just time." Mena left it standing as a question. She didn't attach an
answer to it.*

---

Mena stayed the night in the room behind the repository. Early the next morning she got up and started packing her things again.

"…Are you leaving?" I asked.

"…No. I'll stay a bit longer. There seems to be more to learn here."

"…Learn what?"

Mena pulled a new stack of paper out of her bag. Just as jumbled as before.

"…This. Show me how to lay this down too."

I laughed. Mena laughed along with me.

The Keeper walked by and said, in passing,

"…Mena's become an apprentice."

Mena didn't deny it.

"…That's about right. I spent my whole life teaching. I never once learned to lay things down. Late, but — better now than never."

Hearing that, I felt something strange. I was sixteen, teaching something to a woman past forty. It was strange, but it wasn't unpleasant.

Before she left that time, Mena asked one last thing.

"…Are you going to keep doing this? Laying things down."

"…I think so. I'm not particularly good at anything else."

"…That's enough. My whole life I thought writing was the only thing I was good at. Coming here, I found out that wasn't even true. If what's written doesn't get laid down, in the end it's the same as losing it."

I wrote that down on a piece of paper. Mena saw and said:

"…Writing it down again."

"…Yes. That's how I learned to do things."

"…I'll learn to do it that way too, now."

---

Mena stayed on for a few more days after that. Every morning she brought a new stack of paper, and every evening it sat there in order. It wasn't a big change. Just a stack shrinking a little, day by day.

On the morning she left, Mena turned back at the door.

"…When the next person comes, do this for them. Just like now."

"…Who's coming?"

"…I don't know. But someone will. Scattered, like me."

Mena left it at that, and went. I watched her back for a long time. Her walk was still slow, but her shoulders sat a little straighter than when she'd arrived.

*I didn't know it then. That after Mena, so many people really did
come. And that every one of them carried scattered things with them,
each in their own way. Mena doesn't seem to have known this ahead of
time and told me. She only said, honestly, that she thought it would
be so.*
