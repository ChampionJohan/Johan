# Chapter 1. The Day, Seven Years Ago

Outside, people die.

I knew that from a very young age. In our settlement, one or two people went missing every winter. When an old person went missing, it was sad but not surprising. When a child went missing, it was surprising and sad. But both happened.

The adults never hid it. They couldn't have. The houses stood so close together that everyone heard whoever was crying at night.

*I still don't think that was a bad way to raise a child. I had already learned the word "die" before I was ten. So seven years later, when I first heard the word "preservation," I understood exactly what it promised.*

*I was deceived precisely because I understood it exactly.*

Our settlement had several words for death. Went. Left. Stopped. The adults used them differently depending on the situation. If someone was sick a long time before dying, they said "stopped." If it was sudden, they said "went."

I understood that distinction from a young age. I judged, from which word I heard, whether I should go to that house or not. For a "went," I went that day. For a "stopped," I went a few days later. "Stopped" meant everyone there had already been crying a long time.

*Later, in this city, I would see that none of those words is used. Not went, not left, not stopped. With no use for them, the words disappeared too. I would spend a long time looking closely at what moved in to take the place where the words used to be.*

---

There were four of us at home.

My father went missing when I was five. I won't write that story at length here. My mother raised us alone after that. I was the elder brother, and I had one younger brother.

My brother was eight years younger than me. A late child. He was born when I was eight, so I remember the day he was born. His face was red and wrinkled, and my mother laughed, looking at him.

My brother followed me around closely. So closely it was sometimes a nuisance. Wherever I went, he came along. When I went to draw water, when I went to gather wood, when I played with my friends.

There was a time I got angry at him.

"…Don't follow me."

My brother stopped where he was. And he didn't follow. I felt some relief that day, and some guilt by evening. But I didn't apologize. An eight-years-older brother doesn't do that.

*I have replayed that scene hundreds of times over seven years. Not because I regret not apologizing. It's that I remember, too exactly, the spot where my brother stopped that day, and that exactness frightens me. The path from our house to the well, in front of the third stone wall. I still know that spot exactly.*

My brother was afraid of water.

He was afraid of the sound of the well bucket going down. The rope unspooling makes a long sound, and at the end there's a splash, and the space between them was, for my brother, apparently too long.

I knew this, and I deliberately let the rope out slowly. It was a mean thing to do.

My mother saw it once and slapped my back.

"…Why make something he's afraid of even more frightening?"

"…He'll get used to it soon enough."

"…Is getting used to something the same as not being afraid of it?"

*I let that question pass over me at the time. I thought of it again after coming to this city. The people of this city are afraid of nothing. But that isn't because nothing frightens them — it's because there is no longer anything to be afraid of. My mother's question, unchanged, becomes a question about this city.*

---

Seven years ago, the year I was nine, it happened.

A sickness went around the settlement. First it was a cough. Then it was fever. And then people lay down.

The adults gathered and talked for a long time. I never heard the whole of it. They told the children to go outside.

That was when people came from the city.

*I want to note first that those people were not bad people. That's the part of this record I most want to be careful with. They didn't come to deceive us. They came to truly help, and they truly did help.*

They explained something called preservation. If a sick person was preserved, they said, that person would not get any worse. Not sick anymore, and not gone.

"…Does it cure them?" someone asked.

"…No," the person said. "It doesn't cure. It stops."

I thought that answer was honest. I still think so. That person did not lie.

*Nobody in this city lies. I would confirm that for myself, later. And I would learn that a person can end up like this even without a single lie.*

That night the adults argued for a long time.

One side said they had to send them. The other side said they must not. Someone from the side against it said this:

"…Stopping isn't the same as living."

Someone from the side for it answered.

"…And is dying living?"

I heard that from outside the door. I was nine, so I only half understood what it meant.

*I have carried that half-understood exchange for seven years. And coming to this city, I learn that both sentences are true. Both are true, and they cannot stand together. This book is written in the space between those two sentences.*

---

My mother lay down first, and then my brother lay down.

I did not lie down.

*I still don't know why I was the only one who wasn't infected. I've asked. No one could answer. They just said it happens.*

My mother took to her bed on the third day. My brother, on the sixth.

In between, I drew water. Four times a day. There was nothing else I could do.

My brother lay there and looked at me.

"…Brother."

"…Yes."

"…I hear the water."

I was holding the bucket rope. I had forgotten he was afraid of that sound.

"…Scared?"

My brother shook his head.

"…I like it."

*I didn't understand that answer at the time. I didn't know why a sound he'd always feared had suddenly become something he liked.*

*Now I know. That sound meant I was there.*

The day of departure was set. The people from the city would take the sick with them. Eleven left the settlement. Two left from our house.

I did not go. I wasn't sick.

The morning they left, my mother called me.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes."

"…You stay here."

"…No."

"…Anemos."

My mother said my name twice. Our mother said your name twice when she was angry. But that day, she wasn't angry.

*I can still play that voice back exactly. It wasn't a voice of anger — it was a voice asking something of me. I didn't hear that then. I didn't hear it until I was sixteen.*

"…That's a place for sick people," my mother said. "You're not sick."

"…Then I'll come later."

My mother laughed at that.

"…All right. Come later."

---

I was outside the village that day.

I went out because I didn't want to watch them leave. It was the greatest resistance a nine-year-old could make. I sat behind the hill until the sun went down.

When I came back, everyone was gone.

Inside the house, things had been put in order. My mother must have tidied up despite being sick. The blankets were folded, and a jar of water sat drawn in the kitchen.

I looked at that water jar for a long time.

My mother had drawn the water before she left. It was the water I was to drink that evening.

*I didn't drink that water for three days. If I drank it, it would be gone. On the fourth day it went bad, and I threw it out. And that day, I cried for the first time.*

*Seven years later I would come into a city without water. I don't think that's a coincidence. It probably is a coincidence. It just never felt like one to me.*

---

*I regretted that choice for seven years. I could have looked at their faces one last time, and I didn't. I could have held their hands, and I didn't.*

*But writing this record now, I think differently. Sitting behind that hill that day wasn't resistance. I think I already knew, even then. That once those people left, there would be no going back to before. A nine-year-old doesn't know a thing like that in words. But he knows it in his body.*

---

Seven years passed after that.

I stayed in the settlement. I grew up in a relative's house on my father's side, started working at twelve, and at fourteen learned to dig wells.

Outside, people kept dying. Over those seven years I saw seventeen funerals. I counted them. Counting became a habit.

*I became someone who counts. That's why numbers appear so often in this record. While you're counting, you don't have to think.*

Learning to dig wells at fourteen was an accident. There was an old man in the village who dug wells, and he needed a hand, so I went.

The old man hardly spoke. We dug together all day and he wouldn't say ten words. But there was exactly one thing he said, over and over.

"…You don't find water."

"…Then what do you do?"

"…You make it a way. Water is everywhere already. It just has nowhere to go."

I took that as a technical remark. I thought he meant something about the layers of the earth.

*I would learn, three years later, that it wasn't a technical remark at all. In a city without water, standing beside someone digging a well that nobody had asked him to dig.*

The old man stopped the winter I turned fifteen. Since he'd been sick a long time, they said he'd "stopped."

I went to that funeral too. The ninth of the seventeen.

Word came from the city a few times during those years.

The preservation had succeeded, they said. Nine of the eleven had been preserved. What became of the other two was not written down.

*I would learn later where those two had gone. I'll write that in Part Two.*

My mother and my brother were among the nine.

The notice read like this:

> The subject has been stably preserved, and a visit may be applied for at any time.

I looked at that sentence for a long time.

*At any time. I thought that was a kind thing to say. It really was a kind thing to say. This city truly does let you visit at any time.*

*But the sentence also meant something else. It meant there was no urgency. Since the other side would not change, there was no reason to hurry.*

*I did not hurry for seven years. And during those seven years, I was the only one who grew older.*

Once I took the notice to my uncle.

"…Can I go now, with this?"

"…You can."

"…Then why haven't you gone, all this time?"

My uncle said nothing for a long while. Then he said this.

"…If you go, you have to see it."

I didn't understand what he meant, and I asked again. My uncle didn't answer.

*Seven years later I would give that same answer myself, out of my own mouth. On the day I came out from a visit, turning away from the counter after going to apply for a third visit.*

*If you go, you have to see it. That's all there is to it.*

---

In the spring of the year I turned sixteen, I packed my things.

My uncle asked why now.

I hadn't prepared an answer. I just said this.

"…I think I can go now."

My uncle looked at me for a long while.

"…What are you going to do there?"

"…Bring them back."

My uncle said nothing to that. And he gave me a water flask. Something given to people from outside when they travel far.

"…I hear there's no water there," my uncle said.

I didn't know what that meant. How could a city without water exist.

*I would learn what those words meant on my first day in the city. And I didn't know then, either, that this flask would become the thing I carried to the very end of this story.*

---

On the road, I put my prepared words in order.

I had prepared a great many words over seven years. Things to say to my mother, things to say to my brother. I had even fixed the order. First, greetings. Then, everything that had happened since. Then, the main thing.

The main thing was this.

*Let's go together now.*

I had prepared those five words since I was nine. I prepared them while digging wells, and I prepared them while watching funerals.

The road took four days.

On the third day I passed through another settlement. I asked the people there which way the Preserve was.

An old man pointed with his hand. Then he asked,

"…Who do you have there?"

"…My mother and my brother."

The old man nodded.

"…We have one too."

I asked, glad to hear it.

"…Do you go visit?"

The old man looked at me. I would remember that look many times, later.

"…I went, at first."

"…And now?"

"…I don't go."

I started to ask why, but I couldn't. It felt like something I shouldn't ask.

The old man spoke first.

"…They're exactly the same."

*I heard those words on the road and didn't understand what they meant. I would understand four days later. And I would learn that the old man wasn't a bad person. He didn't stop going because he was tired of it. He stopped because he couldn't bear that he was the only one growing old, every time he went.*

*In the end, I never manage to say those words myself. To be precise, I do say them, but I learn that the words are of no use at all. It takes this whole book to understand why.*

*I'll set down one thing here, ahead of time. I thought I was going to that city to wake my family. I was wrong. I was going back to the time when I was not alone.*

*That those two things are different — I understood that very late.*
