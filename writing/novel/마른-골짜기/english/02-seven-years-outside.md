# Chapter 2. Seven Years Outside

> _Previously_ — Nine-year-old Anemos was alone outside the village the day his mother and brother left for the Preserve. Seven years later, now sixteen, he sets out with a water flask to bring them back. On the road, an old man he meets tells him, "…They're exactly the same." He does not yet know what those words mean.

While I walked the road, I kept walking backward.

My body went forward, but my thoughts kept returning to seven years before. It was a four-day road, and through the third day there was a great deal of time to think. In that time I put those seven years in order.

*I wrote "in order," but really it was a matter of counting. I was already a person who counted, even then.*

---

Between the year I turned nine and the year I turned ten, five people went missing from our settlement. Between ten and eleven, three. The year after that, four. I remember all those numbers.

Counting became a habit around age ten. At first I just counted out of boredom. The number of times the well bucket went down, the number of axe-strokes to split wood, the number of steps left until evening. Then at some point I was counting people.

*I learned later that this was a way of avoiding grief. Numbers don't cry. Names cry.*

My uncle's house had three other children besides me. I was the fourth. Fourth for the rice bowl, fourth for the clothes. My cousins were never unkind to me. They simply never thought of me as a child of their own house. Neither did I.

"…Whose kid are you?" a village child once asked me.

"…I have a mother," I said. "In the Preserve."

The child didn't understand what that meant. Not many people in the village knew much about the Preserve. It hadn't been long since the city people came and took them, and no one yet knew exactly what it meant.

*Only I knew. Only I was weighing the weight of that word.*

My uncle's youngest was two years younger than me. His name was Gaon. Gaon didn't take to me. For the first few months he wouldn't even look at me.

"…Why won't you look at me?" I asked once.

"…You're going to leave, aren't you," Gaon said. "The kids who were at your house before did too. Whoever we got attached to always left."

I couldn't answer that. Gaon was right. I lived in that house planning to leave someday. There were times I deliberately worked at not getting attached.

*I learned very late that the effort had failed. Attachment doesn't fail to form just because you try to keep it from forming. It forms with every shared meal. I ate about two thousand meals in that house. I never counted, but that's what the arithmetic gives now.*

---

At twelve I tried to leave once.

I packed my things and told my uncle. He looked at me for a long while and said this.

"…You'll die if you go alone."

"…I won't."

"…It's four days' walk. Where will you get water?"

I had no answer. I had neither the strength to walk four days nor the sense to ration water. My uncle unpacked my bag again and said,

"…You have to be sixteen at least."

"…Why sixteen?"

"…Because by then you'll be an adult too. It's an adult going to meet adults, not a child going to fetch them."

*I didn't understand that distinction then. Now I do. My uncle wasn't worried I'd fail to get there. He was worried about what I'd see once I arrived.*

From that year I started counting how many years were left until sixteen. Four years. I crossed those four years off one day at a time.

---

A friend of mine died the winter I was thirteen.

I won't write the name here. I was told that writing it makes it cry, so I won't write it here. That child was my age, drew water with me, played with me. It started as a cough and stopped in eight days.

While that child was sick, I kept thinking one thing.

*Couldn't they be sent to the Preserve?*

I said that thought to my uncle. He shook his head.

"…That's not a place you go for this."

"…Why not? My mother and brother went because of sickness."

"…That was because the sickness had gone through the whole settlement, so the city sent people. The city doesn't come because one person is sick."

"…What if we brought them ourselves?"

"…Four days' road, carrying a sick child on your back?"

I had no answer. That child stopped in eight days. I went to the funeral. The third of the seventeen I counted.

*That was the day I first learned the boundary of the word preservation. It didn't come to just anyone. Certain conditions had to be met. Nearly a matter of luck.*

*I would learn later that this boundary was the whole structure of this city. Some come in and some cannot. The standard isn't cruel. It's simply procedure. But I didn't know yet, then, that procedure can be more frightening than cruelty.*

After the funeral, Gaon came and sat beside me. He hadn't even been close to that child, yet he came.

"…Brother," Gaon said. "Your mom and your brother aren't going to die, right?"

"…No."

"…Then that's a good thing."

I meant to agree, but I couldn't. Even knowing it was a good thing, the word wouldn't come out of my mouth. I didn't know why, then.

*Gaon treated me a little differently after that. I think my effort not to get attached broke apart that day. In the moment Gaon sat down beside me.*

---

At fourteen I learned the trade under the old well-digger.

The old man lived alone, a little way off from the settlement. The village people liked him but never got close. People find someone who doesn't talk hard to be around.

I didn't find it hard. Being beside someone who didn't talk was, if anything, comfortable. If he didn't talk, I didn't have to answer.

We dug all day, every day. Some days the water came quickly. Some days we dug all day and nothing came.

"…Guess there's none today," I'd say on the days nothing came, and the old man would shake his head.

"…It's not that there's none. You just haven't met it yet."

"…What's the difference?"

"…If there's none, you can dig all you want and nothing comes. If you just haven't met it, one more spadeful and it comes."

*I took that as a technical remark. Now I know it was the only sermon that old man ever gave me. He hated sermons. And yet that was one.*

The old man's hands were cracked. The knuckles were thick and coarse. I would look at that hand and then look at my own. Still soft.

*It took three years for my hand to crack like that. I measured it in time. The old man never measured it in time. He just dug.*

The old man never once asked me why I was learning to dig wells. I never asked him why he didn't go to the city either. But one day the old man brought it up first.

"…You're going to that city."

I stopped shoveling, startled.

"…How did you know?"

"…You keep looking at that flask. You tend to it even though there's no use for it yet."

*That was the first time I realized I had a habit of tending my flask. It was the one my uncle had given me. I wiped it down with a cloth every night. I didn't know myself why I did it.*

"…What will you do when you get there?" the old man asked.

"…Bring them out."

The old man planted his spade in the ground and looked at me. A long look. Then he went back to digging.

"…All right."

That was all. He asked nothing more. I stayed confused for a long time, unable to tell whether that short answer meant he was against it or for it.

*Now I know. It was neither. The old man already knew what I was going there to do. He already knew what "bring them out" meant. He also knew that telling me anything would be useless. Some things can only be understood by going and seeing for yourself.*

---

The year I turned fifteen, I saw a city person for the first time.

A traveling peddler. He went back and forth between the city and the settlements, selling goods. The city's emblem was stamped on his handcart.

I caught hold of him and asked.

"…Have you been to the Preserve?"

"…Been there. To deliver goods."

"…What are the people like there?"

The peddler thought for a moment, then answered.

"…Quiet."

"…They don't like noise?"

"…No. There's nothing to make noise about."

I found that answer strange. I didn't know what "nothing to make noise about" meant.

*I learned the meaning later. A place with nothing to fight over, nothing urgent, nothing to cry about. That was the real meaning of "quiet."*

"…Do the people there go on visits a lot?" I asked again.

The peddler looked at me for a long while.

"…Everyone goes a lot at first. Then it tapers off."

"…Why?"

The peddler answered while pulling his cart.

"…You'll know once you go."

*I let those words pass too, at the time. A month later I heard something similar from an old man passing through the settlement. I let that pass too. I should have caught it the third time I heard the same words, but I let all three pass.*

---

Four months before I turned sixteen, I received a notice at the settlement hall.

It said nine of the eleven sent to the city seven years before had been stably preserved, and a visit could be applied for at any time. There were no names for the remaining two.

I read that notice three times. The same sentence every time. I think I was hoping the sentence would change. It never did.

*That night, for the first time, I did not count toward sixteen. I did not count the four months either. I just started packing.*

My uncle watched me do it and said nothing. The man who had told me I was still too young to walk four days, that I had to be sixteen — that man did not stop me from leaving four months early.

*I thought that was permission at the time. Now I know. It wasn't permission. It was resignation. My uncle knew I would go eventually, and he knew that pulling four years forward by four months wouldn't change much.*

---

The old man stopped the winter I turned fifteen.

I had stayed by his side for the days before. He said almost nothing to the end. Just once, looking at my hand, he said this.

"…Cracked now."

He'd been looking at my hand. Three years of digging in the dirt had made it that way. I took it as a compliment. In the old man's way, it would have been one.

"…What happens to you now, teacher?" I asked. A foolish question. But the old man didn't call it foolish.

"…I'll stop."

"…If you're sent to the Preserve—"

The old man shook his head. He had little strength left, so it was only the smallest shake.

"…How would this body make four days' road. And I'm—"

He didn't finish. I wondered a long time about the rest of that sentence.

*Later, I fill it in myself. It was going to be, I don't want to go. There's no proof. But watching the people I met in this city, that guess slowly became a certainty.*

The old man's funeral was modest. A few villagers came. Gaon came too. I cried aloud at that grave for the first time. The ninth of the seventeen funerals, and the first time.

*I knew exactly why I was crying that day. It was because I knew that of everything that man had said to me while he was alive, not one word had been empty. I didn't know then whether I would meet another person like that.*

---

The night before I left, I counted those seven years again.

Seventeen funerals. Six years since I learned to count. Two years working under the old man. One meeting with a city person. One day since the notice. Seven years living with Gaon.

And seven years since I had seen my mother and my brother.

I laid those numbers side by side and looked at them. They were all correct numbers, and none of them made sense together. Only the number seven stood out, unusually large.

*That night, for the first time, I grew afraid. Not because I didn't know what I would see when I arrived. I was afraid that I would see exactly what I already knew. I didn't yet know a name for that fear.*

Gaon came into my room that night and sat a long while without speaking. Gaon, now fourteen, had grown as tall as me.

"…Brother," Gaon said after a long while. "You'll really bring them back, if you go?"

"…Yes."

"…So I'll really get to meet your real family?"

I laughed at that.

"…You're family too, now."

Gaon didn't answer. But I saw his eyes go red.

*That night, for the first time, I understood I hadn't been the fourth child in that house. Two thousand meals had made it so. I only understood it the night before I left.*

The next morning, my uncle handed me the flask. I took it and set out on the road.

*And on that road, as I've already written, I met an old man. What he said still lives in me. Exactly the same. I didn't know what it meant, not until the evening of the fourth day.*

The third night, I sat by a fire and thought again about what that old man had said. Our well-digging teacher had said something similar. That it wasn't that the water didn't come, but that you hadn't met it yet.

*That night I laid the two sayings side by side. Not yet met, and already met so completely they are the same. I thought, then, that these two were exact opposites. Now I know. In this city, the two become the same words.*

That night I opened and closed the flask's cap, again and again. It was still half full inside. I thought I could fill it once I reached the city.

*On the evening of the fourth day, I was told, the city would come into view over the hill. I turned my last steps toward that hill.*
