# Chapter 10. My Brother

> _Previously_ — After meeting his mother, Anemos sat in the waiting room and felt, for the first time, a misalignment. She was exactly the same as seven years ago, and that sameness pushed him into an unfamiliar loneliness. Now he goes to meet his brother.

The officer opened the waiting-room door and said,

"…Shall we go now?"

I stood up. The chair I'd been sitting in still seemed to hold some warmth.

*I begrudged that warmth, just for a moment. As long as I sat there, I didn't have to do anything.*

The officer spoke to me as we walked.

"…Your brother likes to draw."

"…Draw?"

"…Yes. He draws every day."

I laughed a little at that. Even as a small child my brother had been good at drawing. With no paper, he'd always drawn with a stick in the dirt.

*Right then I wondered whether the drawings in the dirt and the drawings now would turn out to be the same drawings.*

We took a different corridor from the one that had led to my mother. Shorter, with only one window.

*Walking that corridor, I counted the doors. There were two. My mother's corridor had had three. I wondered about that difference for a moment, then let it go. It wasn't a thing worth wondering about.*

The corridor was quiet. Only my own footsteps sounded.

*I've already written several times how loud that sound seemed to me. It was the same that day too. I was starting, now, to try to get used to that sound.*

Following the officer, I tried to steady myself.

*I told myself, walking: don't freeze up the way I did with my mother. It's fine if I can't say what I prepared — just look well at my brother's face. I held onto that resolve all the way to the end of the corridor.*

At the door the officer said,

"…Go on in. He's waiting."

I took hold of the doorknob. The same cold I'd felt at my mother's door. I found that cold a little less unfamiliar this time.

The door opened.

I remember that moment exactly, even now. Inside the room, at the window, a small child was standing.

Eight years old.

The floor was marked with several lines. They looked drawn with fingers, not sticks. Circles and triangles, and shapes I couldn't make out.

*Right then I thought of what the officer had said earlier. That he drew every day. In this room with no paper, no sticks, my brother had been drawing on the floor with his fingers. Even the method was exactly the same as it had been outside.*

---

Seeing that face, I stopped breathing for a moment.

It was my brother. Exactly, completely, the brother I remembered. His face, his height, the way he stood.

*Even up until I stepped into that room, some part of me had still been imagining otherwise. That he'd have grown a little. That his voice, at least, would have changed. I learned how wrong that imagining had been the moment I opened the door.*

My brother had been small-bodied from birth. My mother had once said it was because he was born late. Whenever I'd seen that small body I used to worry. It seemed like he wasn't growing the way other children did.

*In that room I thought of that worry again. And I understood, at the same time, how pointless it had been. My brother no longer needed to grow bigger. He was no longer a body that would grow.*

I looked around the room once more. One window, two chairs. The same arrangement as my mother's room.

*I wasn't even surprised by that sameness anymore. It seemed this city had built every visiting room the same size, the same shape. Only the people were different; the rooms were all the same.*

I'd written in Chapter 1 that my brother was afraid of water. Stepping into that room, I found myself listening, out of reflex, for the sound of water. There was none. This room had no water either.

My brother saw me. His eyes went wide.

I tried to find some trace of unfamiliarity in that look. Even the briefest flicker.

*There was none. My brother knew me without a moment's hesitation. It was a look like someone seeing a person he had met yesterday.*

---

"…Brother?"

That voice was exactly the same too. An eight-year-old's voice. The very voice I had failed to call to mind, back in Chapter 7.

"…Yeah. It's me."

My brother came toward me. Small steps. I counted them. Six steps. Exactly six steps to cross the room.

*I don't remember how many steps it had taken in my mother's room. I hadn't counted. I hadn't had the presence of mind to count, that day. In front of my brother, I had returned to being someone who could count again.*

My brother stopped in front of me. And looked up at me.

---

He looked up at me for a long time.

Starting from my face, down to my shoulders, and back down again to my feet.

I felt my own body anew, following that gaze. A sixteen-year-old's body. A body two heads taller than my brother's.

My brother said,

"…Brother, you got taller."

---

At that one line, I couldn't say anything at all.

*I've tried, more times than anything else in this record, to write this line over again. Every time, I failed. Whatever explanation I put in front of that line, it fell short. So I'll just write it plainly, like this.*

*Brother, you got taller.*

*That was true. I really had grown. Nine-year-old me had become sixteen. But the mouth that had said it was still, exactly, an eight-year-old's mouth. In that moment I understood that seven years had passed between the two of us carrying completely different weights.*

I stood there holding onto that line for a long time. My brother stood quietly looking up at me while I said nothing.

*I couldn't count how many seconds that silence lasted. That was the one moment that day I failed to count. I've written several times that counting was how I protected myself. That method didn't work in that moment.*

---

My brother took my hand. A small hand.

*I remember the size of that hand. Exactly the same size as seven years ago. My own hand had grown about twice as big in that time.*

"…Brother, why did you come so late?"

I couldn't answer that question. I didn't know how to explain seven years to an eight-year-old.

"…Sorry."

"…It's okay. You're here now."

My brother said that and smiled. A smile with no resentment in it at all.

*I was grateful for that smile. And at the same time, it broke me. If there had been resentment, it might have been easier. Resentment is proof that time has passed. This smile had no time in it.*

I took my brother's hand in mine. And held that small hand still, inside my own.

*I didn't want to let go of that hand. It felt like if I let go, this moment would end.*

---

We sat for a while in two chairs. A room much like the one where I'd met my mother.

"…Brother, want to see this?" my brother said.

He showed me a small stone. I'd seen it before. The stone he'd picked up beside our well back in the settlement.

"…You still have this?"

"…Yeah. I always carry it."

I took the stone in my hand. Exactly the same weight as seven years ago.

*Everything was exactly the same. The stone, the hand, the smile, the height. In that moment I confirmed again how exact the misalignment I'd felt with my mother had been. In front of my brother that feeling grew even sharper. The distance between eight and sixteen was crueler than the distance between nine and sixteen.*

"…Brother, I draw every day." my brother said. "On the floor. I draw this, and that."

"…Isn't it boring?"

My brother tilted his head.

"…What's 'boring'?"

I couldn't answer that question. It wasn't a question an eight-year-old should be asking back.

*Right then I remembered something my mother had said. He doesn't really use the word "bored," that child. I had just let that pass at the time. Now I understood what it meant. My brother didn't know the word "boring" itself. Not that he didn't know it, exactly — he had never experienced the feeling that word pointed to.*

My brother pointed at one of the drawings on the floor.

"…Know what this is?"

I looked closely at the drawing. A circle, with two lines running down from it.

"…Is it a well?"

"…Right. I drew you making a well."

I laughed at that. My brother didn't know I had learned to dig wells. There was no way for him to know.

*Right then a chill ran through me. What my brother had drawn was our old settlement's well. And yet those words sounded as though he'd drawn it knowing what I was doing right now. I still think it was coincidence. Only, that day, the coincidence didn't feel like coincidence.*

"…Brother, remember the sound of the bucket?" my brother asked.

"…Yeah. I remember."

"…I'm not scared anymore."

I couldn't respond to that at all. My brother said it as though it were nothing. As though talking about something that had happened yesterday.

*I understood, then. For my brother, that fear, and the moment he'd overcome it, weren't seven years past — they were simply recent. Time hadn't been compressed. It hadn't flowed at all.*

---

"…Are you a grown-up now, Brother?" my brother asked.

"…Not yet. But I'm your big brother."

"…You're my brother, so you're my brother."

My brother said that, laughing. Strangely, that answer comforted me.

*Eight-year-olds understand that well. That a relationship comes before a number. A brother is a brother because he's a brother. That simplicity held me up a little, in that moment.*

"…Brother, it's the same here every day." my brother said suddenly.

"…What is?"

"…When I wake up in the morning, I'm here, and before I sleep, I'm here. It's always here."

I didn't know what to say to that.

"…You didn't ask if I was bored." I finally managed.

"…You asked that already, Brother."

"…Oh, right."

My brother laughed at that. I tried to laugh along, but it didn't quite work.

*I held onto what my brother had said for a long time after. Always here. There was no resentment in those words, no weariness. Just a voice stating a fact. That voice was the most frightening thing of all.*

My brother pointed at my flask.

"…What's that?"

"…A water flask. My uncle gave it to me."

"…Is it heavy?"

"…No. Not really."

My brother held out his hand, so I passed him the flask. He held it with both hands, like cradling it.

"…Brother, can I have this?"

I paused for a moment at that.

"…I need this for the road here..."

"…Oh, I see."

My brother handed the flask back without a fuss. Not the slightest trace of disappointment.

*That easy acceptance stuck with me strangely. An eight-year-old who felt nothing at all when he couldn't have something he wanted. I had never seen an eight-year-old like that. The children in our settlement cried or threw fits when they couldn't have something. My brother used to be like that too. Not anymore.*

I tried to recall the words I'd prepared. But none of them came to me. The words that had scattered in front of my mother felt, in front of my brother, like they had never even existed.

*Let's go together now. I couldn't say those five words to my brother either. I couldn't. The child sitting in front of me wasn't a nine-year-old brother — he was an eight-year-old brother. I didn't know what I would even need to explain to him.*

I felt for the paper inside my shirt again. I didn't take it out.

*I didn't even think, in that room, about taking that paper out. In my mother's room I had at least considered it, briefly. In front of my brother, that consideration didn't even arise. I fully understood, in that room, that some words simply aren't the kind that can be prepared.*

"…Brother, next time I'll show you another drawing." my brother said.

"…Okay. I'll look forward to it."

*I think back, later, on how easily that answer came out. I'll look forward to it. I said it with no weight at all. I didn't know then how heavy a promise it would become.*

---

I heard again that time was up.

My brother didn't want to let go of my hand.

"…You'll come again, right?"

"…Yes. I'll come again."

"…Soon?"

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…As soon as I can."

My brother, satisfied with that answer, let go of my hand.

*I knew, right then, that "as soon as I can" was a heavy promise to me, and just a parting phrase to my brother. To him, soon or late made no real difference. Time didn't brush past that child.*

My brother stood up and held me once. His arms didn't even reach my waist.

*I gauged the height of that embrace. Seven years ago it had reached my legs. Now it didn't even reach my waist. I knew that calculation was strange. My brother's height hadn't changed at all. It was the calculation that kept changing. What was changing was me.*

---

Leaving through the door, I turned to look at my brother once more.

He stood at the window, waving. An eight-year-old's wave.

*I came down the stairs watching that wave. I tried to count the steps. I couldn't. That was the first time numbers failed to help me.*

Outside, I gripped the flask tightly. I didn't open the cap.

*That afternoon, for the first time since arriving in this city, I felt completely lost. I had set out to find my family, and now, standing in front of that very family, I didn't know where I was supposed to go.*

Only then did I open the flask's cap. I took a mouthful of water. It wasn't that I was thirsty. My hands just needed something to do.

*That night, lying in the lodging house, I thought again of my brother's one line. Brother, you got taller. I repeated it to myself three more times before falling asleep. On the third time, I cried out loud for the first time.*
