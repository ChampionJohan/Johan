# Chapter 20. The Things I Had Prepared

> _Previously_ — Instead of an answer, my mother said, "I'll think
> about it." That night I counted the words I'd prepared over seven
> years. Five of them. I fell asleep without having properly looked
> at even one.

The moment I opened my eyes that morning, I unfolded my fingers
first.

Five. Exactly as I'd folded them the night before. I decided not to
pick any of them at random. I would go in order. The oldest one
first.

*I still remember that morning's resolve clearly. Deciding on an
order gave me, at the time, the feeling that I was in control of
something. I wasn't in control of anything at all, and yet the order,
at least, was mine to decide.*

Counting the five of them gave me a strange feeling. Everything I'd
counted up to then had been out there, outside me. Funerals, steps,
the plaza, laughter. What I was counting this time was inside me. It
was the first time since I'd come to this city that the things I
counted had moved inward.

*Looking back now, that difference is the whole point of this
chapter. Counting what's outside is observation. Counting what's
inside is interrogation. That morning, I began to interrogate
myself.*

---

The oldest one was these five words.

*Let's go home now.*

Earlier in this account I wrote that I'd been preparing this line
since I was nine. That I'd prepared it while digging the well, while
watching a funeral. But taking it out again that morning, I found
that this line had never come with a *why* attached. To the nine-
year-old I'd been, it was simply obvious. You go together because
you're family. It was a sentence that needed no reason.

Now it was different. The moment I said this sentence out loud, my
mother would ask. Why. I didn't already have that answer. A reason
that hadn't been needed at nine had become necessary at sixteen. A
strange thing. The longer time went on, the more certain my love had
become, and yet the words to explain that love had only grown more
scarce.

I said the five words over to myself, several times. Not out loud —
only inside my mouth. Each time I said them, the words grew a little
lighter. Not lighter because they'd had weight and lost it, but
lighter because they'd become useless.

I recalled the place where I'd first held that line. Beside the well
in the settlement, the third stone wall. The same spot where my
brother had once stopped walking. I tried to imagine what expression
the nine-year-old me had worn there. Probably none at all. Back then
there'd been no need for an expression. It had simply been a fact. We
were family, and family goes together.

Now I had to add an explanation on top of that fact. A fact that
needed explaining might, perhaps, no longer be a fact at all. I
stopped myself in the middle of that thought. It seemed to be going
too far.

*That morning I set this first line down completely, for the first
time. Not thrown away. Only, I admitted that it could no longer serve
as an answer. This line belonged to a time before the question. Now
was a time that had the question. That the same words could carry a
different weight depending on the season — that was something I
learned for the first time that morning.*

---

The second and third had already been tried. I'd used them both the
day before.

*"This isn't really living, is it."* My mother had asked back what,
then, our talking together right now was, if not living. I hadn't
been able to answer.

*"I want you to be alive again."* This one had ended more quickly.
My mother had pointed it out on the spot. That's what you want,
that's not a reason.

I looked the two of them over again. Looking them over didn't change
anything. What broke yesterday didn't mend itself today. I spent
about an hour just confirming that fact.

I thought again of the old man I'd met on the road. The one on the
way to the settlement who didn't go to visit his family. "Because
it's always the same." I felt I understood that a little now. My
mother had smiled the same way yesterday and today. Only I kept
bringing different words each time. And the different words kept
breaking on the same spot. It was in that moment I first had the
premonition that I, too, might end up like that old man. That someday
I might run out of words to prepare, and after that, might simply
stop going at all.

*I don't write that hour out in detail here. There's nothing to
write. A stretch of time spent staring again at something already
broken looks, from the outside, like doing something, but is really a
time of doing nothing at all. I learned that fact that day. And even
learning it brought little comfort.*

Leaving the lodging, I opened and closed the flask's cap over and
over. I did it about three times and stopped. Counting, opening and
closing — both were just gestures to get me through the time. The
gestures alone solved nothing.

---

The fourth was a line I hadn't tried yet.

*I missed you.*

This was the one I'd felt most sure of. It wasn't logic. It was
simply a fact. I really had missed my mother for seven years. There
seemed to be no place for a *why* to get a grip on this one. To have
missed her was to have missed her. True no matter what anyone said.

I applied for another visit, to test the line. This time the wait
wasn't long. The attendant led me to the same room I always sat in.
My mother was standing at the door, waiting for me.

"…Here again," my mother said. There was no surprise in it.

"…Yes."

"…What's the matter?"

"…Nothing," I said. "I just missed you."

My mother smiled at that. A soft smile.

"…I'm right here," my mother said. "You're looking at me right now,
too."

For a moment I couldn't answer that at all.

"…So I'm looking at you right now, too," my mother went on. "Isn't
missing someone what you say when you can't see them?"

---

I heard that and froze on the spot.

She was right. Completely right. I was looking at my mother right
now. My mother was looking at me. We were, right now, in the middle
of looking at each other. "I missed you" was a sentence that no
longer held true, in this very moment.

*I watched these five words break during that short visit. The line
I'd been most certain of broke the most easily. The line I'd believed
was fact and not logic turned out, I learned then, to hold true only
under a certain condition. When the condition was gone, the fact was
gone too. I hadn't realized that in this city, that condition was
always met. The sentence that a visit is possible any time — I'd read
it since Part One, and only now did I truly understand what it
meant.*

"…So when can I see you next?" I finally managed to ask.

"…Whenever you file for it."

"…That's it? If I can see you any time, then what more is it that
I'm even asking for?"

My mother didn't answer that question. Instead she looked at my face
for a long time. There was something like worry in her eyes.

"…Anemos, are you all right?"

I couldn't answer that question either. Whether I was all right or
not, I didn't know myself.

My mother reached out and touched the back of my hand again. The
same warmth as last time.

"…Don't prepare too much," my mother said. "Words get heavier the
more you prepare them. I'd rather you came to me light."

I didn't understand what that meant right away. I understood later.
My mother had already guessed what I was doing.

---

I left the visiting room and walked toward the garden. I thought
Eikon might be there. She was. Sitting on her usual bench, looking up
at the sky.

"…That face again," Eikon said, looking at me.

"…What face?"

"…The face of someone with something broken in them."

I sat down beside her. And I told her what had just happened. That
I'd said I missed her, and my mother had asked back whether she
wasn't right there in front of me.

Eikon listened to the whole thing and stayed quiet for a moment.

"…Your mother's right, isn't she," she said.

"…I know. That's what makes me angrier."

"…Angry?"

I was surprised by the word that came out of my own mouth. I hadn't
meant to say angry.

"…No, not angry," I corrected myself. "Just — it's true, and I don't
want to accept it."

Eikon looked at that distinction with something like curiosity.

"…Why don't you want to accept something true?"

"…Because if I accept it, then I'm wrong."

"…What's so bad about being wrong?"

I lost my words at that question. Outside, being wrong was
something shameful. That's how the adults had taught it. But in this
city, apparently even that idea sounded foreign.

"…Have you ever been wrong about something?" I asked.

Eikon thought for a moment.

"…I don't know. I don't think I've had much occasion to be."

"…Why not?"

"…If you don't want anything, there's nothing to be wrong about.
You have to expect something, to be wrong."

That answer left me at another loss for words. I'd been losing my
words in front of this girl's sentences a lot, lately. This time was
no exception.

"…Today," I said, "I confirmed that everything I'd prepared was
wrong."

"…So what do you do now?"

"…I don't know."

Eikon heard that answer and looked toward the flask. She held out
her hand. I opened the cap and passed it over. She took a sip and
handed it back.

"…How does it taste?" I asked. A question that came out of habit.

"…Just like water."

That answer made me smile a little. In a day that had broken
entirely, that one familiar answer, at least, had stayed the same.

"…By the way," Eikon went on, "you said five."

"…Right."

"…You've talked about four. That leaves one."

My body went stiff at that. I'd never told her about a fifth one, and
she'd counted exactly right.

"…That one's not yet," I said.

"…Why not?"

This time I didn't answer. Eikon didn't press either. She just looked
back up at the sky. I was grateful for that silence. In this city,
someone not pressing you could be kindness, and it could be
indifference too. That day, whichever it was, I was grateful for it.

---

Walking back to the lodging, I looked at my last finger.

The fifth line still had no name. I'd never turned it into a
sentence before. That day, for the first time, I tried.

I rewrote it several times. Once it came out like this: *Then I'm
alone.* I revised it. *Without my mother, I become alone.* I revised
it again. *Because I don't want to be alone.*

I started to read each of these sentences out loud, one by one, and
stopped myself. It felt like saying them aloud would make them real.

I thought about telling Eikon first, at least. In front of her,
almost anything was relatively easy to say. But it seemed this one
line, even in front of her, wouldn't come out. The other four had
been words aimed at my mother. This one was aimed at myself. Bringing
out a line aimed at yourself, to someone else, first — that took a
different kind of courage.

I wrote only the first character of that sentence in the corner of a
sheet of paper. *I.* My hand stopped there. I didn't write the rest.
I stared at that one character for a long while, then folded the
paper and put it in the drawer.

*That night I never finished the fifth line. I know now that it
wasn't that I couldn't finish it — it's that I didn't want to. This
one line was the one I couldn't test in front of my mother. If this
one broke, I would have nothing left. The other four, I could at
least pretend were for my mother's sake. This one had been, from the
start, for my own.*

---

That night I opened all five fingers again. Four were already
broken. One I was still holding closed. Holding it, only — I had no
courage to use it.

*Writing this book now, I can finish that fifth sentence. The boy I
was that night could not. This account is, among other things, the
road toward that finishing. I don't want to get too far ahead of
myself, so I'll leave it at this much here. A day comes, eventually,
when this last finger has to open too.*

I looked out the window. The light was as steady as ever. Watching
it, I thought about what I should do the next day. I had no more
words left to bring to my mother. Four of the five I'd prepared had
broken, and one I'd hidden away myself.

That night, for the first time, I thought of my brother. I knew I
would have to ask him the same question I'd asked my mother. I also
remembered what my mother had said in the visiting room, with worry
in her eyes. What you'll do, once you hear his answer.

*I brushed that worry off lightly, at the time. My brother was
eight. I thought, if anything, it would be easier with him. I thought
there'd be no need for complicated logic, no words that could break.
How wrong that thought was, I would learn the next day.*

That night, for the first time, I decided not to prepare any words.
I'd tried five, four had broken and one I'd hidden, and now there
were no more words left to prepare. Strangely, that felt like a
relief. Having nothing left to prepare felt, somehow, like having
nothing left to fail at.

*How wrong that feeling was, I didn't know then. Having no prepared
words didn't mean there was no failure to have. It was closer to the
opposite. Going in unprepared, I would hear, that place, an answer
of a kind I had never written down before.*

I folded my five fingers again. This time with no order, all at
once. And I turned off the light. In the dark I laid that hand on my
chest and lay down. Five fingers, still there, carrying five weights.
