# Chapter 27. Digging Together

> _Previously_ — for six days the city never got angry at Speon. It only
> told him, precisely and politely, that it wasn't needed. Speon never
> argued and kept digging. I carried water to him every day. Lying in my
> room, I looked at my clean palms for a long time.

The night before, I couldn't fall asleep for a long time.

I kept going back over what Speon had said. No need to think. Your body
will decide on its own. I hadn't believed it. I'd always been a person
who thought first. Sitting behind that hill at nine years old, and later
setting the order of every word I meant to say on the way — both had
been the result of thinking. I had never once let my body decide on its
own.

*But that night, strangely, my body won. The moment I opened my eyes in
the morning, my feet turned toward the pit first. Thought followed
after.*

Leaving the dormitory, I thought about my mother and my brother. I could
apply for a visit any time. Any time at all. Since they never changed,
if I didn't go today I could go tomorrow, and if not tomorrow, the day
after. Whether that thought made things easier or harder for me, I
couldn't tell at the time.

The next morning, I picked up a shovel.

I woke the moment the sky lightened. It had been a long time since that
happened. Until then I'd always woken at a fixed hour, around when the
distribution station's bell rang. That day I was already awake before
the bell even sounded.

No one had told me to. There was a spare shovel Speon had left beside the
pit. I'd only looked at it for days. That day my hand simply reached for
it first.

"…Want to try?" Speon asked.

"…Yes."

I gripped the handle. It was a familiar weight in my hand. The wooden
handle had worn smooth. I knew right away that the smoothness wasn't
strange to me at all. My body already knew the angle between palm and
handle, even the direction the weight leaned.

*I hadn't made anything with my hands since coming to this city. I'd
signed my name on forms, opened and closed the flask's cap, knocked on
doors. That was all. I'd been a person who worked with his hands since
fourteen, but in this city there was nothing for those hands to do.*

I gripped the handle with both hands and weighed it. A little shorter
than the shovel I'd used outside. The blade was thinner. It didn't seem
like something made in this city, but something Speon had brought with
him. The handle was chipped in places — marks left by long use.

"…How old is this?" I asked.

"…This shovel? About twelve years."

"…That's old."

"…Good wood lasts. No reason to change it."

I heard that and set the blade against the dirt. Before the first stroke,
I paused a moment to steady my breath. It was such a small motion, and
yet strangely, I felt tense.

---

I drove the shovel into the earth.

I stepped on the upper edge of the blade and put my weight into it. The
feel of the soil splitting traveled up through the sole of my foot. I
pulled the shovel back. A clump of earth broke free.

It was a simple motion. And yet my body remembered it exactly — the
angle, the timing of the force, how to use my back. I felt my body move
before my mind had even finished thinking.

"…You're good at this," Speon said.

"…Did some of this before."

"…The old man taught you?"

"…Yes."

I took a second stroke. A third, a fourth. I was counting in my head.
By the tenth my breath had grown a little short. By the twentieth my
shoulders had started to ache. By around the thirtieth, my breathing
eased again instead. A rhythm was building. Unhurried, but never
stopping — the same rhythm Speon had shown me that first day.

*Sinking into that rhythm, I felt something shift. Since coming to this
city I had been a person who did nothing but wait. Waiting for a visit,
waiting for an answer, waiting for a procedure to end. There was no
waiting in digging. Dig, and earth came up. The result came right away. I
only realized, in that moment, how much I had missed that.*

---

I took a small knife out of my pack and hung it at my waist. It had been
the old man's. He'd given it to me before he died. The handle was wood,
worn glassy smooth from long use. I hadn't taken it out once, in all my
time in this city. There had been no need to. In this city, there was
almost nothing to cut or shape. Everything came already made.

That day I used the knife to smooth a rough patch of wood grain on the
shovel handle. Speon saw and asked.

"…That's a good knife."

"…It was his. The one who died."

"…Take care of it."

"…I will."

About half an hour later, Eikon passed by.

She stopped again this time too. But this time she was watching me, not
Speon. Watching my hands, to be exact — watching me hold the shovel.

"…Why are you doing that?" she asked.

"…Helping."

"…You didn't come here to live, Anemos."

"…No."

"…So why are you doing this city's work? It isn't even needed."

I answered without pausing my digging.

"…It's fun."

Eikon's face went a little strange at that answer. Not angry, not
mocking. A face that truly didn't understand.

"…Are fun and needed different things?"

I stopped digging at that question. It was a good one.

"…I think they're different," I said. "Something needed is something
you'd be in trouble without. Something fun is... something you don't
need, but want to do anyway."

"…Why do something you don't need?"

I couldn't answer that. Eikon didn't wait for an answer and started
walking again. I watched her go and drove my shovel back into the earth.

*I held onto that question for a long time. Why do something you don't
need. It really wasn't a strange question in this city. Here, every
action had to have need attached to it as its reason. Fun wasn't a
reason. That day, for the first time, I felt strange to myself, doing
something for no reason but fun.*

---

Around that time I began to feel that time moved differently inside the
rhythm of digging. Over the past month in this city, I had mostly
experienced time as waiting. I'd submit a visit request and wait days.
Hear the answer and wait more days. Inside that waiting, time crawled.
A day would pass with nothing happening, and vanish without my even
knowing whether it had been there at all.

Something like a bell sounded once from the direction of the Preserve's
building. The sound announcing visiting hours. I heard it, and I didn't
stop digging. It was the first time that had happened. Since coming to
this city, every time that sound rang, my body had turned toward it on
reflex. Not that day.

*I only realized it afterward. That morning, for the first time, I let
that bell pass right through me. I felt like I should have felt guilty. I
strangely didn't. That was the stranger part.*

While I was digging, time went differently. Every time I set the shovel,
I could see with my own eyes how much earth had come up. Dig ten times,
and there was ten strokes' worth of pit. A hundred times, a hundred
strokes' worth. It was time that accumulated into a result. I was
feeling that accumulation for the first time in a long while.

*I couldn't put that difference into exact words that day. I can now.
Waiting time is time that passes over me. Digging time is time I make
myself. Since arriving in this city I had lived entirely inside the
first kind of time. That day, for the first time, I touched the second.*

About an hour in, my palms began to burn.

I had no gloves. Speon took off his own and gave them to me.

"…I'm fine," I said.

"…Blister now, you'll be useless for days. Put them on."

I took the gloves and put them on. They were a little large. Still, the
fit felt familiar. It felt like returning to my fourteen-year-old self,
my fifteen-year-old self.

*I want to describe that feeling exactly. It wasn't that I hadn't used my
body in seven years. I'd kept working the whole time — drawing water,
gathering wood, digging wells. But every bit of that work had been for
survival. Work someone told me to do, work I had to do. This digging was
different. No one had told me to. Nothing would happen if I didn't. I was
simply doing it because I wanted to.*

*Nothing like that had happened once in seven years. So I give this
chapter its title on that account. For the first time in seven years, I
used my body.*

---

At noon, Speon said we should rest.

We leaned against the dirt pile. He shared out some dried food. Hard,
strongly salty. Outside food. Nothing like the taste of this city's
rations.

"…This city's food has no taste," I said.

"…It's not tasteless, it's just always the same," Speon said. "Same
ration every time, same ingredients every time. No reason for it to be
good or bad."

"…And this?"

"…This came out salty by accident. Nobody measured the salt going in."

I laughed at that.

"…A mistake?"

"…Something like that. But it's not bad, is it."

I chewed the salty food and turned that thought over. A mistake, and not
bad. This city had no mistakes. Everything was exact. There was nowhere
for a mistake to happen.

*That was the first time I missed the thing called a mistake. A mistake
was proof that something was alive and moving. After seven years spent
among things that were only ever exact, one salty bite of food felt
strangely welcome.*

Outside had been full of mistakes. The day my mother put salt in the soup
twice and it turned too salty. The time, after my father was gone, when
my mother tried fixing the roof for the first time and made the rain
leak in some new, wrong place. The time I tied the bucket rope wrong and
dropped the bucket to the bottom of the well. Things like that happened
all the time. And every time, someone laughed, or got angry, or fixed it.
That commotion was ordinary life.

This city had none of that commotion. No leaking roof, no dropped
bucket, no oversalted soup. Everything went exactly according to plan. I
only learned, coming to this city, how quiet that kind of perfection
was. A perfect place had nothing to talk about. The words people shared
shrank right along with it.

*That afternoon, looking at the half-eaten salty food, I felt, for the
first time since coming to this city, like telling someone what had
happened that day. I told Speon the story — the time I tied the bucket
rope wrong. He laughed out loud. I remember that laugh being louder than
any sound I'd heard in this city.*

---

That afternoon, I got a blister. The gloves hadn't helped. A small,
clear blister rose on the inside of my palm, just below the thumb.

Speon took my hand and looked it over.

"…That's a lot of work for a first day."

"…It hurts."

"…It's supposed to. That's how a hand gets made new."

"…What do you mean?"

"…Once the blister breaks and a callus grows in, that's a well-digger's
hand. Until it does, it isn't."

I heard that and looked at my hand. It was still a hand that was nothing
much. But that one blister felt like the start of something.

I thought of my brother. He used to sit beside me every time I drew
water and watch my hands. When a blister came up he wanted to touch it,
but I wouldn't let him, because it hurt. He still kept watching from
beside me, all the same.

"…What's wrong with your hand?" he asked me once.

"…It's from working."

"…Will I be like that someday too?"

"…You don't have to be."

"…Why?"

I hadn't answered him then. My brother had been preserved at eight, so
that hand was still, even now, the same smooth hand of an eight-year-old.
Without blisters, without calluses — a hand that had never made a single
thing.

*Thinking that, I stopped my hand. A strange guilt rose in me. While
blisters rose on my hand, my brother's hand stayed exactly as it was. I
was growing, and he wasn't. That fact suddenly weighed on me, all over
again.*

*I had dug a well with this hand at fourteen. But that hand had been
gone a long time already. Over seven years, through other kinds of work,
calluses had risen and worn away, risen and worn away again. Coming to
this city, they'd disappeared altogether. That day, I had started making
that hand again.*

Speon wrapped my hand with a scrap of cloth instead of a proper bandage.
His fingers were clumsy at it. He was a well-digger's hand, not a
bandaging hand, it seemed. The knot came loose twice before it finally
held on the third try.

"…You're no doctor," I said, laughing.

"…Never had much use for doctors. Get hurt, wrap it up, keep working."

"…Doesn't it hurt?"

"…It hurts. But it's better than stopping work. Stop, and you start
thinking too much."

I said nothing to that. Stop, and you start thinking too much. I heard
that as being about me. Since coming to this city, I'd spent most of my
time stopped. And in all that time, I'd thought far too much. Words I'd
prepared, words I hadn't managed to say, words prepared all over again.

*Speon said it as if it were nothing. But it stayed with me from that day
on. Stop, and you start thinking too much. Every time I turned that
sentence over, I realized all over again how long I'd been stopped in
this city.*

---

As the sun leaned toward evening, the station attendant passed by and saw
us. He stopped and watched for a moment.

"…Digging together?" he asked me.

"…Yes."

"…You don't even need it."

I answered without preparing anything, just what came out of my mouth.

"…I'm not doing it because I need it."

The attendant paused a moment at that. His face looked like he'd never
heard those words before.

"…Then why?"

"…I just want to."

He seemed to turn that over for a while, then nodded without saying
anything more and walked on.

*I remember that moment for a long time. It was the first time I'd
confirmed for myself, out loud, that I wasn't doing this because I
needed to. In this city, every action had to have a reason — need,
procedure, regulation. That day, for the first time, I did something
with no reason at all. And I learned how freeing that could be.*

Even after the attendant walked off, I stood there a while longer,
looking at the dirt on my hands. Earth was packed deep under my
fingernails. It didn't look like it would come off easily even washed. I
didn't mind that. If anything, that dirt felt like proof I'd done
something that day.

Speon kept digging the whole time. I glanced at the side of his face for
a moment. Sweat had mixed with dust and left streaks across it. He showed
no sign of tiring. He really did seem like someone who could do this work
all day.

"…Isn't it hard?" I asked.

"…It's hard."

"…But you don't look like it's hard."

"…Hard and unbearable are different things," Speon said, driving the
shovel in. "This is a hard I can bear. There's a hard that can't be
borne too. That's a different story."

I thought about that distinction for a long time. People in this city had
no hard things at all. They were people who didn't even know what hard
meant. Speon knew what hard was, and kept choosing it anyway. I didn't
know, at the time, exactly what that difference meant.

*Writing this record now, I take that thing Speon said out again. A hard
that can be borne, and a hard that can't. I went on, after that, telling
these two apart for the rest of my life. Which kind was the hard of
walking back from visiting my mother? I didn't know then. I would only
learn it much later in this book.*

The pit grew noticeably deeper over that one day. Twice as fast as Speon
working alone. I felt proud of that. It was the first time I'd felt
proud of anything since coming to this city.

---

By evening, when I set down the shovel, my hands were shaking. My arms
ached. My back hurt. I welcomed all of it.

"…Coming back tomorrow?" Speon asked.

"…Yes."

"…What about your family?"

I couldn't answer that right away. It was true, I'd come here to bring my
family back. And yet all that day, I'd barely thought of them at all.

"…I can visit any time," I said. "Since they never change."

Speon heard that and looked at me for a long while. His face looked like
he wanted to say something, but in the end he said nothing.

"…But," he said, starting again. "That they never change — is that a
good thing, or a bad thing?"

I couldn't answer that right away either. I'd always thought it was
good. Not disappearing, not suffering, not being forgotten. But faced
with Speon's question, that answer suddenly wavered.

"…I don't know," I said. It was the answer I'd been giving most, these
days.

"…Not knowing is fine," Speon said, standing and shouldering the shovel.
"Better than pretending to know everything."

"…See you tomorrow, then."

Walking back to the dormitory, I kept looking down at my hand. Blisters,
streaks of dirt, earth packed under my nails. A hand I couldn't have
imagined, just days before.

On the way I passed the distribution station. The door was shut. For the
first time, I walked past without filling my flask. There was still
about half a day's water left in it. That half-full flask seemed to show
how much I'd moved that day. I'd drunk far more than usual, and there was
still water left. Working the body harder should mean more thirst, not
less — I wondered briefly why there was any left, then let the question
go. It wasn't one that needed an answer.

*I slept deeply that night, for the first time in a long while. No
dreams. The next morning when I opened my eyes, the first thing I felt
was my hands aching. Strangely, I liked that ache. It was proof the body
was alive.*

*I started to count that ache and stopped myself. That day I learned,
for the first time, that some things are better left uncounted.*

From that day I went to the pit every morning. The first few days my
body was so worn out I collapsed into sleep each evening. Then my body
began to adjust to the work again. Blisters broke, and thin calluses
settled where they'd been. Every time I touched those calluses, I felt
like I was meeting my fourteen-year-old self again.

*In those twelve days, I never once applied for a visit. I knew it was
strange and didn't stop anyway. I sensed, dimly, that I was avoiding
something. I wouldn't learn what it was until I went down into the
valley.*

On the twelfth day, the pit had grown well past my own height. Speon
built a ladder and set it in place. Climbing up and down it, I found
myself feeling, all at once, that this was no longer just a pit. It was
turning into something else.
