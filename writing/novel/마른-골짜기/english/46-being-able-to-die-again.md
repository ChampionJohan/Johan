# Chapter 46. Being Able to Die Again

> _Previously_ — Eikon asked about release, but did not decide that
> day. Instead she said, "Ask me again later." I promised her I would.
> She left the door neither open nor closed.

Around that time, we found a house.

Two rooms, deep inside the city. Smaller than the room the Preserve
had lent us. But it was ours. The day my mother first crossed its
threshold, she stopped for a moment and rested her hand on the door
frame.

"…A house of our own." she said. "Was it always allowed to be this
small?"

I laughed at that. The house at the settlement had been small too. My
mother seemed to have forgotten.

---

We spent our first ordinary days in that house.

My mother woke each morning and drank water, my brother played in the
yard, and I still went out to help Speon. In the evenings the three of
us sat together and shared bread. I still hadn't gotten used to that
ordinariness.

*I remember those days exactly, even now. Days where nothing happened
at all. I had not had days like that in seven years. I only half knew,
then, how precious that was.*

---

Then my mother began to cough.

At first it was just a dry cough. I thought it might be her throat,
still unused to things. Speon had said the same — that her throat was
just startled, after so long.

But by the third day, the cough hadn't stopped.

---

That night my mother ran a fever.

I only found out when I touched her forehead. It was hot. Facing that
heat, I didn't know for a moment what to do.

*I had seen sick people many times before. At the settlement, across
seventeen funerals. I had thought I knew what to do in front of
someone sick. But that night, for the sole reason that it was my
mother, I knew nothing at all.*

My brother woke.

"…What's wrong with Mom?"

"…She has a fever." I said. "She'll be fine."

Saying it, I wasn't sure I believed it myself.

---

The next morning I carried my mother on my back to the Preserve.

The Preserve had a physician. A place the city's people rarely needed,
since they never fell ill. I opened that door for the first time.

The physician examined my mother. It didn't take long.

"…This is common after release." he said. "The body's starting to use
functions it hasn't used in a long time, and the immune system hasn't
fully found its footing yet."

"…Is it dangerous?" I asked.

"…Not right now." he said. "But we need to watch it. This kind of
thing can turn suddenly."

---

I stood there for a moment, hearing that.

*Can turn suddenly. I knew that phrase. All seventeen funerals had
begun with it. First a cough, then a fever. The year sickness swept
our settlement had begun exactly that way too.*

In that moment, for the first time, it truly hit me. My mother was now
a body that could die again. I had known that sentence many times
over. I'd read it in the paperwork, heard it from the Curator,
repeated it to myself more than once. But until I laid my hand on that
hot forehead, I had not truly known it at all.

---

I brought my mother back home.

I gave her the medicine the physician had given us. I had her drink
water often. That was all there was to do. That was so little, it
frightened me.

"…Brother." my brother asked. "Will Mom get better?"

"…She'll get better." I said.

"…How do you know?"

I had no answer to that question. I didn't know. I truly didn't know.

---

From that day I didn't leave my mother's side.

I told Speon I couldn't come out for a few days. He nodded without
much comment.

"…Go on." he said. "The ditch isn't going anywhere."

I turned away without even managing to say thank you properly.

---

Eikon came by that evening.

She didn't come inside. She just stood leaning against the door frame,
looking into the room.

"…I heard your mother's sick." she said.

"…Yeah."

"…Are you worried?"

I looked at her for a moment at that question. It wasn't a question
she would have asked before.

"…Yeah. A lot."

"…I still don't really know what that's like." Eikon said. "But
looking at your face, Anemos, I don't think it's only a bad thing."

I didn't know how to answer that.

---

"…Why do you think that?" I asked.

"…Because if it were only bad, I don't think you'd keep staying beside
her like that." she said. "Wouldn't you avoid something that was only
bad?"

I couldn't answer that. I had no room, right then, to judge whether
she was right or wrong.

Eikon asked nothing more. She set down a water flask by the door and
left.

"…For your mother." she said. "I don't really know, but I heard people
bring things like this at times like this."

I looked at that flask for a long while. I didn't ask who had told her
that.

---

*Writing this chapter, I thought I should set one thing down honestly.
During the seven years my mother was preserved, I never once needed to
stay by her side. She wasn't sick, she wasn't in danger, she didn't
disappear. The phrase staying by her side didn't even apply. How do
you stay by someone there is nothing to stay for?*

*But now, my mother was sick. And I did not leave her side. I set
those two sentences beside each other and looked at them for a long
time. I've decided not to explain them in this book. I'll just leave
them side by side.*

---

The nursing was simple.

Touching her forehead, fetching water, tucking in the blanket. I
repeated those simple tasks several times a day. At night I sat beside
my mother and listened to her breathing.

I barely washed those days. I didn't change my clothes. My brother
cooked the meals instead. Clumsy, but edible. Eating that food, I felt
for the first time how heavy it was that there were only the three of
us in this house. At the settlement, the neighbors would have brought
over porridge. Here, we didn't have neighbors like that yet.

Once, at night, my mother gave a small moan. I shot upright at the
sound. It turned out she'd just been lying awkwardly. I couldn't lie
back down after that and sat up until morning.

There were nights her breathing wasn't even. On those nights I
couldn't sleep either.

*I was a counter. On those nights I counted her breaths. One, two,
three. When it was uneven, I counted more often. I'd thought, more
than once, that it was a bad habit. But on those nights, there was
nothing else I could do but count.*

---

On the second day, the Curator came.

He looked into the room for a moment from the doorway and didn't come
in.

"…Is there anything you need." he asked.

"…We're all right." I said. "The physician came by."

The Curator nodded. But he didn't leave.

"…To be honest with you." he said. "Every time I see something like
this, something in me goes complicated."

"…In what way?"

"…I kept your mother preserved for seven years. None of this happened
in that time. No sickness, no sleepless nights like this one. That was
the result of my work going well."

I didn't know how to answer that.

"…But now." he went on. "This feels more right, somehow. I still can't
fully explain why."

He left it at that and turned away. I watched his back for a long
while.

On the third night, the fever rose and fell again. I wrung out the wet
cloth fresh, over and over. My hands cracked. I tried not to count
that either.

---

On the fourth night, the fever climbed higher.

My mother spoke in delirium. Things about the settlement, about the
water jar, names I couldn't make out. I sat beside her, holding her
hand. Her hand was hot.

My brother watched from the doorway and cried. Trying hard not to make
a sound.

"…Brother." he said. "I'm scared."

"…Me too." I said. Honestly, for the first time.

---

*I thought about that moment for a long time afterward. I had played
the adult in front of my brother, all this time. I know, it'll be
fine, words like that. That night, those words wouldn't come. I was
scared too. I didn't hide it. Strangely, that seemed to put my brother
more at ease.*

We both slept beside my mother that night. Or more precisely, we
didn't sleep. I counted my mother's breaths until dawn. My brother
fell asleep against my shoulder at some point.

---

By morning the fever had come down a little.

The physician came and examined her again.

"…I think she's past the worst of it." he said. "But we'll need to
watch her a few more days."

I nearly sank to the floor at that. I only realized then how hard it
had been just to stay standing.

---

Speon came by that afternoon.

He brought a water flask and dry bread. He set them down without a
word and turned to go, and I stopped him.

"…Speon."

"…Yeah."

"…Is this how it really is? Becoming a body that's alive again."

Speon looked at me for a moment.

"…Yeah." he said. "This is how it is."

"…I'm scared. I didn't know it would be like this."

"…If you'd known." he asked. "Do you think you wouldn't have done it?"

I couldn't answer that question right away.

---

"…No." I finally said. "I think I'd have done it anyway."

"…Why."

"…I don't know. It's just — having something to watch over gave me
something else, alongside the fear. Something different."

Speon nodded at that.

"…Water's the same." he said. "Once you cut a channel, you also get
the worry of it overflowing. When there's no water, there's no such
worry either. But is that worry a bad thing? I don't think so."

---

*I held on to that for a long time. I'd heard this book's question
asked in different words, more than once by then. Is what's preserved
different from what's alive. I'd tried to find the answer through
argument. But that answer came, that night, not through argument, but
through my hand — laid on my mother's hot forehead.*

*There is nothing to watch over beside a preserved person. There is
something to watch over beside a living person. Having something to
watch over means being afraid. And I know this too — that without that
fear, I would have had no reason to sit this long beside my mother.*

---

On the fifth day, my mother opened her eyes. Her mind was clear.

"…Anemos." she said. Her voice was weak.

"…Yes."

"…How many days was I like that."

"…Four days."

My mother closed her eyes, then opened them again.

"…Were you here the whole time?"

"…Yes."

"…Did you not sleep at all?"

"…I slept. A little."

My mother's face didn't believe that. In truth I had almost no memory
of really sleeping across those four days.

---

"…Why." she asked. "You're busy. You have the ditch to dig."

I laughed at that question. A strange laugh.

"…I didn't know back then." I said. "When you were preserved. There
was nothing to watch over, so I didn't need to. But now there's a
reason to watch over you."

My mother listened quietly to that. Her face understood something.

"…So that's what being able to die again means." she said, almost to
herself.

"…Yes."

"…And." she went on. "You staying by my side like this — that's part
of it too, isn't it."

I had no answer to that. It was true.

---

My brother heard that and ran into the room.

"…Mom!"

"…Hey." my mother smiled. A weak smile, but a real one. "Mom's okay."

My brother took my mother's hand. Didn't let go.

"…I was scared."

"…Me too." my mother said. "But I'm not scared now."

---

That afternoon, Eikon came again. This time she stepped a few paces
inside the door.

"…Are you all right?" she asked my mother directly.

"…I'm all right." my mother said. "Thank you for coming."

Eikon looked a little flustered at that.

"…I didn't really do anything."

"…You brought the water flask." my mother said. "Anemos told me."

Eikon had no answer to that. I saw something unfamiliar cross her
face. The face of someone who wasn't used to being thanked.

"…Was that really that big a thing?" she asked. Her face was genuinely
curious.

"…It was a big thing." my mother said.

Eikon turned that over for a long while, then left without another
word. Watching her walk away, I knew that another question had come to
settle inside her.

---

*I wanted to leave that as the last line of this chapter. But I'll set
down one more thing after it. I only fell asleep that night. Beside my
mother, with my brother next to me. For the first time while falling
asleep, I didn't count. Not because there was nothing to count. That
night simply wasn't heavy, even without counting.*

*I wrote this chapter more carefully than any other in this book. I
didn't want to turn it into a story where being sick is a good thing.
My mother was sick, and that was bad. But alongside that badness, there
was something that hadn't been there before. I set both of those down
here.*

*I stayed by my mother's side for several more days after that. The
fever never rose again. I didn't dig the ditch once in all that time.
Speon never once asked about it. I learned, in my body, that a channel
growing a little slower doesn't mean it will run dry.*
