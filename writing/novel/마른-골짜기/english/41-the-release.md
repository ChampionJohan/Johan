# Chapter 41. The Release

> _Previously_ — My mother consented. "I want to see the two of you
> standing here, side by side." Not an argument. She simply said it.
> The Curator began preparing the procedure.

The procedure was set for three days later.

The Curator said there was no need to rush, but no need to put it off
too long either. Something in the way he said it let me guess how many
times he'd already been through this.

*I didn't count those three days. It was the first time I ever let a
number go.*

---

Through those three days I helped Speon as usual.

If I wasn't working, other thoughts kept crowding in. Digging at the
earth was better than nothing.

"…Nervous." Speon asked.

"…Yeah."

"…What's the most nervous part."

I thought about that question for a long while.

"…That nothing will change." I said. "That after making a decision
this big, once the procedure's over, nothing will actually be
different."

Speon stopped digging at that.

"…No chance of that." he said. "Once water starts coming out, the
ground changes. Even if you can't see it from outside, the inside
changes. People are the same."

---

On the second day, my brother came and asked me,

"…Brother, is Mom going to get old now?"

"…Probably. That's just how people are."

"…So will Mom get older than me?"

"…She always was. She's the grown-up."

My brother thought about that answer for a long while.

"…I'm fine with it." my brother said. "Even if Mom gets old."

I could tell that wasn't a prepared line. It was just a mind an
eight-year-old had made up on his own.

---

On the evening of the third day, Eikon came looking for me.

"…Tomorrow, right." she said.

"…Yeah."

"…How do you feel?"

I tried to answer that honestly.

"…I don't really know. Kind of glad, kind of scared."

"…It's strange to me, that you feel both at once." Eikon said. "I only
ever feel one thing at a time. And not even that, often."

I turned that over for a long while.

---

The day before the procedure, I looked at the papers.

The Curator had shown them to me ahead of time. A single sheet. At the
top, my mother's name and registration number. At the bottom, a line
for a signature.

The content was short. The undersigned consents to the release of the
preservation state, and acknowledges and accepts all resulting physical
consequences thereafter — including illness, aging, and death.

*I read that sentence over many times. The whole of this book was
inside that short sentence.*

---

"…This is all of it?" I asked.

The Curator nodded.

"…That's all. There's no reason for it to be complicated. Perpetuity
doesn't judge a person. It asks no eligibility, no reason. If you want
it, this is enough."

"…Then why doesn't anyone do it?"

"…Because no one wants to." he said. "Not because there's anything
blocking them."

---

I brought the paper to my mother myself.

My brother came too. The air in the room felt different from usual. My
mother saw us come in and straightened where she sat. I felt something
even in that small motion. That this person was getting ready for
something.

My mother took the paper and looked at it for a long while. She didn't
rush.

"…Should I read it to you?" I asked.

"…No. I can read it myself."

My mother really did read it start to finish. I stood beside her
holding my breath.

---

"…Including illness, aging, and death." my mother read that part
aloud.

"…Yes."

"…This includes all of it."

"…Yes."

My mother set the paper down.

"…I understand."

That was all. She didn't ask anything else.

My brother couldn't bear the silence and asked,

"…Mom, are you scared?"

My mother looked at him and smiled.

"…No. But it is strange."

"…What's strange?"

"…That one sheet of paper decides something this big."

I couldn't answer that. I'd been thinking the same thing myself.

---

I lay awake again that night.

This time it wasn't anxiety. I just had two feelings at once — wanting
tomorrow to come quickly, and wanting it to come a little later.

*That night, for the first time, I understood that those two feelings
weren't fighting each other. Wanting it to come quickly and wanting it
to come slowly were, in fact, two faces of the same feeling. Both came
from the same place: this moment mattering too much.*

---

On the day of the procedure, the room was smaller than I'd expected.

One desk, three chairs. There was one window, facing the direction the
channel ran. I didn't think that was an accident.

The Curator set the papers on the desk.

"…Are you ready." he asked.

"…Yes." my mother said.

"…I'll confirm one thing again." he said. "This procedure cannot be
reversed. After release, you may apply for preservation again, but you
cannot return to exactly the state you are in now. Do you understand."

"…I understand."

"…And this procedure proceeds only by your own will. Here, in this
room, you are signing by your own will, not your family's. Do you
confirm this."

My mother looked at my brother and me for a moment.

"…I confirm it." she said. "This is my will."

*I still remember those words exactly. All this time, I'd thought I was
the one leading this. But in that room, my mother said it plainly. This
is my will. In that moment I understood that I was only someone
standing beside her. And I liked that better.*

---

I stood in that room with my brother.

No one came to celebrate. No one from the rest of the city, no one from
our settlement. Just the three of us, the Curator, and one sheet of
paper. That was all.

*I didn't find that strange at the time. Thinking back on it now, it is
strange. That no one was there for something this large.*

*But now I know. This wasn't a thing to celebrate. It wasn't a thing to
grieve, either. It was just one person returning to her own body.*

---

My mother picked up the pen.

Her hand didn't shake. That struck me as strange. That her hand
wouldn't shake, in front of a decision this big.

*I only understood later. For a hand to shake, there has to be fear.
My mother didn't properly know that fear yet. Fear too was an emotion
you learn only once you become a living body again.*

My mother wrote her name in the signature line.

The sound of the pen touching the paper was the loudest sound in that
room. Hearing it, I only then realized I'd been holding my breath.

Once she'd finished writing her name, my mother set the pen down.

"…It's done." she said. It sounded like something said to herself.

---

The Curator took the paper and checked it.

"…Confirmed." he said. "I'll begin the procedure now."

He opened a small panel on the wall. Inside was a single simple switch.

"…Is that all it is?" I asked.

"…Yes." he said. "Perpetuity isn't a complicated system. Preservation
and release are both simple. What's difficult isn't the procedure. It's
the decision."

---

He pressed the switch.

No sound came. No light. I'd expected something dramatic to happen.
Nothing happened.

Strangely, in that moment, I was disappointed. And I was ashamed of
that disappointment. What had I expected? Light bursting out, wind
blowing, some visible proof.

*I set that disappointment down honestly, here. At that moment I still
wanted something dramatic. It was a feeling exactly opposite to what
this book means to say. Perpetuity does not seize a person with any
fanfare. Release does not let a person go with any fanfare either. Both
are quiet.*

My mother closed her eyes for a moment. Then opened them.

"…How do you feel?" the Curator asked.

My mother couldn't answer that right away. She looked down at her own
hands.

---

"…Different." she finally said.

"…How?" I asked.

"…I don't know. It just feels different."

My brother took my mother's hand.

"…It's warm." my brother said. "It's different from before."

My mother looked at her own hand again, hearing that.

"…Was warmth always like this." she said, almost to herself.

Hearing that, I reached out and took my mother's hand too. It really
was warm. I had touched my mother's hand at every visit, all this time.
It had been warm then too. But today's warmth was different. I had no
words to explain the difference. It was just different.

---

The Curator spoke as he put the papers in order.

"…Your registration has now changed. You are no longer a subject of
preservation. You've been converted to a regular resident."

"…What does that mean?" my brother asked.

"…It means Mom's the same as us now." I said.

My brother beamed at that. My mother didn't smile. Her face wasn't
ready to smile yet.

---

We sat in that small room for a while, just as we were.

No one said congratulations. No one cried. I didn't think that was
strange. This wasn't that kind of thing.

*I wanted to write this scene the most quietly of anything in this
book. That day, I truly didn't feel anything large. Joy, fear — those
came later. Inside that room there was just one sheet of paper, one
switch, and three people.*

My brother broke that silence first.

"…Are we going home now?" my brother asked.

I stopped at that question. The word home sounded unfamiliar. We hadn't
had a home, all this time. The house at the settlement had passed into
other hands seven years ago, and in this city I had simply been living
in a room the Preserve had given me.

"…Yeah." I managed to say. "We'll have to find somewhere to live
together now."

Hearing that, my mother smiled a little, for the first time.

"…A home." she said. "That's a word I haven't heard in a while."

---

The Curator watched us as he gathered up the papers. His face carried
the same weary look as always. But something else was mixed into it
too. I couldn't read exactly what it was, at the time.

"…You're not saying congratulations." I said.

"…I don't know that I'm entitled to say something like that." he said.
"I'm the one who builds this procedure. Not the one who goes through
it."

"…Still, you must be glad today."

The Curator was quiet for a moment at that.

"…I am glad." he said quietly. "But it doesn't feel right to show that
too openly, here. This is your day, not mine."

*I held onto that for a long time. This man handled even his own
gladness with care. I only understood later that it came from the same
place as the way he handled his sorrow.*

---

The Curator opened the door for us and said,

"…You may go out now."

We left that room. Standing in the hallway, I looked at my mother. She
looked the same. But something was different.

*I couldn't put that difference into words, at the time. I understood
it later. From that moment, my mother had become someone who could die
again. Nothing had changed on the outside. But that one fact changed
everything.*

---

The three of us walked down the hallway side by side.

The sound of water came from outside the window. My mother turned her
head toward it.

"…I want to hear that sound up close." she said.

That surprised me. It had been a long time since my mother had said
she wanted something. Or maybe — it had been seven years.

"…Let's go." I said. "Let's get close to it."

---

We walked to the edge of the city. Our steps were slower than usual. My
mother didn't seem used to her own body yet. A walk that checked
something with every step, at every footfall.

My brother walked beside her, holding her hand. I walked on the other
side. It was the first time the three of us had walked side by side in
seven years.

*I didn't count those steps. I didn't want to. Some things only get
smaller when you turn them into numbers.*

---

The closer we got to the ditch, the louder the sound of water grew. My
mother stopped walking.

"…So this is it." she said.

Speon happened to be working on the channel there. He saw us and set
down his shovel.

"…I won't say congratulations." he said to my mother. "I'll give you a
mouthful of water instead."

He filled the flask with water and held it out to her.

My mother took the flask. She wrapped both hands around it.

*In that moment I remembered what Speon had said to me a few days
before. I won't say congratulations. Because it's just the beginning. I
only understood then that those words were a principle of his. He must
have said them every time water first came out.*

My mother stayed still for a moment, holding that flask. She didn't
drink. She just seemed to be feeling the weight of it in her hands.

I watched that from beside her. The sound of water kept flowing.

My brother said nothing, and neither did Speon. Everyone seemed to know
that this was a moment that shouldn't be hurried. In that silence, I
thought of the water jar from seven years ago. The jar my mother had
filled and left behind before she went away. I hadn't drunk from it for
three days. Because drinking it meant it would be gone.

The water in my mother's hands now was water that could disappear and
it would still be all right. Because it could be drawn again. I thought
that difference might be the whole of everything that had happened.
