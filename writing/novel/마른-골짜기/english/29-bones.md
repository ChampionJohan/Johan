# Chapter 29. Bones

> _Previously_ — I finally reached the floor of the Dry Valley. Speon
> hadn't come with me. "That's a place you have to go." I took my first
> step toward the scattered things. I hadn't yet gone close enough to
> know what they were.

The floor of the valley was lower than the city, wider, and quiet.

Even the angle the light came in was different from the city's. The sky
showed as a thin strip between the cliffs, and the light falling through
that gap colored the whole valley a pale, milky white. The shadows were
thick and long. I stood there a moment and let my eyes adjust to that
light.

The cliffs rose high on both sides. They blocked off the upward direction
where the city was, so that now only a narrow band of sky was visible. I
followed the grain of the cliff with my eyes. Layers of differently
colored soil were stacked one on another. A red layer, a brown layer, a
gray layer. A cross-section with a long span of time engraved into it
exactly as it had happened.

Near the base of the cliff, things that looked like roots showed through
the soil. Traces of something that had tried to grow and hadn't finished.
I followed those roots with my gaze. I couldn't see where the ends led.

There was a smell in the air. The city had almost no smell at all. In a
place where everything was managed to an exact, regulated quantity, it
seemed smell didn't survive either. The valley had a smell. The smell of
dry earth, of old wood, and another smell besides that I couldn't quite
explain. Not sweet, not rank — just very, very old.

The first thing I saw was a shoe.

Only one. Half buried in the soil, its shape still intact. The leather
was dry enough to crumble at a touch. The laces had rotted through and
snapped. I crouched down in front of it. Inside, the shape of a foot
still faintly remained. No one was wearing it. Only the trace of someone
who had.

I reached out to touch it, then stopped myself.

A few steps further on there was another shoe. This time a pair. Set
side by side. As if someone had taken them off, set them neatly, and
walked away somewhere. That neatness stayed with me a long time. It was
that day I learned, for the first time, that what is arranged carefully
outlasts what is left in disorder.

There was a small shoe too. A child's. I stood longest in front of that
one. The toe was especially worn through. It must have belonged to a
child who ran a great deal.

---

Walking a little further, I came to scraps of cloth.

Things that had once been clothing. All the color had faded until it was
hard to tell the cloth from the soil. In one spot several pieces lay
overlapping. I guessed they must once have been a single garment. The
place where a sleeve had been, the place where a collar had been. It had
settled into the ground in the same shape a person once wore it.

Every time the wind blew, those scraps of cloth stirred lightly. As if
something in them were still alive. And yet they looked as if they would
crumble the instant a hand touched them.

Further on the scraps grew more numerous. In some places several layers
of clothing lay folded over one another. I thought that must not be one
person's trace but several people's, overlapping in a single spot. As
time had piled up in layers, so had the traces.

Some cloth still kept a trace of color. A scrap with a reddish tint, a
thread that still held a bit of blue. I couldn't gauge how long that
color had managed to hold out. Everything else had already returned to
the color of the earth, and only that small bit of color was still
holding on.

---

Walking further, small objects began to appear.

A button. A comb. A broken piece of wood. Some I could tell the original
use of; some I couldn't. I walked among them and counted. Ten, twenty,
thirty. I didn't stop counting. If I didn't count, I couldn't bear it.

In one spot there was a small bundle of paper. It looked as if it would
crumble if I unfolded it, so I didn't. A faint trace of what had been
writing showed through. I couldn't tell whether it had been a letter or
a list.

Around the spot where the paper bundle lay, small shards of glass were
scattered as well. They looked like they had once been a bottle, though
only a few fragments still kept a recognizable shape. I picked one up,
then set it back down. It felt as though the things here ought to stay
exactly here.

On the ground lay a single thin cord. Rotted down almost to a thread. One
end was driven into the earth; the other lay on the soil, connected to
nothing. It looked like a cord that had once been tied to something.

Beside it, small stones were set out in a row. Not arranged by chance.
Someone had deliberately stood them up like that. I followed the row.
Twelve of them. I couldn't tell what they had been counting, or marking.
Only the number twelve was left behind.

Further along there was something that looked like a small toy. It
seemed to have been carved from wood, though almost none of its shape
remained. An animal? A person? I crouched in front of it a moment, then
stood back up.

---

The ground grew darker in color the further I walked.

From red soil to gray-brown soil, and further still, soil that was
nearly white. I remembered what Speon had said about soil colors. Gray,
he'd said, was where water had passed through. But this white was
different even from that. It didn't look like a trace water had passed
through — it looked like a trace of where water had drained out
completely.

Every time I set my foot down, that white soil rose like powder and
settled again. Every breath brought a little of that powder against the
back of my throat. It tasted dry.

Here and there along the valley wall there were things like shallow
hollows. I couldn't tell whether they'd been made by people or had
formed naturally. I looked briefly into one of them. Something was
stacked deep inside, but it was too dark to make out what. I didn't go
in.

There were more traces of small pools scattered across the ground. Some
large, some very small. Regardless of size, the inside of each held the
same cracked pattern. A place where water had been and then vanished
leaves the same trace no matter its size — I learned that that day.

I looked back at my own footprints left in that white soil. A long trail
stretching behind me. It was the only new footprint pressed into this
valley. Every other trace here was old; only mine had just been made.

Every time the wind blew, the edges of that footprint crumbled a little
more. I walked a few more steps and turned to check. The print behind me
was already going soft at the edges. It was then I learned that this
valley didn't hold onto even a new trace for very long.

---

There were a few things whose shape had survived whole.

Something that had been a tree. A tree that must once have been large
stood now hardened gray. Down to the tips of its branches, it was still
exactly there. Only the leaves were gone. I laid a hand against the base
of it. I could feel the grain of the bark just as it was. It was no
different from touching a living tree. Only there was no warmth in it.

That tree wasn't alone. A little further off, several more like it were
standing. It might once have been a forest. I walked among them, stepping
through the lattice pattern their branches' shadows made. Through the
leafless branches, the sky showed in small broken pieces.

One tree's base had split open. Something was inside the crack. I bent
down and looked. A small bone. It looked like a bird's. Where a nest
should have been, there was no nest — only its trace remained.

Beside it was the mark of a small pool. I could tell at once it was a
place water had been. The cracked pattern on its floor spread out like a
spider's web. The trace left by soil shrinking as water dried away. White
powder had gathered in the gaps between the cracks.

I ran my fingertips along that cracked pattern. The depth varied from
place to place. The deepest crack went in about the width of a finger
joint. I tried to gauge how large that pool must once have been. Walking
its edge, it came to about thirty of my steps. Not a small pool.

At the very center of the pool's floor, in the spot where water must
have lingered longest at the very end, a small mark remained. A round,
shallow depression. I recognized it as a footprint and stopped walking.
It was the trace of someone who had stood beside the last of the water,
until the very last moment.

---

Walking further, the valley widened.

The cliffs on either side drew back, and my view opened up. I tried to
take in, all at once, everything scattered across that wide space, but I
couldn't take it all in. I couldn't see an end to it. I had no way to
gauge how long, or how widely, these traces had been accumulating.

The oldest things had already decayed to where they were barely
distinguishable from the soil. Traces holding only the barest hint of
shape, that would collapse at a touch. The most recent things still kept
a form you could recognize as having once belonged to a person.

Watching that difference, I felt that this valley moved by the way time
accumulates. The city was a place where time had stopped. The valley was
a place where time flowed, very slowly, but unmistakably.

Inside the widened valley, spots crowded with traces and spots utterly
empty of any trace alternated one after another. Every time I passed one
of the empty spots, I felt, strangely, a heavier weight settle on me. A
spot with traces told you, at least, that something had been there. An
empty spot told you nothing at all.

At the center of one empty spot, one very small thing lay by itself. A
ring. The metal hadn't decayed; it remained intact. I looked down at that
ring for a long time. Something was engraved on the inside, but soil had
gotten into it and I couldn't make it out. I left that unwiped too, and
moved on.

---

It was around then that things that looked like bones began to appear on
the ground.

At first I wasn't sure they were really bones. The color was close to the
soil's, and their shape looked on the verge of crumbling. But looking
closer, they were unmistakably bones. Some long and slender, some short
and round. In some places they still held a complete shape; in others
they were scattered.

I walked carefully among them. I tried not to step on any. In some spots
they were gathered together in a single place, as if someone had once
lain down right there. I stopped in front of one such spot.

That kind of spot wasn't only one. As I walked I started counting them.
Five, ten, seventeen. Some spots lay close together. I thought they might
have been a family. Some lay far off, alone.

Some were divided up. Places where no complete shape remained — only a
few fragments scattered here and there. I noticed the degree of
scattering was different from place to place. In some spots the
fragments were still gathered close, as if scattered only recently; in
others they'd been scattered so long ago that the fragments had spread
far apart from each other.

In one spot, unusually small ones were gathered together. I knelt down
in front of it. Judging by the size, they belonged to a child. I couldn't
move from in front of that spot for a long time.

*Writing this chapter, I think this is as far as the most accurate
description I'm capable of can go. I don't intend to carry it further
into words. Some scenes become a lie the moment you add explanation to
them.*

I passed that spot and kept walking. At some point, without my noticing,
even the habit of counting my steps had stopped. There is a point counting
cannot carry you past — I learned that for the first time, that day.

Along one wall of the valley there was a row of shallow hollows worn
into the rock. They looked like places where people had once sat, backs
leaned against the stone. I started to count those worn hollows one by
one, then gave it up. I didn't want to count again.

Below the wall, beside those hollows, a thin groove ran a long way. It
looked like a mark scraped long and slow by a fingernail. I didn't follow
that groove. I only swept it with my eyes and passed on.

---

Every time the wind blew, dry earth spun low across the ground.

Inside that spinning, small things rose and settled again together.
Threads, thin fragments of bone, unnameable bits of debris. I watched
them catch a brief glint in the air and then sink back down to the
earth.

As the sun lowered, long shadows fell across that white soil. The
shadow of a tree, my own shadow, and the shadows of the scattered
things. Looking only at the shadows, it seemed as if something were
still standing there.

The lower the light dropped, the deeper the colors grew. Things that had
been plain white, plain gray by day caught the sunset and turned orange,
turned red. Watching that change, I understood that this valley wore two
entirely different faces, by day and by evening. The valley by day
looked like a land of dead things. The valley by evening looked like
something was burning.

In that light, I felt, for the first time, that this valley was
beautiful. The moment that thought came to me, I was startled at myself.
I couldn't tell whether I was allowed to find a place like this
beautiful. But the sunset asked no permission of mine before it kept
deepening into red.

---

I walked further into the valley.

The sound of things crumbling underfoot went on and on. Dry leaves, dry
earth, dry bone. The sounds were all much the same. I tried to tell them
apart, then gave that up too. There was no need to tell them apart. They
were all the same sound.

The further I walked into the valley, the sparser the traces actually
became. I thought that was strange. I'd assumed the farther from the
city, the more would have accumulated — it was the opposite. The mouth
of the valley held the densest crowd of traces, and going inward they
thinned out more and more.

I didn't understand the reason then. Only, it occurred to me that this
sparser space might actually hold the oldest things of all. Whatever had
arrived here first might have gone in deepest, and been worn by wind and
soil the longest, until by now it left almost no trace at all.

There was something on the ground that looked like a dry riverbed. I
could tell from its winding shape that it was a place water had once run.
I followed that dry course. The soil underfoot was a little firmer there
than elsewhere. Earth packed down long ago by a current.

Far off, deeper in the valley, I saw something moving.

It was too far to make out what it was. I stood in place and looked that
way for a long time. The movement was slow and steady. Different from
something merely stirred by the wind.

I stood still and kept watching that direction. The sky was nearly dark
by now. Stars were beginning to come out, one by one. The same stars I'd
seen from the city's edge. That familiarity was, in a small way, a
comfort.

The movement didn't vanish. If anything it grew clearer, little by
little. I started walking that way. The sound of dry things crumbling
underfoot went on. I decided not to count that sound.

The further I walked, the fewer the scattered traces became. In their
place, the ground grew firmer. I didn't yet know what that change meant.
Only, I could feel that I was drawing closer to something different.

*Until that moment I had believed no one else was in this valley besides
me. It didn't take long to learn how wrong that belief was.*
