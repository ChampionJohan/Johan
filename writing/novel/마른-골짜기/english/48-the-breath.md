# Chapter 48. The Breath

> _Previously_ — I went down into the valley and asked him, "…How long
> have you been here?" He only laughed and gave no answer. Speon said
> he had once asked the same question and gotten the same answer. That
> night I fell asleep having learned that some things can be kept
> close without being known.

It was about ten days later that the channel reached the edge of the
city.

I was digging soil with Speon that day, same as always. But something
was different that day. The dampness at the tip of the shovel ran
deeper than it had yesterday.

"…We're almost there." Speon said.

"…Where."

"…The edge of the cliff." he said. "Past that is the valley."

---

I set down my shovel at that and walked to the edge.

The place where the city ended. Beyond it, the ground dropped away. I
thought back to the day, months before, when I'd first stood at this
spot and looked down. I'd known nothing then. Now I knew who was down
there.

The channel had come to a brief stop at that edge. One last mound of
earth still stood in its way.

"…So all we have to do now is break that open?" I asked.

"…Yeah." Speon said. "But we're not breaking it open today."

"…Why not?"

"…To give a little time to get ready." he said. "For the water, and
for people too."

---

That evening I brought Speon something I'd been building up to say
for a long time.

"…Speon." I said. "Will you teach me how to dig wells. Properly."

Speon stopped putting away his shovels and looked at me.

"…Isn't what you've learned so far enough?"

"…I've only ever helped from the side. I want to learn it properly.
Start to finish."

---

He looked at me for a long while. It wasn't a judging look. It was a
weighing one.

"…Why." he asked.

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…I don't know." I finally said. "But I want to keep doing this. When
one city's done, I want to go to another. There might be no water
there either."

Speon smiled a little at that.

"…You said you learned from that old man, at fourteen."

"…Yes."

"…What did he tell you?"

"…That you don't find water. You make it a way."

---

"…I heard something similar myself." Speon said. "From someone else."

"…Who?"

"…That's an old story." he said. "I learned it when I was about your
age too. That person barely spoke either. But once, just once, he told
me this. Water finds its own way even where no one's watching. Whether
a person's looking or not, water just goes to the low ground."

I turned that over for a long while.

"…Where is that person now?"

"…He stopped." Speon said, briefly. "A long time ago."

I didn't ask anything further at that. I thought I understood why he
didn't say the name.

---

"…That's how the craft gets handed down." Speon said. "The one who
teaches disappears someday, but the teaching doesn't. You got it from
that old man, and I got it from someone else. And now I'm giving it to
you. Someday you'll give it to someone too."

"…Will I be able to teach it?"

"…Don't know." he said. "But that's not something to worry about now.
For now, just learn."

---

"…Still don't fully know what that saying means?" he asked.

"…I know a little." I said. "But not all of it."

"…Good." Speon said. "If you knew all of it, I wouldn't have anything
left to teach you."

He handed me a shovel. A little longer than the one I'd been using.

"…Let's do this properly, starting tomorrow." he said. "This one was
mine, a long time back."

I took the shovel in my hands. The handle was worn smooth. The shape
of a hand that had used it for a long time was still pressed into it.

---

*Taking that shovel, I thought back to the moment the old man had once
handed me one of his own. I was fourteen then. I'm sixteen now. I
started to count the years between, and stopped myself. Some things
carry on without being counted.*

Eikon was there. Sitting by the channel, the way she always did each
evening.

"…So Anemos is becoming a well-digger too now?" she asked.

"…Learning to be."

"…Is that a good thing?"

"…I think it is." I said.

Eikon listened to that quietly. She didn't ask anything more. I knew
she was still holding onto that one question of her own. I didn't ask
about it either. I'd promised.

---

The next morning, a handful of the city's people gathered at the edge.

Word must have spread. That the channel would open into the valley
today. Not a large number. Little more than ten. Most of them had the
faces of people who'd simply come to watch.

The Curator was there too. He was holding his papers, as he always
did.

"…Do you record this kind of thing too?" I asked.

"…No." he said. "I only came to watch, today."

I was a little surprised by that. It was the first time I'd ever seen
this man standing somewhere for a reason that wasn't work.

"…Are you all right?" I asked. "Your face looks a little different
today."

The Curator laughed at that. It was the first time I'd seen that too.

"…I've managed preservation in this city for a long time now." he
said. "But today, I feel like I've come to watch over something
different. Not to manage it."

"…What does that feel like?"

"…I can't quite explain it." he said. "But it isn't a bad feeling."

I decided to remember that answer for a long time. I hadn't heard this
man say anything other than sorrow very often.

---

My mother and brother came too.

My brother had made a friend at the place he'd been attending lately,
and had brought him along. The unfamiliar boy looked down at the
channel with wonder.

"…Does that really go all the way down there?" the boy asked.

"…Yeah." my brother answered proudly. "My brother dug it."

I wanted to correct that. I hadn't dug it alone. But I let it stand.
For my brother, that was the true version.

---

Among the crowd there were a few unfamiliar faces too. Not people of
this city — people who had heard the rumor and come from a nearby
settlement. One old man among them came and stood beside me.

"…So it's true, water's coming out here." he said.

"…Yes."

"…I was half doubting it." he said. "I'd heard this city never had a
well of its own."

"…It has one now."

The old man nodded at that, looking down over the edge of the cliff.

"…I thought about going down there myself, once." he said. "I have
someone from my family down there too."

I looked at him, startled.

"…Have you gone down?"

"…No." the old man said. "There was no reason to go. But now, I don't
know. Seeing this water, it feels different, somehow."

I said nothing back to that. I just stood beside the old man for a
moment.

---

Speon stood in front of the last mound of earth.

"…One thing before we open this." he said, to the gathered people.
"Opening this doesn't suddenly change anything. Water is just water.
It only flows downward. The rest is each person's own."

No one answered. Speon didn't seem to mind that silence. He picked up
his shovel.

"…Anemos." he called me over. "Let's do this together."

I stood beside him. We dug the last mound of earth together.

---

After a few strokes of the shovel, the water began to move.

Slow at first. It seeped forward through the gaps in the soil. Then,
at some moment, it suddenly quickened, and went over the edge of the
cliff.

The sound of falling water echoed inside the valley. I heard it from
the edge. Not loud. But it was the first sound to ever break that
valley's long quiet.

No one cheered. No one clapped. The people just listened to that
sound.

---

Eikon came and stood beside me.

"…That sound. I'm hearing it for the first time." she said.

"…The water falling?"

"…Yes. It's strange. I've always heard the sound of water, but this
is a different sound."

"…How's it different?"

Eikon thought about that question for a moment.

"…The channel's sound was just a flowing sound." she said. "This is a
sound of something falling. Downward."

I thought that distinction was exact. I had never split sound apart
that way before.

"…Doesn't it scare you?" I asked.

"…No." she said. "It just sounds big."

We stood there side by side and listened to that sound. She asked
nothing. I asked nothing either.

---

*I set this moment down more quietly than anything else in this book.
I had expected something dramatic. Light bursting, the ground shaking.
Nothing like that happened. Water simply ran down the face of a cliff.
But that quiet felt, to me, larger than any dramatic scene could have.*

My brother took my hand.

"…Brother, look at that."

I looked where he pointed. The water was falling down the cliff face
in a thin thread, glinting silver.

---

That afternoon, I went down into the valley alone.

I wanted to see the place where the water reached the bottom. Even on
the path down the cliff, the soil had already changed. Not dry brown
anymore, but wet brown.

Reaching the bottom, I found the water threading thin among the
scattered remains. Not a large stream yet. Just a thread of a flow.
But it was moving, unmistakably.

I walked along that flow. I passed the place where a single shoe had
lain. The leather had darkened where the water had touched it a
little. I crouched in front of it and looked for a long while. It
hadn't crumbled. It had simply gotten wet.

I passed a pair of shoes lying side by side too. The stream of water
was grazing past them. I paused my steps there for a moment, then
walked on.

I passed the place where a small child's shoe had been. The one worn
thin at the toe in that particular way. The water hadn't reached that
far yet. I stood in front of it for a moment, thinking the water would
reach there someday too. Just not today.

*I set down here that I stood in front of that shoe for a long time. I
never did find out who that shoe had belonged to, that day. I probably
never will. Standing there for a moment in front of it was the only
thing I could do.*

---

He was standing beside that stream of water.

He looked at me as I approached.

"…You came." he said.

"…Yes."

We stood side by side and looked down at the water. It passed a dry
shoe, then flowed on toward an old scrap of cloth. The soil took in
that water little by little, darkening as it did.

"…Do you think these can live again?" I asked. Handing back, word for
word, the question he had once asked me.

He smiled a little at that.

"…I don't know either." he said. "You gave the same answer back
then."

"…Yes."

"…That answer still holds."

---

"…Then why did this water come?" I asked. "If it wasn't to bring
these back to life."

"…It may well have come to bring them to life." he said. "But you
still don't fully know what living means. I won't claim to know it all
either."

"…Can you tell me just one thing? What it means to be alive."

He fell silent for a long while at that question. I didn't expect an
answer this time. Sure enough, he laughed.

"…Same question again." he said. "Just dressed in different clothes."

I laughed at that too. He never did answer directly, to the very end.
By that day, even that refusal to answer no longer felt strange to me.

---

I listened to that and watched the water run for a long while.

*I tried to find an answer in this book. Is what is preserved
different from what is alive. I never did find that answer through
argument. But I know this now. My mother became a person who could
grow sick again, and I stayed by her side. My brother is still eight,
and I will ask him again every year. Eikon left the door half open,
and I've decided not to rush her. This valley is being wetted, for the
first time, right now.*

*I don't tie all of this together into one answer. I can't.*

---

As the sun began to sink, I walked a little further along the stream.

The water sought out the low ground, flowing on. Exactly as Speon had
always said. That you don't find water — you make it a way. I watched
that water already finding its own way, on its own.

At one spot I stopped walking.

There, among the soil the water had touched, I saw a very small green.

---

It was smaller than the width of a finger's joint.

I crouched in front of it. I didn't know what it was a sprout of.
Grass, or a tree, or maybe just something like moss. It wasn't big.
It wasn't showy. Just, among the dry soil, in one small wet patch,
there was a very small green.

I looked at it for a long while. I didn't touch it. I just left it
there.

The wind blew once. That small green shivered slightly, then went
still again. It didn't come loose. I noticed that too.

---

*I mean to close this book with that one small green. I don't want to
write it as some great miracle. I'm not writing an ending where the
valley becomes a forest overnight. This isn't that kind of book.*

*At the start of this book, I mentioned that valley from the book of
Ezekiel. The valley full of bones, the very dry ones. And that
question. Can these bones live. To the very end of this book, I never
answered yes to that question. I never answered no, either. I only
set down that I saw one very small green thing. That was the whole of
what I could do.*

*I set down only this one thing. In a place that had only ever been
dry, one very small thing had come to be. Whether it will grow, or dry
up again, I don't know. But I know that I stood in that place that
day, and watched my own footprint stay pressed into the soil. Before,
a print like that would have crumbled away in the wind before long.
But that day, the soil was wet, and it stayed.*

---

I stood beside that footprint for a while.

He was watching from a little way off. He said nothing. I said
nothing either.

The sound of water spread low through the valley. Listening to it, I
stood there still not knowing how much more of this valley would
grow wet, still not knowing how much larger that small green would
grow.

*But I know now that it's all right not to know that.*

Before the sun went fully down, I climbed the cliff again.

---

Reaching home, I found my mother standing outside the door.

"…You're late." she said. Not a scolding voice.

"…I went to see the water. I went all the way down into the valley."

"…How was it."

I thought for a moment about how to answer that question.

"…There was one small thing there." I said. "It's hard to explain."

My mother didn't press for more at that answer. She just touched my
shoulder once and went inside.

My brother was already asleep. Passing by him, I looked at the wall.
One more line had been added. I didn't count how many there were. I
didn't need to count to know that it was still growing, and that was
enough.

My brother's breathing spread evenly through the room. I sat beside
him for a moment and listened to that sound. I didn't count it. I just
listened.

---

That night, I lay down with the water flask set by the window.

I don't carry that flask everywhere with me anymore. This city has
water running through it now. But I never threw it away. I just keep
it close by.

*I often find myself picturing this flask beside the water jar my
mother left filled, seven years ago, the day before she was preserved. I
didn't drink that water for three days. Because drinking it would
have used it up. The water in this flask, now, is water it's all
right to use up. Because I can go and draw more. I've carried that
difference with me all the way to the last chapter of this book.*

*At the start of this record, I wrote that I had come to wake my
family. And I confessed, somewhere around chapter thirty-nine, that
another meaning was hidden inside those words. That I had come to go
back to a time when I wasn't alone. I've carried both of those hearts
with me to the very end of this book. I haven't set either of them
down. Because both of them were real.*

---

Tomorrow I'll dig the channel again with Speon. Tomorrow I won't ask
Eikon anything. Tomorrow my brother will draw one more line on the
wall. And this valley's small green thing will still be there, even
while I'm gone, standing in wet soil, quiet.

*(The End.)*
