# Chapter 9. Exactly the Same

> _Previously_ — Anemos met his mother for the first time in seven years. She wasn't sick or sad, and she welcomed him gladly. He never got out even half of what he'd prepared to say. Before meeting his brother, he had to sit in the waiting room a moment and rest.

Sitting in the waiting-room chair, I did nothing at all.

Even when I tried to think, thoughts wouldn't quite take hold. I just sat there and breathed. I picked up the flask and set it back down, over and over.

*I couldn't even count that stretch of time. It was the one moment I gave up counting. I couldn't bring myself to.*

---

I thought of the man next door. The one who had come three times in three years. Footsteps that had shrunk from once a month down to three times in three years.

*At the time I guessed that shrinking was laziness. Now I see it differently. It wasn't laziness. Going to see the same exact thing, every time, takes something out of you. What that something was, and where it went, I began to understand for the first time that day.*

I thought again of what that man had said to me earlier. "It was good." I had already written down, at the time, how little goodness I could actually hear in that tone. Now I could understand the tone. It wasn't a lie. It really must have been good. Only, that goodness had stopped in the same place, every single time.

I recalculated the man's numbers. Once a month would have made thirty-six times in three years. But it had been three.

*Thirty-three had gone missing. Right there, I had no way of knowing where those thirty-three had gone.*

After a while I came back to myself a little. And I went back over what my mother and I had said, one thing at a time.

The way her hair was tied. The color of her clothes. The warmth of her hand. The way she smiled.

*I went through them one by one, and then I stopped. It wasn't counting. It was checking. And checking, I finally, for the first time, looked something straight on.*

All of it was the same.

---

I called to mind even the folds of her clothes. The way she'd sat, the shape of her collar where it had been creased. Wanting to see how close that was to the last time I'd seen her, seven years ago, I closed my eyes and laid the two scenes side by side.

My mother on the morning she left, and my mother today.

*I couldn't find a single difference between those two scenes. Even the color of her hair tie was the same. I knew it couldn't be chance, and yet in that moment I wished it were. If it were chance, it could someday be different.*

At first I took that fact only as a good thing.

*She hadn't aged. She hadn't changed. So it was a good thing. I repeated that logic to myself over and over in that waiting room. The logic was sound. But my heart wouldn't follow the logic.*

I tried to understand why my heart wouldn't follow. My mother mustn't be sick. My mother mustn't be sad. My mother mustn't fail to recognize me. And she had been none of those things. She had been exactly what I'd hoped for.

*So why does this feel so strange.*

I thought of the people back in our settlement. When they grew old, wrinkles gathered on their faces, their steps slowed, their hair whitened. I had once found that change sad. I had once imagined how my father's face might have changed, if he were still alive.

*I used to think that change was proof of sorrow. But in that waiting room I was thinking the opposite. That not changing could be proof of sorrow too. That thought was so foreign to me that I erased it several times over.*

---

Trying to find the answer, I went back over what my mother had said.

"…You've grown so much."

"…Your voice has deepened too."

*Those words. My mother had seen exactly how I had changed. And yet she hadn't been surprised by that change. It was there, right then, that I first put my finger on the difference. Seeing a change and being surprised by a change were two different things.*

I thought that day I wished there had been a mirror. I wanted to see my own face. I wanted to check for myself how much I had changed in seven years.

*Later I saw my own face in the mirror at the lodging house. It was unfamiliar. Someone who recognizes an unfamiliar face and shows no reaction to that unfamiliarity at all. It was only then that I fully understood — that was my mother.*

I thought back over the way she had looked at me, in that waiting room. I'd already written that there had been no surprise. More precisely, there had been, inside that gaze, both the recognition that "you've changed" and the acceptance that "I suppose you have," at the same time. There had been no gap at all between the two.

*Anyone from our settlement would have had something come out of that gap. A gasp, a laugh, tears. That gap of being startled, "you've grown so much." My mother had no such gap. Recognition and acceptance were one body.*

*I tried, at the time, to tell myself that wasn't frightening, only different. Writing this record now, I admit that it was frightening.*

---

I opened my hand and looked at my own palm.

There was a callus. A callus from digging wells. Something that hadn't been there seven years ago. I compared that hand to my mother's hand, in memory.

*My mother's hand had wrinkles. Wrinkles that had already been there seven years ago. In that brief span I never got to check whether those wrinkles were the same or had grown. Later I regretted not checking.*

*I know now. Even if I had checked, they would have been exactly the same.*

I folded and unfolded that hand again. I could feel the callus.

*That feel struck me as unusually strange that day. It seemed like I was the only person in this whole city with a callused hand.*

---

In that waiting room, for the first time, I said the word "exactly the same" out loud.

"…Exactly the same."

It was to myself. No one heard it. But saying it out loud made something come clear.

*I had heard that phrase three times on the road to this city. From the peddler, from the old man, from the woman. All of them had only said "you'll see when you get there," and kept that phrase to themselves. It was in that waiting room that I first fully understood what it meant.*

*I don't resent those three people. I still don't. If they had heard this phrase beforehand, the way I had, they probably wouldn't have believed it either. Some words are just letters until you go through them yourself. That day I passed through the moment when those letters stopped being letters.*

I counted back over those three moments. The peddler, the old man, the woman. All three had said it with the same expression.

*I couldn't read that expression at the time. Now I could. It hadn't been a warning. It had been resignation.*

Being exactly the same wasn't strange in itself. That, on its own, was a good thing. The problem was that the sameness left only me out.

I thought of the trees lining this city's streets. The tree outside the waiting-room window. The one whose leaves barely stirred even in the wind. I had guessed, back then, that it was the terrain, not the wind, that caused it. But another thought crossed my mind, right then.

*Maybe those trees don't grow new leaves either. I knew that thought was a groundless leap, and I couldn't shake it anyway. In this city, the boundary between what changed and what didn't was too wide. People, trees, lamps — all of it crowded onto one side.*

*Only I had changed. My mother had stayed the same. The distance between us was exactly the same now as it had been seven years ago. But I had lived through all seven of those years, while my mother had stood outside them.*

---

At that moment I made a strange calculation.

*What if I came back seven years from now. If I saw my mother again at twenty-three. She would be exactly the same then too. I would go through something like this again. Only more apart.*

*Not seven years, but ten, twenty. I started that calculation and gave it up. I didn't stop because it frightened me. I stopped because there was no need to calculate. The result was already in.*

*That distance would never close. While I grew old, my mother would stay exactly where she was.*

I turned that calculation into numbers. Seven years, then fourteen, then twenty-one. A picture where the gap widened with every multiple.

*The picture was simple. But my heart couldn't accept that simplicity.*

I pushed the calculation further. When I turned thirty. When I reached my uncle's age. When I was old someday and leaning on a cane. Even then I would come to this city, to this room, and face the exact same face of my mother that I faced now.

*Picturing that gave me a strange feeling. It wasn't a frightening picture. But it wasn't a comfortable one either. I tried several words to name that feeling. It wasn't sorrow. Not exactly fear either. The closest word was misalignment. The sense that time itself was misaligned between my mother and me.*

I laid that misalignment over something our well-master had once said. That you don't find water, you make it a way. Remembering that, I thought — in this city there was no need to make a way. Nothing needed to flow. My mother, the trees, the lamps — all of it was already standing still. Still water needs no way.

---

Thinking on that, another thought crossed my mind.

*Is this really preservation, or is it something else.*

I erased that question on the spot, right away. I thought of the notice I'd read in Chapter 5. The preserved converse, remember, judge. They do not disappear.

*That was true. I don't doubt that fact. I still don't. My mother really had recognized me as me, really had remembered Gaon, really had brought up my father. All of that had been real.*

*But that realness and what I had wanted were wearing different shapes. That was the first day I felt that difference. I couldn't name it yet.*

I went back over that notice from the information office, one line at a time. Does not judge. Asks for no qualification. Can be visited any time. Requires nothing new.

*Requires nothing new. That day, for the first time, I truly chewed over that sentence. My mother really did require nothing new. The things I had brought her — that I had grown, that I had lived through seven years, the words I had meant to say — none of it filled any lack in her. Because she had no lack left to fill.*

*It was then that I first thought this: if the things I had brought weren't needed by her, then what had I even come all this way for.*

*I erased that question right away, on the spot. It was a frightening question. It would only be much later in this book that I would face that question head-on again.*

Instead I fled into a different question. What would my brother be like. Would he be exactly the same as my mother too. I already knew the answer. My mother had said, "He hasn't changed one bit." And still, I didn't want to believe that answer until I checked it for myself.

*Not wanting to believe is an odd phrase. I didn't want to believe a good thing. Not aging should have been a good thing. And yet, in that moment, I was hoping it wasn't. So that this feeling I had wouldn't be a strange one after all.*

Sitting in that waiting-room chair, I opened the flask and took one more mouthful of water. My throat kept going dry. It was a thirst that felt less like a dry body than a dry heart.

*That was the first time I felt this thirst. Since arriving in this city there had never been a shortage of water. And yet in that waiting room my throat burned strangely. I didn't know why, that day. Now I can guess. It was the body, telling me — in place of the mind — that something was being lost. Not the body, but the heart.*

The officer came back again and said,

"…Shall we go see your brother now?"

I came sharply back to myself at those words.

"…Yes."

Rising, I set aside, for now, the unfamiliar feeling I'd just had. This was time to meet my brother. I decided to take that feeling out again later.

*I was used to setting feelings aside like that. It was a method much like counting. If I set something aside, I didn't have to think of it, at least for that moment.*

*But this time it didn't quite work. Even walking the corridor toward my brother, I couldn't shake that unfamiliar feeling entirely.*

---

Walking the corridor, I looked out the window.

The sky was clear. That I was the only one for whom time flowed under that sky felt strangely frightening.

*I've written several times now that I hadn't yet named this feeling. I can name it now. It was loneliness. The kind of loneliness you can feel surrounded by people, loved, even after meeting your family.*

*That was the day I first knew that loneliness. And I would go on carrying it with me to the end of this book.*

Walking, I touched the flask. I didn't open the cap. I just ran my fingertips over its surface.

*This flask was, even then, the one thing in this city that belonged to me alone. That fact felt unusually large to me that day. I was the only one whose body was changing, the only one who went thirsty, the only one who had to keep filling something. Holding that flask, I felt like the only living person in this city. I knew it was an illusion. I couldn't shake that illusion that day even so.*

---

The officer walked ahead of me. Following behind her, I took one deep breath.

*I thought, at the time, that this breath was to prepare for meeting my brother. Looking back now, it wasn't preparation. I simply didn't want to feel that misalignment I'd just discovered all over again, in front of my brother.*

*That wish wouldn't be granted. In a little while, I would come to face that misalignment again, in a far sharper shape. In a face that was still exactly eight years old, in a single sentence spoken up at me.*
