# Chapter 31. Can These Bones Live

> _Previously_ — I met him in the valley. When I asked how long he'd been
> here, all he said was, "I was here first." Not angry, not laughing
> either. I spent that night beside him. I fell asleep watching a back
> that never trembled.

When I opened my eyes in the morning, he was already up.

He stood not far off, looking deeper into the valley. I watched his back
a moment, then pulled myself up. My back was stiff from a night lying on
bare ground.

"…Sleep all right?" he asked, without turning around.

"…Yes."

"…Not too cold?"

"…A little."

He nodded at that. It wasn't a worried gesture. Just a confirming one.

The morning light lit the valley differently. Not the red of the evening
before, not the dark of night. A white, cold light. Under it, the
scattered things showed more clearly than before.

*I remember that morning light for a long time afterward. It was a light
that hid nothing. Evening light made everything beautiful. Morning light
simply showed things as they were.*

---

We walked together.

Much the same walk as the day before. He went ahead, and I followed a
few steps behind. He stopped now and then to look at something. I
stopped with him each time.

As we walked I asked him a few more things. Mostly he answered short. To
some questions he gave no answer at all, and simply kept walking. I was
coming to understand, more and more, that silence too was a kind of
answer.

"…How many people are in this valley?" I asked. "Traces like this one."

"…That's not a thing you can count."

"…Why not?"

"…The traces overlap. You can't tell where one ends and another begins,
or whether it's the same person."

Hearing that, I thought of the spots I'd seen in Chapter 29 — where I'd
counted up to five, ten, seventeen, and then stopped. I'd already learned
there was a point past which counting couldn't go. This man seemed to
have known that from the start.

"…Aren't you curious?" I asked. "Who all these people were, who came
here."

"…I am."

"…Then why don't you look into it?"

"…Searching and walking are different things."

I kept walking without understanding that answer.

We walked a long while, and came to a wide, open space. Wider than the
one I'd seen the day before. A place where bones had gathered in
unusual numbers. In some spots they still held a whole shape; in others
they were scattered.

He stopped walking there. He stayed stopped a long time.

I stood beside him and looked at that landscape with him. I didn't speak.
It felt like a place where speaking wasn't allowed.

*I still can't describe that silence exactly, even now. It wasn't a sad
silence, and it wasn't a frightening one. It was just a very large
silence. Standing in front of that size, I felt very small.*

---

He lowered himself and sat on the earth. I sat down beside him.

The wind blew once. Dust rose low and settled again. Inside that dust,
small things rose together and came down again. Threads. Thin fragments
of bone.

He watched that scene for a long time. I watched along with him.

After a long while, he spoke.

"…Anemos."

It was the first time he'd said my name. It landed on me, strangely,
with weight.

"…Yes."

He didn't go on right away. He took up a handful of soil, then let it
run out through his fingers. The dry powder fell slowly.

"…Do you think," he said, "these can live again?"

---

I understood the question. Every single word in it was plain. And yet it
took time to take in what it meant.

These. I looked at the scattered things in front of us. Bones, rotted
scraps of cloth, crumbled shoes. Things that had once been a person.

Live again. That phrase rang strangely inside me. Living again, from a
state opposite to being alive. I felt there was something more inside
that question. A feeling that this wasn't only about this valley.

"…I don't know," I said.

It didn't take me long to say the words. But saying those three, I felt
strangely ashamed. As if I should have offered something more
convincing.

"…What do you mean, you don't know?" he asked. It wasn't a pressing
tone.

"…I really don't know," I said. "If I say yes it feels like a lie, and
if I say no it feels like a lie too."

---

He said nothing to that answer. Only he glanced at me for a moment.
There was something in that look, and at first I took it for
disappointment.

*I would learn later it wasn't disappointment. I couldn't tell the
difference then.*

"…Why not say no?" he asked. "That's just bone. Look at it. It's dry."

At that I looked again at the scattered things. Truly dry. They looked
very far from anything living.

"…Even so, I can't bring myself to say no," I said.

"…Why not?"

I tried to find an answer to that question. I looked for it and didn't
find it.

"…I don't know. I just can't."

---

He traced something in the dirt with a fingertip. A meaningless line.
Then he wiped it away.

"…Then why can't you say yes?" he asked again.

I couldn't answer that one either, right away.

"…I've never seen anything like this," I said. "Outside, when a person
died, they were just dead. I've never seen anyone live again."

"…What about the city, then? There are people there who live again,
aren't there. Release, I think it's called."

I was startled by that. It was strange that he even knew the word
release.

"…That seems like something different from living again," I said. "It's
turning a preserved person back — not bringing a dead person back."

"…Why is that different?"

I stopped a long while at that question. The preserved really do not
disappear. They exist in a state that has not died. But the things in
this valley had disappeared. Only traces remained. I'd thought that
difference was very large, and yet when I tried to explain it, the words
wouldn't come.

"…I don't know," I said again. "I thought it was different, but now I'm
not sure."

---

*Writing this passage, I meet myself again as I was that day. I kept
saying, over and over, that I didn't know. I was ashamed. Since coming
into the city I had always been the one learning something, never the
one giving the answer. But in front of this man, even what I'd learned
was no use. None of it could answer that question.*

He asked again. This time the same question in slightly different
words.

"…What I mean is — do you think that becomes a person again," he said,
pointing his hand at the scattered things. "Breathing, walking. So that
if you called its name, it would turn to look."

I stopped again in front of that specific picture. Breathing, walking, so
that calling a name makes it turn. I laid that picture over my mother
and my brother. My mother didn't breathe. More precisely, she had no
need to breathe. My brother was the same. And yet both of them turned to
look, if you called their name.

The things in this valley didn't turn, however you called. I knew that
difference. And still no answer came.

"…I don't know," I said, a third time.

---

For the first time he looked at me a little longer.

I didn't look away. There was no reason to. I truly didn't know, and I
didn't want to hide that.

"…That's all right," he said.

That surprised me.

"…What is?"

"…Not knowing."

It took me time to understand that.

"…Shouldn't I give the right answer?" I asked.

"…What's the right answer?"

I couldn't answer that question either. Yes didn't feel like the right
answer, and no didn't feel like it either. Neither one was it, but then I
didn't know what was.

"…Saying I don't know," he said, "is sometimes the right answer."

---

"…Does that really count as an answer?" I asked.

"…It does."

"…How? You haven't said anything at all."

He picked up a small stone from the ground. Held it a moment in his
hand, then set it back down.

"…Not knowing isn't the same as saying nothing," he said. "It's not
lying that you know."

I turned that over for a long time.

"…I wanted to say yes," I said, honestly. "It felt like I should. I want
those people to be alive again."

"…Then why didn't you say yes?"

"…Because it would have been a lie. I don't actually believe that's
going to happen."

*In that moment I felt I'd been honest with myself for the first time.
What you wish for and what you know are different things. I wished. But
I didn't know. That day was the first time I'd said those two without
mixing them together.*

In that moment I thought of that night seven years before. The adults
arguing behind the door. One side had said, "Stopping isn't the same as
living." The other had asked back, "And is dying living, then?" At nine
years old I'd only half understood those two sentences.

Standing in front of this valley, I felt for the first time why both of
them were right. Stopping and living are different — that's true. The
city had shown me that. But dying isn't living either. These scattered
things had shown me that. Both sentences were true, and neither one was
the answer I wanted.

"…I've been carrying those two sentences around for seven years," I
said. I told him briefly about that night.

He only listened, all the way through. Even after hearing it all, he
didn't say which side was right.

"…Both are right," he said. "That's why they don't stand together."

"…They can't stand together?"

"…No. But that's not the problem. The problem is that you have to keep
living between the two of them."

I turned that over for a long time.

---

"…You could have said no," I said. "That would've been easier. It's
just bone, after all."

"…Then why didn't you?"

"…That felt like a lie too."

As I gave that answer, I tried to find the reason I felt that way. I
found it.

"…I've seen water running toward this valley," I said. "It hasn't
reached here yet. Speon is digging the channel. Even before the water
arrives, I already felt like something here was alive. I don't know why
that is. But I don't want to call that feeling a lie either."

Hearing that, his eyes widened slightly, for the first time. The
closest thing to a surprised expression I'd seen on him.

"…That," he said. "That's what not knowing is."

---

I didn't fully understand what he meant. But I could feel it was
something important.

"…Will these bones live again, or won't they?" I finally asked again.
This time it was my turn to put the question to him.

He shook his head.

"…I don't know either."

That surprised me.

"…You've been here longer than I have."

"…Is being here long the same as knowing?"

I nearly laughed at that question. I didn't laugh. It wasn't a laugh
that belonged in that place.

"…I just walk," he said. "Beside these. I don't pretend to know."

*I felt, then, that I understood a little of why this man was here. I
still can't explain it. Only, he wasn't someone who held the answer to
this valley. He was someone who kept carrying the question of this
valley, on and on.*

---

We sat in that spot a long while longer.

The wind blew a few more times. Dust rose and settled. I watched that
repeat. I didn't count it.

"…Are you disappointed?" I asked. "That I couldn't give an answer."

"…No."

"…Why not?"

"…Not many people have given the answer, I don't know."

That surprised me a little.

"…What did other people say?"

"…Yes, or no."

"…Which one more?"

"…No, more. Then yes."

That order struck me, strangely, as sad.

"…And I don't know?"

He looked at me a moment.

"…You're the first."

---

I couldn't answer at all, hearing that.

*I keep this sentence among the ones I hold onto longest in this whole
book. You're the first. I still don't know, even now, whether that was
praise or just a fact. Only, from that day on, I came to know that saying
you don't know something isn't a shameful thing. An honest not-knowing
is better than a hastily built knowing.*

He stood up. He started walking again.

I sat there a little longer, then stood and followed.

"…Do you ask other people this question too?" I asked.

"…Probably."

"…Why did you ask me?"

He answered without stopping his walk.

"…Because you brought water with you."

I didn't know what that meant. Whether he meant the flask, or something
else. He explained no further.

*I turned that over for a long time. I still haven't fully worked it
out, even now. Only, later in this book, I would come to a place that
brought that line back to me again.*

We walked together the rest of that afternoon. We didn't return to that
question again. Both of us seemed to know there was no need to.

As we walked, I asked myself that question again, silently, more than
once. Do you think these can live again. The answer still didn't come.
But the not-coming was less uncomfortable now than it had been at first.
I couldn't explain why, that day.

*I can explain a little now. Even not-knowing becomes bearable once it
finds its place. That afternoon, for the first time, I was able to walk
inside a state of not-knowing — without rushing to find an answer.*

When the sun rose high, he stopped walking and looked at me.

"…Going back to the city?" he asked.

I couldn't answer right away. Wanting to stay here longer and knowing I
had to go up were both in me at once.

"…Yes," I said finally. "I think I should go."

"…All right."

He didn't try to hold me back. He didn't try to stop me either.

"…Can I come down again?" I asked.

"…If you want to."

I nodded at that answer. Then I turned and started toward the slope I'd
come down. A few steps on, I looked back. He was already walking again.
Among the bones, without a tremor.
