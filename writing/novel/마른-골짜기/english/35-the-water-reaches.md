# Chapter 35. The Water Reaches

> _Previously_ — The Curator spoke his own sorrow for the first time. He
> said he kept doing his work knowing exactly what it was. "If I don't,
> someone else will. At least I can do it without hiding anything." I
> carried those words back to the pit with me.

That afternoon, the sound from the pit changed.

I felt the difference from far off. It wasn't the sound of a shovel. It
was some kind of splashing.

I hurried my steps. The weight that had sat on me ever since I'd left the
Curator's room lifted, all at once, at that single sound. I had no time,
then, to think about where that lightness had come from.

---

When I reached the pit, Speon was standing inside it. Water had risen to
his ankles. He saw me and grinned.

"…It came."

I didn't understand that right away.

"…What did?"

"…The water."

I looked down into the pit. Water was rising from the bottom. Not clear
— closer to muddy. But it was unmistakably water. It moved, it caught
the light, it flowed.

*I remember that moment exactly. I'd seen water many times since coming
to this city. The distribution station's water, the water in my flask.
All of it water that had been held somewhere. The water I saw that day
was different. It was water rising up out of the ground. Not water
someone had set there — water that simply came, from there.*

---

"…How long has it been?" I asked.

"…About an hour."

"…Why didn't you call me?"

"…There wasn't time to," Speon said, laughing. "First it just got
damp, and then all at once it surged up."

I crouched down there and looked at the water. I reached out to touch
it and stopped.

"…Can I touch it?"

"…You should be the first."

I dipped my hand into the water. It was cold. Grains of soil slipped
between my fingers. I let myself feel that touch for a long time.

Outside, I'd never once thought of this touch as anything special.
Water had been everywhere. In every well, along every riverbank. It was
only here, in this city, that I felt as if I were relearning this
sensation from the beginning. That it was cold. That it flowed. That it
slipped between my fingers. Things that had once been simply given were,
here, new and astonishing, one by one.

*I felt something strange in that moment. This water came from the
thirty-seventh well — thirty-second, out of the thirty-seven he'd dug. I
could count that number. But the water itself couldn't be counted. It
just flowed. It was the first thing to arrive in this city that couldn't
be caught by a number.*

---

Water kept rising the whole afternoon. Speon shaped the walls of the pit
and steered where it would run. I scooped soil out beside him.

Toward evening, people began to gather, one by one.

The distribution station attendant came first. He looked into the pit
and his eyes went wide.

"…It really came."

"…Yes," Speon said.

"…Amazing."

The attendant said only that and stood there a long while. He didn't
drink it. He just watched.

Next came the older woman — the same one who'd once asked Speon what a
well even was.

"…Well," she said. "It really was coming, after all."

"…Yes," Speon said. "I told you."

"…I believed it, but seeing it for myself is different."

She didn't drink either. She stood at the edge of the pit and simply
looked down at the water for a long while.

---

The official in gray came too. The same one who had asked about the
permit on the first day. He still had his collar's insignia on. He
stood in front of the pit and looked at the water for a long time.

"…Congratulations," he said to Speon. It was a businesslike tone, though
not as stiff as before.

"…Thank you," Speon said.

"…This will need to go into the record now. As a successful excavation
case."

"…You're welcome to."

The official took out his notebook and wrote something down. Watching
him, I remembered him writing "Not required" on the first day. I was
curious what he was writing now, but I didn't ask.

"…Is this water fit to drink?" he asked.

"…It'll take a few days to clear," Speon said.

The official nodded and closed his notebook. Then, like everyone else,
he left without drinking it.

The children came too. The same ones who'd once been caught reaching for
a shovel. This time no one stopped them.

"…Can we drink this?" one child asked.

"…Still a little cloudy," Speon said. "Once it settles you can drink
it."

The children looked disappointed at that, then quickly turned to
watching the grains of soil swirl on the water's surface. Stirring it
with a finger, watching it settle.

*Watching those children, I saw for the first time, in this city, a face
purely enjoying itself. Not enjoyment born of need — just enjoyment born
of wonder. I was glad to see that face.*

Eikon came too.

I recognized her coming from far off. Her walk was as unhurried as ever.
She stood in front of the pit and looked down at the water for a long
while. Her expression barely changed.

"…It really came," she said. The same words the attendant had used, but
with a different temperature.

"…Yeah," I said.

"…It's interesting, I guess," she said. "But I don't understand why it's
drawing this many people."

"…You don't find it interesting?"

"…I do. But interesting and liking are different things."

I laughed at that. It had the same grain as what she'd said before —
that curious and lacking were different things.

"…Want to try some?" I asked.

Eikon shook her head.

"…I don't need it."

"…What about just drinking it out of curiosity?"

"…Curiosity is satisfied by looking," she said. "Drinking is a
different matter."

That distinction was exactly like her. I didn't press her further. She
stood a little longer, then left quietly, like the others. Once, on her
way, she looked back. There was something in her eyes, but I couldn't
read what it was, that day.

*I would recall that look again, later. In Chapter 44, I learn its
name.*

---

By the time the sun had gone fully down, nearly ten people had gathered.

That was a large number by this city's standard. I was surprised,
counting it. No one had called anyone, and yet this many had come.

I noticed the way they stood too. Not crowded close together. Spaced at
a regular distance, each looking down into the pit on their own. No one
pushing, no one trying to get ahead. That spacing was exactly this
city's people. Even in front of something astonishing, they didn't
hurry.

They all wore a similar expression. A face that didn't need this, but
had come to see it because it was interesting. I felt a strange comfort
among those faces. This city's people weren't entirely numb. There had
just been very little, for a very long time, to move them.

No one drank. I didn't find that strange. It was to be expected. This
city's people weren't thirsty. The distribution station's water was
enough. This new water wasn't need — it was something else.

It was only then I realized that none of these people knew what it felt
like to be thirsty. The sensation of thirst itself had vanished from this
city long ago. I thought that maybe they hadn't come here because they
were thirsty, but because some faint memory in their bodies still
recalled, dimly, what thirst had once been.

---

*I thought a long time that night about what that something else was. I
can say it a little now. It was proof. Proof that in a place everyone had
called unnecessary, something could really come out of the ground. There
was no need to drink it, but there was a reason to want to see it.*

People began drifting off, one by one. At night everyone went in at
their set time. That exactness felt, that day, a little regrettable to
me.

The last one left was the distribution station attendant. He crouched
at the pit's edge and looked at the water for a long time.

"…What happens to it now?" he asked.

"…Let it settle," Speon said, "and then we'll have to cut a channel
for it."

"…To where?"

"…Haven't decided that yet."

The attendant nodded. Then he reached out his hand.

---

He held his hand out and paused for a moment. I didn't know what that
pause meant. It looked like hesitation, or maybe just a moment spent
making up his mind.

He dipped only the tips of his fingers into the water.

A very brief motion. Not meant to drink. Just a gesture to confirm, with
his fingertips, that the water was really there.

A drop gathered at his fingertip and fell.

The ripple spread to the edge of the pit and faded. I followed that
small ripple with my eyes for a long time. That was the only motion left
on the water's surface.

He looked at his hand a moment. His wet fingers. Then, without a word,
he drew his hand back.

*I remember that gesture exactly, even now. He said nothing. He didn't
drink. He just dipped his hand in and took it out again. I think that
motion is the biggest thing that happened in this city that day. No one
would call it that, but I do.*

*He hadn't dipped his hand in because he needed to. He was a man who
received water from the distribution station. He lacked nothing. And
yet he dipped his hand in. There was no reason for that gesture. Or
rather — there was a reason, but it was the kind of reason "need" can't
explain.*

---

"…It's cold," he said. I couldn't tell whether he was speaking to
Speon or to himself.

"…Yes," Speon said. "Water from underground is always cold."

The attendant stood up. He wiped his wet fingers on his clothes. Then he
looked at me a moment.

"…Anemos, have you tried this water?"

"…Not yet. I only touched it."

"…Why not drink it? You're the one who wanted it."

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…It doesn't feel like the time to drink it, yet," I said.

The attendant tilted his head at that. His face said he didn't
understand. But he didn't press further, said goodbye, and left.

---

After the sun had set fully, one more person came, last of all.

I recognized the silhouette from far off. The Curator. He had no folder
with him. Empty-handed, unlike his usual self.

"…I heard the news," he said, standing in front of the pit.

"…Already?" I asked.

"…Word travels fast in this city. It just never swells."

He looked into the pit for a long time. The water dimly reflected light
in the dark.

"…How do you feel?" I asked.

The Curator's face took on a moment of choosing his words.

"…It's strange," he said. "I've told people today, more than once, that
this city has no need for water. It's the truth. But seeing this now, I
feel something else."

"…What?"

"…I can't explain it," he said. "It's just — good."

That sentence felt heavier to me than anything I'd heard from anyone
else all day. This was a man who knew the weight of every procedure he
carried out. A man like that had just said, without explanation, that it
was simply good.

He didn't dip his hand in. He just stood there a long while, looking
down. Then he said goodbye and turned to walk away.

*I watched his back for a long time. I thought of what he'd told me that
afternoon. If I don't, someone else will. At least I can do it without
hiding anything. That night I thought a long time about how those words
and this — standing here doing nothing but watching — could live inside
one person. Some people were already wet, without ever dipping their
hand in.*

---

Only Speon and I remained. The water in the pit was still, little by
little, rising.

"…Why didn't you drink it?" Speon asked.

"…I don't know," I said. "It just doesn't feel like it's time yet."

"…Not a bad answer."

I looked at the stars reflected in the water. Even in that still-cloudy
water, a few stars shimmered on the surface.

*That night, I kept coming back to the attendant's gesture. He hadn't
drunk it. He'd just dipped his hand in. I think this whole book is inside
that small motion. Even a person who says he needs nothing still reaches
out his hand. Without explaining why. Of the nearly ten people who came
to the water that day, in the end only that one had reached out. Counting
that number, this time, I didn't find it lacking at all.*

The sound of water ran all through the night. Small, steady. I walked
back to the dormitory listening to it. For the first time in days, I
walked without counting anything.

On the way I passed the Curator's building. One light was on. His room.
I looked up at that light for a moment. It meant he was still working, at
this hour. Even on the day the water had come, he had gone back to his
own place.

*Watching that light, I thought of my mother. I wanted to tell her about
this. That the water had come, that people had dipped their hands in.
But in that moment I knew — she would not come, hearing this news, to
dip her hand in. She had no need. Something in me ached at that thought.
The two people closest to me in this city were, that day, the two people
with the same reason for not being there.*

Back at the dormitory, I opened my flask. Half of the distribution
station's water was still inside. I drank it. It tasted the same as
always. And yet that day even that water's taste felt different.
Water that had been held, and water that had come running up out of the
ground — the same water, and yet they reached me with a different
weight.

*Last, that night, I had this thought. This water hadn't wet anyone's
throat yet. And yet it had already done something to this city. Nearly
ten people had gathered for no reason at all, and one person had dipped
in a hand. These were things need couldn't explain. I liked those things
that couldn't be explained.*
