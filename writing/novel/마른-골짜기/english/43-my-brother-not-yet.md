# Chapter 43. My Brother, Not Yet

> _Previously_ — The morning after my mother was released, we went out
> to the yard and looked at the water channel. My mother took a deep
> breath. I watched her back.

The three of us spent those two days in the room the Preserve had lent
us.

We hadn't even started looking for a house yet. My mother's body still
felt strange to her, and moving any distance was hard. I spent that
time helping Speon with the ditch, and my brother played beside it.

*I think those two days were the most ordinary two days in this whole
book. Nothing big happened. And it was exactly that ordinariness that
felt strange. I couldn't even remember the last time the three of us
had spent two days like that, with nothing happening at all.*

---

Two days later, my brother asked.

"…Can I do it now too?"

I had been waiting for that question. And now that I heard it, I
didn't know how to answer.

"…Do what." I asked back. A question meant to buy time.

"…Release."

---

The three of us were sitting in the yard. My mother spoke first.

"…Why do you want to?"

"…I want to be like you and Mom."

"…Be like us how?"

My brother thought about that for a moment.

"…Sick." my brother said. "You said it yourself. Being sick is still
being alive."

---

I remembered that I had said that myself, a few days ago. I hadn't
expected my own words to come back to me like this.

"…Is being sick a good thing?" I asked.

"…I don't know. But when Mom was sick, I want to be sick too."

*Hearing that, I felt my breath stop. My brother hadn't meant it badly.
It was only a way of saying he wanted to be with us.*

I remembered how my brother used to be afraid of the sound of water
when he was small. The rope of the well-bucket unspooling, and the
long silence in between. I remembered once deliberately unspooling it
slower, just to scare him more. My mother had seen it and hit me on
the back. Why would you make something scarier when it already scares
him.

*I bring up that memory here for a reason. That day, I didn't want to
make the same mistake in front of my brother. I didn't want to make
something frightening more frightening. But at the same time, I didn't
want to rush something he wasn't ready for either. I swung a long time
between those two.*

---

"…But how is that different from you saying, if you do it, then I
will?" I asked.

My brother's eyes went wide at that.

"…How's it different?"

"…Before, when I asked you, you said if I do it, then you would too.
Isn't this the same thing now? Don't you want it just because your
brother and Mom did it?"

My brother thought about that for a long while.

---

"…No." my brother said. "Back then it was because I thought you'd like
it. Now I want it myself."

I couldn't tell whether that distinction was real. I couldn't tell
whether an eight-year-old could really make that distinction at all.

*In that moment I thought back to Part Two. When my brother said if I
do it, then I will too, I had judged that this was not consent but
obedience, and withdrew the application myself. I still believe that
judgment was right. And now, today, I had to make the same judgment
again.*

There was one difference from that time. Back then I had judged it
alone. Now my mother was beside me. I thought that was a good thing. I
didn't want to make this decision by myself.

My mother looked over at me. Her face was thinking the same thing I
was. We confirmed it between us without a word.

---

My mother sat my brother on her knee.

My brother shifted a little in her lap.

"…So Mom says no too?" my brother asked. His voice had hurt in it.

"…It's not no." my mother said. "It's not yet."

"…What's the difference between not yet and no?"

My brother asked that with a serious face. I saw something in that
face that didn't belong to an eight-year-old. And at the same time, I
saw a stubbornness in it that belonged entirely to one.

My mother was quiet for a moment at the question.

"…Not yet changes, when enough time passes." she said. "No doesn't
change."

---

"…I think I understand why you want this." she said. "But this is a
different kind of thing."

"…How's it different?"

"…This can't be undone." my mother said. "You're eight years old right
now. But after release, you'll grow up again. Nine, ten, and on. You
don't know yet what it means to grow older."

---

"…Does my brother know?" he asked.

"…Your brother already grew up that way." my mother said. "You
haven't, yet."

My brother turned that over.

"…So is getting older a bad thing?"

"…No." my mother said. "It's not bad. It's just something you can't
know until you've lived through it."

---

I listened to that conversation beside them and thought of the
Curator's words.

*The consent requirement. Release requires the consent of the
preserved person themselves. I had heard the word consent many times
by then. But it was in front of my brother that I first felt, all the
way through, how heavy that word was. To consent means you have to
know what it is you're consenting to.*

---

"…It's not." I said carefully, "that I'm saying no because you're
eight. It's just that I'm not sure yet whether you really understand
what you want, and really want it."

"…I understood it." my brother said.

"…Understood what?"

"…That I can get sick."

"…That you can die, too?"

My brother had no answer to that at all.

---

*I set that silence down here honestly. I took the silence as proof
that my brother didn't understand it yet. And at the same time, I knew
that even adults don't fully understand death either. I had watched
seventeen funerals and I still don't know.*

After a long while, my brother spoke again.

"…What happens when you die?"

I didn't know how to answer that question. My mother seemed the same.

"…I don't know." I said, honestly. "Nobody knows for sure. Even
grown-ups don't."

"…You don't know either?"

"…No. I don't know either."

That answer seemed to put my brother a little at ease, oddly.

*And still, I had to make a decision right there. Complete
understanding couldn't be the standard. But some minimum was needed.
My brother didn't seem to have reached even that minimum yet.*

---

"…Brother." my brother said. "I still want to."

"…I know." I said. "But not now."

"…Why."

"…I want to wait until you can be asked again, later, and really,
truly want it for yourself."

My brother's face went dark.

---

"…I don't like waiting."

"…I know. I didn't like it either."

"…Then why are you making me wait?"

I couldn't answer that right away. My mother answered instead.

"…Because you're precious." she said. "You don't rush what's
precious."

---

My brother's face still wasn't fully convinced by that.

But he didn't push any further either. He just sat quietly and looked
at the channel.

*I don't think that silence was consent. It was just an eight-year-
old's kind of acceptance. And I needed to be clear, that day, that
acceptance and consent are not the same thing.*

---

I sat down beside my brother.

"…Instead." I said. "I'll ask you again every year. Whenever you want
it."

"…Every year?"

"…Yes. Every birthday."

My brother brightened a little at that.

"…So you'll ask me again on my next birthday?"

"…Yes."

"…Promise?"

"…Promise."

My brother held out his little finger. I hooked my finger through his.
A promise from when we were small. I noticed, all over again, how
small that finger was.

"…Mom too." my brother said.

My mother hooked her finger in as well. The three of our fingers
tangled together at once.

*I know that pinky promise is a completely different kind of promise
from signing a document. It had no legal force at all. But I don't
think it was lighter than the document. I think, if anything, it was
the opposite.*

---

*I wrote this scene more carefully than any other in this book. I felt
the temptation, more than once, to resolve this cleanly — to have my
brother suddenly say something wise beyond his years and convince me,
or to have me convince him with some great argument. I didn't write
either of those scenes. They didn't happen in fact, either.*

*This book doesn't resolve everything. My brother has not been
released. I believe that was the right decision. And at the same time,
I know that belief could be wrong. I close this chapter holding both
of those at once.*

---

In the afternoon I saw Eikon. I told her about it.

"…My brother said he wanted to be released. I told him no."

Eikon tilted her head at that.

"…Why? Your brother and your mother got to."

"…He's eight. I'm not sure he really understands and really wants it."

"…I don't understand that." she said. "If you want something, don't
you just want it? Why does understanding have to come into it?"

---

I couldn't answer that right away either.

"…Some things are dangerous to want without understanding." I managed.
"Especially things you can't undo."

"…There's plenty I don't understand either." Eikon said. "But I've
never really wanted much of anything. So I don't really understand
that danger you're talking about."

*Something went cold in me hearing that. Eikon had nothing to want, so
there was no danger for her either. My brother had something to want,
and that was exactly what made it dangerous. I confirmed again, right
then, that this difference split their two worlds completely apart.*

---

"…What about you, Anemos." Eikon asked. "Did you understand and want
it? Coming here."

I stopped for a moment at that.

"…No." I said. "I didn't come here with full understanding either. But
I'm an adult. So I carry the responsibility for my own choice, myself."

"…And your brother can't carry it?"

"…Not yet."

Eikon listened to that answer quietly and went on her way.

---

That evening I told the Curator about it.

"…You did well." he said. "I've seen the opposite far more often — a
parent deciding for a child instead of the child."

"…I'm not his parent."

"…And yet you're standing in that place right now."

I had no answer to that.

---

"…Could I." I asked. "Could I be wrong about this?"

The Curator thought for a moment.

"…You could." he said. "But I also know it was the best decision you
could make right now. The best isn't always right. But it's better
than what isn't the best."

"…And if I regret it, years from now?"

"…Then you decide again, when that time comes." he said. "If there's
one good thing about this line of work, it's that a decision you
couldn't make today, you can make again tomorrow. Unlike death."

I held on to that for a long time.

---

I ran into Speon on the way back.

"…Your face is heavy." he said.

"…My brother said he wanted to be released. I told him no."

Speon set down his shovel and looked at me.

"…Good."

"…How can you say that so fast?"

"…I had something similar happen once, a long time ago." he said.
"There was a kid who wanted to learn to dig wells way too early. I
told him no. He was hurt by it."

"…What happened after that?"

"…He came back a few years later. By then he was really ready."

---

"…What happened to that kid?"

"…He became a well-digger." Speon said. "Like you."

I laughed a little at that.

"…So all I have to do is wait, then."

"…I told you before, waiting isn't the same as doing nothing." he
said. "You're waiting right now. That's work too."

---

That night I lay awake and listened to my brother's breathing for a
long while after he fell asleep.

His breath was even and slow. I tried not to count it. My body counted
anyway. One, two, three.

My mother was listening beside him too.

"…When will the right time be?" I asked.

"…I don't know." my mother said. "But when it comes, I think we'll
both know."

---

I decided to believe that.

---

The next morning, my brother drew something on the wall of the room.

One small line. About the width of a finger's joint.

"…What's that." I asked.

"…A mark." my brother said. "I'm going to count how many days until my
next birthday."

I looked at that line for a long while.

*I had always thought I was the only one who counted. That day, for
the first time, I found out my brother had started counting too. Only
what he counted wasn't something that had already passed — it was
something still coming. I thought that difference mattered. I counted
to hold on to what had slipped away. My brother counted to wait for
what hadn't arrived yet.*

"…Do you want me to help?" I asked.

"…No." my brother said. "I'm counting this one by myself."

I said nothing back to that. It felt like the right answer.

From then on, my brother drew one more line every morning. Every time
I passed that wall, I counted the lines with my eyes. Once I'd
counted, I never said the number out loud. That number belonged to my
brother.

That year, his birthday was still many months away. I didn't know how
many lines that wall would end up holding. But I knew that each one of
those lines was a way for us to wait.

*Writing this chapter, I thought more than once about other endings.
An ending where my brother finally wins the argument and gets
released. An ending where my brother changes his own mind. I wrote
neither. I understood that this isn't a story that ties off every
thread before it ends. Some threads carry over, unresolved, to the
next page.*

*My brother, that night, and even now as I write this record, is still
a preserved body. Not released. Still eight years old. I don't think
that's a sad thing. There is simply a time that hasn't come yet.*
