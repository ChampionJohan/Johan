# Chapter 22. Looking Down

> _Previously_ — My brother said, "If you'll do it, I'll do it." I
> decided not to accept that answer as consent, and withdrew the
> application myself. For the first time I felt that I had chosen
> something.

After I parted from Eikon, my body felt strangely light and heavy at
once.

The lightness was from withdrawing the application. It felt like I'd
finished something. The heaviness was from the feeling that
finishing it had solved nothing. Not with my mother, not with my
brother — I still had nothing, either way.

That afternoon I didn't go straight back to the lodging.

If I went back, I felt I'd end up counting something again. Five
fingers, the blanks on a form, the number of my brother's steps. That
day I didn't want to count anything. So I just walked. With no
direction chosen.

*I remember that walk strangely well, even now. It's one of the few
scenes in this account where I walked with no purpose at all. Up
until then I'd always been going somewhere. To the visiting room, to
the Curator's office, to the garden. That day was different.*

As I walked, the streets grew steadily less familiar. At some point I
realized I was moving away from the city's center. Fewer buildings,
fewer people. No more benches from the plaza, no more garden trees.

Even though I'd been in this city a fair while, I'd never once come
this way before. The visiting room, the Curator's office, the ration
station, the garden — the paths I walked always connected those four
places. This road led to none of them.

Even the ground underfoot was different. The streets at the city's
center were all packed hard, but here bare earth showed through in
patches. I walked on that earth. The feel of it under my feet was
familiar again, after a long while. It was close to the feel of the
roads outside.

The people I passed grew fewer too. At first there'd been one or two,
here and there, and then at some point there was no one at all. That
absence felt, strangely, like a relief. It was a road where no one
studied my face.

*Walking that road, I learned for the first time that this city had
something like an "outskirts." At the center, people gathered.
Visiting, walking, crossing paths. At the outskirts, there was no
one. A place with no reason to go. At the time I thought that was
simply a fact of geography.*

---

When I reached the edge of the city, at first I didn't realize that
was where it ended.

The ground simply vanished there. More precisely, the ground suddenly
bent downward. I stopped in front of it.

A low railing ran along that boundary. Not high. About waist height.
Not so much a barrier meant to keep you from crossing, as a height
meant only to mark that this was a boundary. I set my hand on that
railing. It was cold.

I stepped forward and looked down.

---

At first I couldn't see anything. More precisely, it took my eyes a
while to adjust to that landscape. Eyes accustomed to the city's
light couldn't read the colors below right away.

Slowly, shapes came together.

It was a wide valley. Wide enough that I couldn't quite see its end.
The floor was a dry, pale color. I couldn't tell if it was the color
of soil or of ash. Here and there were darker marks, deeper in tone,
but from up here I couldn't tell what they were.

There were no trees. Or — there were, but only a few, standing far
apart, and they had no leaves. Only branches remained, standing bare.
I couldn't tell whether those trees were dead or only sleeping.

The cliff was steeper than I'd expected. I tried to gauge the height
from the city's ground down to the valley floor. It was higher than
any hill I'd seen in the settlement. Layers showed in the cliff face.
Soils of slightly different colors, stacked in horizontal bands. Red
near the top, growing paler going down, until near the bottom it was
almost white. I could only guess that those bands were the mark of a
very long stretch of time. It was a kind of layering that nothing the
old man had taught me could fully explain.

The dark marks scattered across the floor kept catching my eye. They
came in every size. Some were very small, some as large as a
gathering of several people. From that distance I couldn't tell
whether they were only shadows, or the trace of something else. Not
being able to tell, if anything, held my eyes there all the more.

*I've redrawn that first sight of the landscape in my mind many
times. Writing this account, I've felt the temptation to attach some
grand emotion to that scene. But to be honest, in that moment I
wasn't afraid. I wasn't sad, either. I was simply — strange. Down
there was a color that existed nowhere else in this city.*

A wind blew upward from below. A dry, lukewarm wind. A wind I could
hardly feel anywhere else in this city. I stood there a long while,
taking that wind on my face.

---

I looked at the ground beneath my feet again. Even the ground right
in front of the railing had a different texture from the ground
farther inside the city. Softer, and it crumbled a little underfoot.
I tried to find, with my eyes, exactly where that boundary began. I
couldn't find it. Between the city's hard ground and the valley's dry
ground, there was a stretch that shifted gradually. Ground that
belonged to neither side.

There was no sound, either.

More precisely, it was a different kind of absence than the city's.
The city was quiet because there was little sound. Down there seemed
quiet because there was nothing, in itself, to make a sound. I
couldn't put that difference into exact words in that moment, but my
body felt it.

Out of habit, I walked along the railing counting my steps. Twenty to
the left, thirty to the right. I was trying to find the valley's end.
I couldn't. Both ways, it kept running on past what I could see.

*I gave up trying to gauge its size. Right there, for the first time,
I admitted that some things can't be gauged.*

---

A person was walking past nearby. An older man, carrying a small
basket, on his way somewhere. I called out to stop him.

"…Excuse me."

The man stopped walking and looked at me.

"…What's down there?" I asked.

The man glanced briefly where I was pointing. Then he looked back at
me. His expression didn't change much.

"…The valley."

"…What's in it?"

"…I don't know."

I was a little surprised by that short answer.

"…You don't know? Living right here?"

"…I've never been down."

"…Not even once?"

"…No."

"…Why not?"

The man smiled faintly at that question, for the first time. Not a
mocking smile. Just the smile of someone finding the question
unfamiliar.

"…There's no reason to go."

---

I turned that answer over in my mind.

"…Aren't you curious?" I asked.

"…Being curious and having a reason to go are different things."

That distinction was unfamiliar to me. Outside, if you were curious,
you usually went and looked. Curiosity itself was the reason. I
wanted to check that once more.

"…Have you never once wanted to go down?"

The man took the question seriously. There was no sign he was
brushing it off.

"…I think maybe I thought about it once, as a child. But I don't
remember well."

"…Why didn't you go?"

"…Because I didn't know what would be down there — would that do,
as an answer? But that's not quite the exact reason. More precisely,
nothing changes whether I go or not."

"…Why does it matter, whether something changes?"

The man smiled a little at that question.

"…It doesn't matter. That's exactly why I don't go, either."

I couldn't find another question to ask, in front of that circular
logic.

"…Has anyone ever been down there? Anyone at all?"

"…Well. I've never heard of it."

"…Not a single one?"

"…Not a single one."

That answer gave me a strange chill. Since this city was built — or
at least, as far as this man knew — no one had ever set foot in that
valley.

The man, having said that, walked on. He adjusted the basket in his
hands, without looking at me as though I were especially strange.

*Watching his back, I felt something cold pass through me. At first
I thought it must be a forbidden place. I expected a wall, at least,
or a sign. There was none. Only a low railing. Anyone who set their
mind to it could climb over. And yet no one did. Not because it was
blocked, but because there was no reason to go.*

*I'd already met that kind of wall in this city more than once.
Release was the same. Nothing blocked it, and yet no one did it. The
valley was the same. Nothing blocked it, and yet no one went down. In
that moment I sensed, only dimly, that those two walls were built
from the same material.*

---

I drew one deep breath. And I stayed exactly there, standing still,
for a long while.

The wind kept blowing. I tried to count it. One gust, two. I counted
a few times and gave up. Wind wasn't something you could count, the
way steps or laughs were. It kept blowing and stopping, and there was
no telling where one ended and the next began.

*In front of that wind, I had a strange thought. I didn't yet know
that my name meant wind. That meaning wouldn't come to light until
Part Four. But rewriting this scene now, it's hard for me to simply
pass over that coincidence. I stood there a long time, for the first
time, in front of something that couldn't be counted. And later I
learned that the name of that uncountable thing was also my own name.
For now I'll set down only this much, and save the rest for when the
time comes.*

Far off, a single bird flew across above the valley. Even seeing a
bird in this city was rare. I watched to see whether it would fly
down into the valley. Near the edge of the cliff it turned and flew
back toward the city instead. It didn't go in.

*Not even that bird went in. In the moment, I let that pass as
unremarkable. Later it came back to me. If even a thing that could
simply fly over never crossed in, then maybe it wasn't blocked at
all — maybe there truly was nothing inside it. I would soon learn how
wrong that thought was.*

---

I leaned against the railing again and looked down.

The sun was sinking little by little. The valley's colors began to
shift. The dim gray-brown of daytime caught the evening light and
slowly reddened. I watched that change for a long while.

There was one spot where an especially dark shadow lay long across
the ground. I tried to tell whether it was the shadow of something,
or just a shadow made by a fold in the land. The distance was too
great.

*In that moment I thought, very briefly, about going down. I didn't
hold on to that thought for long. Not that day. I didn't yet know
what was down there, and even if I had, that day was already full
with a different weight. My brother's letter, the withdrawn
application. That was as much weight as the day could hold.*

Instead I reached my hand out over the railing. As far as my hand
could reach, there was only empty air. I opened and closed my hand in
that emptiness a moment. Nothing to grasp. Even knowing that was
obvious, I wanted to confirm it.

*I only understood that gesture later. I hadn't wanted to touch the
valley. I'd wanted to confirm, with my own body, that I couldn't
reach it yet. My heart had already crossed that edge; my body was
still inside this city. I felt that gap for the first time that day.*

I stood there and watched the valley turn completely red. And then
watched the red turn back to gray, and then to dark. Once the dark
settled in, the valley became invisible altogether. The boundary, the
cliff, those dark marks — all of it. As though none of it had ever
been there. That vanishing felt, to me, larger than anything I'd seen
by daylight.

---

Walking back, I thought again of that man. There's no reason to go.
That day, for the first time, I understood fully that this sentence
applied to almost everything in this city. There was no well, because
there was no reason for one. There was no release, because there was
no reason for it. No one went down into the valley, because there was
no reason to. All of it was explained by the same sentence. It isn't
needed.

*I felt, for the first time that night, how frightening a sentence
that was. Bad things, mostly, have a reason behind them. If there's a
reason, there's something you can fight. If there's no reason, there
is no target for the fight at all. That night, for the first time, I
understood that this city had no target to fight.*

Back at the lodging, I opened the flask. Not much water was left. I
didn't drink it all. I left some. I didn't know why. I just wanted to
leave some behind.

Outside the window, the city's lights came on one by one. I followed
the order they lit with my eyes. Always the same order. Watching that
familiar order, my mind kept drifting back to the dark gray and the
red I'd seen earlier. This city's lights and that valley's sunset
felt like two different languages.

*Before falling asleep that night, I had this thought for the first
time. I'd thought this city was simply spread out flat, not rising
above anything. But in fact there was another layer, underneath.
That day I saw that layer for the first time. Having seen one, it
seemed there might be others. That night, for the first time, it
occurred to me that this whole city might be a far thicker place than
I'd known.*

Lying under the covers, I couldn't fall asleep for a long time. When
I closed my eyes, I saw that reddening valley again. I'd never once
seen that color, anywhere in this city. The city allowed only an
exact brightness, an exact shadow. Down there, there was no such
exactness. I set it down here honestly — that irregularity frightened
me, and yet I found, strangely, that I couldn't look away.
