# Chapter 28. Going Down

> _Previously_ — I went to Speon's pit every morning. Blisters rose on my
> hands, and calluses settled in. For twelve days I didn't apply for a
> visit. I sensed, dimly, that I was avoiding something. The pit had
> passed my own height, and a ladder had gone in.

On the morning of the thirteenth day, I didn't go to the pit.

My body was turned in a different direction from the moment I opened my
eyes. I decided not to fight that feeling. Speon had been right. There
were times the body knew something before thought did.

Instead I walked to the edge of the city. I hadn't been that way in a
while — to the spot where I'd first looked down into the Dry Valley, and
where Eikon had told me what that valley was. I met no one on the whole
walk there. The city was unusually quiet, that early in the morning.

I stood at the edge and looked down.

The valley was the same as ever, that day too. Dry ground, faded soil,
a landscape without a trace of moisture, however far you looked. I'd
looked down into it several times before. But that day it looked
different.

*I thought for a long time about where that difference came from. I know
now. Before, I had only ever looked down the way you look at something.
That day, for the first time, I was imagining myself going down into it.*

For twelve days, digging that pit, I had gone down and down every day.
The moment it passed my own height, I'd started climbing up and down a
ladder. The digging itself had already been a movement downward. That
morning, for the first time, I felt that fact clearly. I had already
been practicing how to go down. It just so happened the thing I'd been
going down into was a hole in the ground.

*Writing this sentence now, I think that coincidence might not have been
a coincidence at all. I'm not certain. But this much is true: a body that
had spent twelve days repeating the same downward motion, every day,
walked one morning toward a far larger downward, on its own.*

Writing this record, I often go back and count coincidences like that
one. Meeting a well-digger. My hands remembering their work again beside
him. And then, twelve days later, heading toward the valley. Take away
any single one of them, and I wouldn't have been standing at that edge
that day.

Standing at the edge, I said nothing for a long time. Wind blew. That
was rare in this city. It came up from the direction of the valley — dry,
lukewarm wind. I let it hit my face and closed my eyes.

---

I went back to the pit and told Speon.

"…I'm going to go down into that valley."

Speon stopped digging. He looked at me for a long while. Not a surprised
face. If anything, the face of someone who'd been waiting.

"…Why?" he asked.

"…I don't know. I just feel like I have to go."

"…Looking for something?"

I couldn't answer that right away. I didn't know myself what I was going
to look for. Eikon had said there were traces there of people whose
preservation had failed. I couldn't tell whether I wanted to see those
traces, or whether I was looking for something else entirely.

"…I don't know," I said again.

Speon nodded.

"…Will you come with me?" I asked.

He drove the shovel into the earth pile. Then, for the first time, he
went quiet for a long while. That silence was unfamiliar to me. Speon
was usually quick to answer. A question would come and an answer would
follow, almost right away. This time was different. He touched the
pit's wall once, as if stroking it, looked up at the sky once, and only
then opened his mouth.

I was counting the length of that silence. By the time I reached ten, he
finally spoke.

---

"…No." He said it, at last.

"…Why not?"

"…That's a place you have to go."

I didn't understand that at once. I couldn't help asking again.

"…What does that mean?"

"…I'm a man who digs wells. I can decide where the water goes. But why
you have to go down into that valley isn't something I can decide for
you. Only you know that."

"…I don't know it either."

"…Then you go not knowing. That's how it should be."

I wanted to argue with that, but I had nothing to argue with. Speon kept
smoothing his shovel and neither pushed me nor tried to stop me.

*I didn't understand it then. I understood later. Speon was a man who
drew water toward a place. He wasn't a man who led people toward one.
If I had to go down into that valley, it had to be on my own feet. Not
led there by anyone's hand.*

I stood there a while longer. From inside the pit I could hear Speon
digging again — earth being lifted, feet pressing down. That familiar
rhythm only made me more restless. As long as that sound went on, I kept
feeling like I was putting something off.

"…Scared?" Speon asked, without stopping his digging.

"…Yes."

"…Scared of what?"

I couldn't answer right away. I couldn't tell whether it was the valley
itself that frightened me, or what I'd see there, or the fear of coming
back up somehow changed.

"…I don't know."

"…So you're afraid of not knowing." Speon said. "That's a fine kind of
fear to have. The bad kind of fear is being afraid of what you know."

"…What's the difference?"

"…Fear of what you know keeps you from going at all. Fear of what you
don't know just means you're afraid while you go."

I couldn't say I understood that distinction. But hearing it, my feet
felt a little lighter.

I asked Speon one last thing.

"…What if I don't come back up?"

Speon's hand stopped. For the first time, his face turned serious.

"…Then I'll come down."

"…You just said I had to go alone."

"…You not coming back up alive, and you not being able to come back up,
are different things," Speon said. "If you see something down there and
decide not to come back up, that's your own to decide. But if you're
hurt and can't come back up, that's mine to go for."

I liked that distinction. It wasn't a promise never to come for me. It
was a promise to respect me.

"…So you're worried?" I asked.

"…I am. But being worried doesn't mean I can go in your place."

I heard that and looked at Speon's face again. It was a worried face.
And yet that worry didn't hold me back. That was the first day I learned
that both things could live inside one person at once.

---

"…Do I need to prepare anything?" I asked.

"…Water." Speon said it shortly. "Not much else."

"…Is it dangerous?"

"…Don't know. No one's ever gone down."

That answer surprised me a little. He'd always seemed, until then, like
a man who moved only once he knew something.

"…You haven't gone either?"

"…No. Thirty-seven wells, and I've never once come across a valley like
this one."

"…Then why tell me to go, without telling me anything?"

"…Because there's nothing I can tell you. If it were something I knew,
I'd teach it to you gladly enough. This is something I don't know."

I turned that over. Since coming to this city, I'd always been the one
learning something someone else knew. Procedure, from the Curator. This
city's logic, from Eikon. Digging, from Speon. The valley was a place no
one knew anything about. I found, strangely, that I liked that.
Somewhere I didn't have to follow in anyone's footsteps. Somewhere my
own first footprint would become the only record of that ground.

*I can only say it now — that it actually put me at ease. If it was a
place no one knew, there was nothing shameful in not knowing it myself.*

---

I filled my flask and shouldered the small sack Speon had packed for me.
A little dried food, a coil of rope.

"…Why the rope?"

"…Don't know. There are times it's good to have."

I laughed at that. It was such a Speon answer. A man who prepared without
certainty. And yet his preparations always fit exactly what was needed.

Before heading back to the dormitory, I ran into Eikon. By chance. She
was standing where she always walked.

"…Where are you going?" she asked, looking at the sack on my shoulder.

"…The valley."

Eikon's expression stiffened for a moment.

"…Why?"

"…I don't know. I'm curious."

"…There's nothing to see there," she said. "They're just failures."

"…I'm curious what failure looks like."

Eikon looked at me for a long while. Then she said this.

"…Don't come back a different person."

I didn't understand what that meant and tried to ask her again. But she
turned and walked off before I could.

*I turned that line over for a long time afterward. Don't come back a
different person. In this city, changing at all was itself something to
be feared. Eikon had shared that fear with me. I couldn't tell, at the
time, whether it was worry or warning. Thinking of it now, I think it was
both. And either way, it meant she was thinking of me.*

I opened the sack once more and checked what was inside. Rope, dried
food, a small knife — the old man's knife. Packing it, I felt somehow
steadier. I thought for a moment about how the old man might have walked
a road like this one. He probably would have just walked, without a
word.

On the way I passed the distribution station. The attendant saw me and
asked.

"…Where are you off to? You filled your flask twice."

"…I'm going down into the valley."

The attendant's face moved, for the first time, in a big way. A face of
surprise.

"…There? Why?"

"…I don't know. I just feel like I have to go."

"…No one goes there."

"…I know."

He looked at me a moment, then, without a word, filled my flask to the
very top. More than the regulation amount. I noticed and said nothing.
He said nothing either.

*I remember that moment clearly. In a city that ran on exactness, that
day he stepped just slightly outside the regulation, for the first time.
No one made anything of it. It felt to me like proof that something was
still left in the people of this city. Some small wish to slip, just a
little, unnoticed, past what was set.*

I thanked the attendant and started walking again. I half-waited for a
moment to hear him call after me. No sound came. I decided that wasn't a
bad thing either.

"…When are you coming back up?" he asked.

"…I don't know."

"…All right. Go, not knowing."

I heard that and started walking. A few steps on, I looked back. Speon
was already digging again. He hadn't seen me off. He simply went on with
his own work.

*That retreating figure didn't hurt me. It was easier, if anything. Being
seen off gives you one more reason you have to come back. Speon hadn't
loaded me with that reason. My coming back had to be something for which
the reason had to already be mine.*

---

The path down into the valley began at the city's edge.

At first it was a gentle slope. I walked carefully over loose gravel.
The sound of earth crumbling underfoot rose up. It was a sound I'd never
once heard inside the city — every path there was packed hard and firm.

Scattered dry brush lined both sides of the path. Branches without a
single leaf on them. I touched one. It rustled as if it would snap, but
it didn't. It just held on, dried out. That stayed with me, strangely —
things standing there a long time, neither dead nor alive.

After a while the slope steepened. I used the rope several times, hooking
it into cracks in the rock and lowering myself down. It was a method the
old man had taught me. My hands remembered it exactly.

*That was when I understood why Speon had packed the rope. He'd said he
didn't know, but he'd sensed, dimly, that a path like this might exist.
Saying you don't know and preparing nothing at all are different things.
I turned that difference over, going down and down.*

I began counting my steps. Counting was an old habit of mine. Every
hundred steps I stopped for a sip of water. Two hundred, three hundred.
The path kept leading down, and down. At first I kept looking back
toward where the city had been; at some point I stopped looking back at
all.

I came to a stretch thick with rock. I climbed down bracing myself
against them with my hands. Every time the healed-over blister marks on
my palms touched stone, they stung. I bore the pain and kept going down.
The pain almost seemed to show me the way — the farther down I went, the
worse it hurt. Hurting meant I was doing something.

I remembered something the old man used to say. On rough ground, read it
with your hands first. Hands are more honest than eyes. Following that,
I checked the rock face with my fingertips before setting my feet down.
A few times my foot slipped, but I never fell hard.

Around the six-hundredth step, I realized for the first time that I'd
been walking without looking back at all. My eyes had been forward the
whole time.

---

As I went down, the air changed.

The city's air was always cool and dry. The valley's air was drier
still. My throat felt scratchy with every breath. I took a sip from the
flask. The water seemed to be going down far faster than it had in the
city.

The sound was different too. The city had at least a little sound —
footsteps, a door. The valley had none at all. My own breath and my own
footsteps were the only sound there was.

*I thought I'd heard that silence somewhere before. It took me a long
time to place it. The night before my mother fell ill, just before
sickness swept the settlement — the whole village holding its breath.
That silence. The silence right before something happens.*

The light was different too. The city's sunlight was always the same —
falling in exact, predictable brightness, morning like morning and
evening like evening. The valley's light changed color with every angle
it struck. Rock-shadows stretched long, then shortened; dust caught the
sun and drifted in a pale haze. Watching that shifting light, I found
myself thinking this valley felt, strangely, more alive than the city
did. It was a strange thought. I'd been told this was a land of dry,
dead things.

A few times something crumbled underfoot. I stepped on it without
thinking, believing it was stone, and only realized afterward that it
wasn't. Each time, I stopped and looked down. I didn't yet have the
nerve to find out what it was. I just kept walking.

Once I nearly took a bad fall. A rock that looked solid crumbled soft and
dry beneath me. I threw out my arm and caught the rock beside it. My
heart pounded for a long while after. I sat down there a moment to catch
my breath.

*That was the first time I truly felt this path held real danger. Until
then I'd said I was afraid, but it hadn't been a danger my body had
lived through. In that brief moment when the rock crumbled, I felt that I
was truly alone here. No one to help. That fact frightened me, and yet,
strangely, it also felt clear.*

*That was the first time I understood, in my body, the difference
between this city and this valley. The city had no danger. In exchange,
nothing ever happened. The valley had danger. And inside that danger, I
felt, more clearly than ever, that I was alive.*

Sitting there catching my breath, I opened the flask and took a sip. More
than half the water was already gone. I gauged what was left and started
walking again.

Standing back up, I looked at my palms. Bracing myself against the rock
had torn the healed blister-marks open again. No blood. Just thin,
peeled skin left behind. I opened and closed my hand a moment, checking
the feeling in it, and walked on.

The slope went on. I used the rope a few more times. Each time, my hands
grew a little surer at the knots. What had taken two tries to tie
correctly at first came together in one, later on. My body seemed to
have fully remembered the work all over again.

---

Before reaching the bottom, I looked back up one last time.

The city was up there. Small and quiet. That was the moment I first felt,
truly, how far I had come — from where my mother and brother were, from
where Speon was, from the distribution station and the Curator's
building.

*For a moment I thought about turning back. That it wasn't too late yet.
But my feet kept going down. Speon had been right. My body moved before
thought did.*

Dust rose low underfoot and settled again. I stood there and thought of
my mother and my brother. At this very hour, they would be there, with
the same faces, in the same room, waiting for me to come. Whether it
could even be called waiting, I wasn't sure. They didn't wait. They
simply were there. I turned that difference over once more and kept
walking.

The slope beneath my feet began, little by little, to level out. I could
feel I was nearly there. My last few steps came strangely slow. There was
no reason to hurry. If anything, it felt like I shouldn't.

My foot finally touched the valley floor.

The soil was different from the city's. Lighter. It broke apart under my
foot and scattered like powder. I stood there and caught my breath for a
moment.

I looked all around.

It was different from what I'd imagined. I'd expected something
frightening, something terrible. But what first came into view was
simply dry ground stretching wide open — dry ground that seemed to run
all the way to the horizon.

And scattered across it, there were things.

I hadn't gone close enough yet to know what they were. But I could tell,
even from a distance. Those things had once been something.

I turned back and looked up at the path I'd come down. The city was no
longer visible. The slope blocked the view. Strangely, that put me at
ease. Not being able to see the way back didn't mean I couldn't go back.
It only seemed to mean that, for now, I should look only forward.

I closed the flask's cap and fastened it firmly at my waist. Then I took
a step. Dust rose quietly underfoot and settled again.

*I stood there for a long while. I was afraid to take the next step. It
would be a long time — not until I had properly walked the valley — that
I understood exactly what that fear had been for.*

*I took my first step toward the scattered things.*
