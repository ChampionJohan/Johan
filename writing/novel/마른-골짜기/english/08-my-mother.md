# Chapter 8. My Mother

> _Previously_ — In the waiting room, Anemos went back over the words he had prepared for seven years. The greeting, everything that had happened since, and the main thing — "Let's go together now." The officer pointed at the door. He took hold of the doorknob and took one deep breath.

He opened the door.

The room was small. There was one window, and sunlight was coming in. Two chairs stood facing each other.

My mother was sitting in one of them.

*I have relived that moment more times than I can count. Even now, writing this record, if I close my eyes the room is still there exactly as it was. The chairs, the sunlight, and my mother.*

The room had none of the usual things. No painting on the wall, no vase on a table. Only two chairs and one window. I didn't notice that plainness at the time. My eyes saw only my mother.

---

My mother was exactly the same.

The way her hair was tied, the way she sat, even the color of her clothes — all exactly as I remembered. For a moment I felt like my breath had stopped.

She saw me and stood up.

"…Anemos."

Her voice was exactly the same too. The very voice I had called to mind with my eyes closed, for seven years.

"…Mom."

I barely managed that one word. None of the greeting I had prepared came out.

---

She came to me and cupped my face in both hands.

"…You've grown so much."

"…Yes."

"…Have you been eating well?"

I laughed a little at that question. The first thing said after seven years apart was about food.

"…Yes, I've been eating well."

She looked me over for a long time. Up and down, from my face to my feet.

"…Your voice has deepened too."

*In that moment I understood how pointless my worry had been, that she might not recognize me. She knew me at once. She saw exactly how much I had changed. But there was no surprise in that recognition. It was as if she were seeing someone she had said goodbye to yesterday.*

She noticed the flask slung over my shoulder and tapped it lightly with her fingertips.

"…What's this?"

"…A water flask. My uncle gave it to me."

"…Did you think you couldn't get here without water?"

"…Just habit, I guess."

She smiled a little at my answer. This smile had a different texture from the one before. I didn't notice the difference at the time.

"…Same as ever, you." she said. "Even as a child you always carried everything you needed."

I nodded at that. It was true.

---

"…Sit." she said.

I sat in the chair across from her. The distance between the two chairs was just enough for a hand to reach across. She took my hand. It was warm. Only then did I realize I had been holding my breath until that moment.

*The first thing I checked was the warmth of that hand. I had been afraid it might be cold, or strange somehow. But it was just a human hand. Warm, soft, an ordinary hand.*

"…Are you all right?" I asked. "Does it hurt?"

"…No, it doesn't hurt at all."

"…Is anything hard?"

"…No. Everything's fine."

I felt relieved at that answer. And at the same time, something felt empty. Like there was some other question I should be asking, and I didn't know what it was. My mother's expression didn't move at all. To the question about pain, to the question about hardship, the same-temperature answer came back each time. I didn't know then what that emptiness was.

*I would only put a name to that emptiness in the next chapter. Here I'll only note that the feeling was there.*

"…Weren't you scared?" I asked. "Back then, when the fever got bad and you had to lie down."

She thought for a moment, then said,

"…I think I was scared. But I don't remember it well."

"…You don't?"

"…The feeling from back then is blurry. Because now I'm not scared at all."

That answer struck me as strange. Whether a fading memory of fear was a good thing or a bad thing, I couldn't decide. I remembered exactly how many days that fever had lasted, while my mother barely remembered even the fact that it had been frightening.

*I never manage to settle that judgment by the end of this book. Whether a feeling fading is a mercy or a loss — I still don't know.*

"…Mom." I called.

"…Yes."

"…Did you know I was coming all the way here? That I'd come."

She looked at me and smiled.

"…No, I didn't know. But it's not a surprise."

"…Why?"

"…Because you're the kind of child who'd come."

The answer was short. But there was some certainty folded inside it. I was glad of that certainty.

"…Did you miss me?" I asked. It wasn't something I had prepared. It just came out.

She paused a moment at that question.

"…Yes. I missed you."

I felt relieved to hear it. But the speed at which the answer came was a little strange. Too exact a speed. Not sorrow, not longing — just the speed of stating the correct answer.

*I didn't find that speed strange at the time. I know now. It wasn't a lie. She really must have missed me. Only, that missing probably didn't carry the same weight as the missing I had carried for seven years.*

---

I tried to recall the order I had prepared.

First, the greeting. Already ruined. Second, everything that had happened since.

"…A lot has happened to me since then." I said.

"…Tell me."

"…Um, there's a boy named Gaon, my uncle's youngest…"

"…Oh, that child. Is he doing well?"

I was a little surprised that she remembered Gaon. He had been young even before she left.

"…Yes, he's doing well. He's grown almost as tall as me now."

"…That's fast."

The tone was strange. There was no surprise in that word "fast." It was just a tone that confirmed a fact.

*I didn't find that tone strange at the time. I know now. For her, seven years wasn't a fast span of time. The very unit of seven years had no meaning for her.*

---

I started to bring up the old man, then stopped.

*I had set that story as the second item under the second heading. The story of the old man stopping. But when it actually came time to open my mouth, my throat closed up.*

"…What were you about to say?" she asked.

"…Nothing. Later."

She nodded. She didn't press.

*I thought back on that moment later. Why hadn't she asked further? Was it that she wasn't curious, or that she saw I was struggling? I still don't know the answer for certain.*

Instead she asked me something else.

"…Where are you staying now?"

"…There's a lodging house in this city. The information office assigned it."

"…Isn't it lonely being alone?"

I laughed at the question. The question of whether it was hard being alone — the very thing I'd just asked her — she was handing right back to me.

"…I'm fine. There's someone in the next room too."

"…Are they a good person?"

"…Yes, seems like a kind person."

She nodded. There seemed to be some relief mixed into that nod. I was glad of it. It felt like proof that she was worrying about me.

*I kept that moment for a long time as the best moment of that visit. That brief moment of my mother worrying about me. I thought it was only natural. I know now how uncommon that moment was.*

I can still picture her face from that moment exactly. Her eyebrows lightly furrowed, her head tilted toward me. It was the face of someone worrying. Wanting to see that face a little longer, I deliberately answered a little slower.

I couldn't bring up my friend at all. The friend who had stopped after eight days. The moment I tried to say his name, my throat closed on its own.

*I never managed to tell that story in this visit, in the end. A story I had been preparing since I was nine, and in front of her I couldn't say a single word of it.*

"…How's my brother?" I asked instead.

Her face brightened a little.

"…He's doing well. When I told him he'd see you later, he was happy."

"…Does he recognize me?"

"…Of course."

I felt relieved at that, and uneasy at the same time. My brother was eight. That he would still be exactly that, even after seven years had passed, weighed on me for the first time in that moment.

"…Isn't he having a hard time here either?"

"…No, he's fine."

"…Does he get bored?"

She tilted her head at that question, exactly as before.

"…He doesn't really use the word 'bored,' that child."

*I let that answer pass without dwelling on it at the time. Only after meeting my brother in person, later, would I understand what those words meant.*

"…Is he nearby right now too?"

"…Yes, remember, today you're to see me first, and your brother a little after."

"…I want to see him soon."

"…He hasn't changed one bit. Be ready to be surprised."

She said that, laughing. A light laugh. I tried to laugh along with it, but it didn't quite work.

*I took "he hasn't changed one bit" as just a joke at the time. Two hours later I would learn that it wasn't a joke at all, but an exact fact.*

---

"…Is my uncle doing well?" she asked.

"…Yes. Same as ever."

"…I'm grateful. For taking you in."

"…Yes."

"…It must have been hard, in that house."

I started to say it hadn't been, but just nodded instead.

*I don't know why I didn't deny it. I wanted to say it hadn't been hard, but the words wouldn't come. Maybe, in that moment, I was admitting for the first time that it had been a little hard after all.*

"…You must have thought of your father a lot too." she said suddenly.

I was startled by that. Eleven years had passed since my father disappeared. For her to bring him up first was rare. In our family, my father was always a subject someone raised carefully — lowering their voice, watching for reactions.

"…Sometimes."

"…Do you remember his face?"

I couldn't answer right away. I hesitated to say it had gone blurry.

"…It's a little blurry."

She nodded at that. She didn't look sad.

"…That can happen."

That calm was strange to me. In our family, talk of my father was always heavy, but the three words that came out of her mouth were far too light.

*I thought at the time that lightness was because she was strong. I know now. It wasn't strength. In this city, weight itself was measured differently.*

She held my hand a little more tightly.

"…You've done well."

At those two words I nearly cried. I barely held it back.

*I remember nine-year-old me writing "while smiling" in the margin of that paper. I kept that homework half done. I didn't cry. I couldn't smile either.*

---

The third item, the main thing, still remained.

*Let's go together now.*

I kept looking for the moment to say those five words. But I kept failing to find it. My mother was talking about other things, and I couldn't find the courage to interrupt.

"…How is it, staying here?" I asked instead.

"…Good. Everything's good."

"…Isn't it boring?"

She tilted her head slightly at that question.

"…What is there to be bored of."

*I felt something blocked in that answer. But I couldn't put my finger on what it was, right then. I would only come to know the name of that feeling after I met Eikon. I'll write that story in Part Two.*

---

I didn't know how much time had passed. The officer opened the door a little and said,

"…Time's up."

My heart sank at those words. Already.

"…Already?"

"…Yes, I'm sorry."

She didn't let go of my hand, and said,

"…Come again."

"…Yes."

"…Let's talk more next time."

I nodded at that. In that moment I realized I hadn't gotten to the main thing. Let's go together now. In the end I never once spoke those five words aloud.

---

At the door she held me once. Her embrace was exactly as I remembered. Same warmth, same strength.

"…Bye now."

*I didn't think, at the time, how strange that farewell was. Seven years since we'd seen each other, and yet the parting words were exactly what you'd say to someone you'd met yesterday.*

Before leaving through the door I turned to look at her once more. I wanted to hold her face in my eyes one last time. She was still standing right there. The same posture, the same expression as a moment before.

*That feeling was strange too. I would come back anyway, and yet I took in her face as though I would never see it again. I didn't know then where that urgency came from. I know now. My mother had nothing to hurry for. I did.*

I stepped out through the door. Looking back, I saw her waving. Her face was smiling.

*That smile was exactly the same too. I walked out watching that smile. All the way down the corridor, through the waiting room, until I stepped outside, I couldn't think of anything at all.*

Outside, the officer was waiting.

"…You can see your brother in a little while. Please rest a bit, then come."

I nodded and sat back down in a waiting-room chair. I could feel the paper against my chest. I didn't take it out to look at it.

*I never unfolded that paper again that day. There was no reason to. The order written on it had already come apart entirely.*
