# Chapter 13. Fifteen Years Old

> _Previously_ — I went to apply for a third visit. I took the form from
> the window. And then I couldn't write on it, and I turned away. I
> couldn't explain to myself why.

It wasn't that my hand shook.

To be precise, my hand was fine. I held the pen properly. I got as far
as the line for my name. I started the first letter of Anemos, and
stopped there.

I tried, right then, to find the reason I'd stopped. It wasn't that my
mother's face had come to me. It wasn't that my brother's face had
come to me. It was the opposite, actually. No face came at all. What
I'd heard at the second visit and what I'd heard at the first played
back, identical, only in a different order.

*I didn't know this then. I know it now. What stopped me in front of
that third application wasn't fear. It was that I already knew the
result. My body knew, before I did, that the third time wouldn't be
any different from the first, and it stood my hand still.*

I set the pen down and pushed the form back toward the window. The
clerk took it without asking anything. That was the most frightening
moment of the whole day. No one stopped me.

After I turned away, I had nowhere to go.

I didn't want to go back to the lodging. I felt like if I went there
I'd only think about the form again. Instead I walked toward the
plazas. The Preserve had several, and they all looked more or less the
same. There were benches, there were trees, and no one ever ran.

*I tried, later, to count those plazas. I couldn't. They all looked so
alike that I lost track of how far I'd gotten. It was the first time
in that city that counting something had failed me.*

As I walked I kept folding and unfolding the form in my hand. The
clerk hadn't said a word. Even when I'd pushed the form back across
the counter, no one had asked why. They'd just taken it and put it in
a drawer. That was stranger, somehow. If someone had asked me
something, I could have had an answer ready.

*I thought a long time about that clerk's blank face. I learned later.
Here, it isn't rare for someone to change their mind. No — more
precisely, changing your mind isn't really a thing that happens much
at all. Everyone lives by what they first decided. By this city's
standard, my turning away from that window was probably the rare
thing.*

I passed about three plazas. People went by. Everyone's stride was
about the same. Not fast, not slow. I didn't see a single person
running. It seemed like there was simply nothing to run for. It was a
city with no lateness, nothing to chase, nothing to flee.

Among those strides I felt how different mine was. I still walked
fast. It was a habit from outside. When you went to draw water, you
had to walk fast. If you were late, everyone else had already used up
the well.

At the fourth plaza I found a bench. It was in the shade of a tree. I
sat down there.

I still had the water flask with me. The one my uncle had packed for
me. I was the only person in this city who drew water at the ration
station and carried it strapped to my waist. Everyone else had no need
to.

I sat on the bench. I opened and closed the flask's cap, opened and
closed it. It was another habit I had instead of counting. Open, shut.
Open, shut.

"…Why do you carry that around?"

I looked up.

---

It was a girl. She looked about my age. She was standing beside the
bench, looking at my flask. There was nothing strange in the face
asking the question. Not mocking, not worried either. Just a face that
was curious.

"…To drink."

"…Water?"

"…Yes."

She tilted her head. It really was a face that didn't understand.

"…If you're thirsty you can just go to a ration station."

"…Sometimes I get thirsty while I'm out walking around."

"…Ah."

She didn't look satisfied with that answer. But she didn't press
further either. She just sat down beside me. She hadn't asked if it
was all right, she'd just sat, and strangely, it didn't feel
uncomfortable.

*I learned later that this was something everyone in that city had in
common. They don't ask permission. Situations that call for permission
almost never arise there. Taking someone's seat, taking someone's
time — both are rare here.*

"…I'm Eikon."

"…I'm Anemos."

"…That's an outside name."

"…Yes."

I didn't ask how she knew that. She must have known from the flask.

---

"…You're not from here." Eikon said.

"…No. I came for a visit."

"…Who?"

"…My mother and my brother."

Eikon nodded. There was no surprise in the nod. It meant people coming
for visits was common.

"…I came for a visit too. Here." I said it again. "…originally."

"…And now you're not?"

"…I don't know."

Eikon didn't think the answer was strange. She just waited for the
next thing. I couldn't say anything more. She didn't push either.

*I remember that silence. Outside, if someone sat quietly next to you
for that long, you'd feel awkward and say something just to fill it. Eikon
didn't. She seemed like a person with no reason to feel awkward.*

After a good while, I asked,

"…How long have you been here, Eikon?"

"…Seven years."

I was startled by the number. It was exactly the year my family had
been preserved.

"…So how old are you?"

"…Fifteen."

"…Even seven years ago?"

"…Yes. I was fifteen then too."

---

It took me a moment to understand what that meant. I'd known, in
principle. That being preserved means you don't age. My mother and my
brother were the same. But those were family, and family was the side
I'd wanted, wanted with all my longing, to stay unchanged.

This girl was a stranger. A stranger staying at exactly the same age
for seven years was strange in a different way.

"…Is it strange?" Eikon asked. She seemed to have caught it from my
face.

"…No. Just."

"…It's fine. Everyone finds it strange at first."

*That way of talking stayed with me. It wasn't a hurt tone. It was an
explaining tone. As if she'd organized a reaction she'd seen many
times before and was simply informing me of it.*

"…I'm sixteen now." I said. "My birthday passed not long ago."

"…So last year we would have been the same age."

I couldn't do that math in the moment. Eikon did it first.

"…When I was fifteen. I've stayed fifteen the whole time, and you must
have been fifteen last year too."

"…That's right."

"…So we were the same age last year. Now I'm a year younger."

She said it laughing. It was a laugh that found the thing genuinely
funny. I couldn't laugh.

*I did the math again right there. It was correct. A year ago we would
have been the same age. But in that year, only I had moved. She had
stayed exactly where she was. I had, in effect, passed her by.*

*Whether passing her by was a good thing, I didn't know then. I still
don't, really, now.*

---

"…What are you thinking about?" Eikon asked.

"…Nothing."

"…Your face is complicated."

The observation was accurate, so I couldn't say anything. Eikon looked
at my face for a while, then, as if she didn't much care either way,
turned her eyes back to the flask.

"…What do you do here, Eikon? All day."

"…Various things. Meeting people, walking, looking around."

"…You don't work?"

"…There's not really anything to do. There's not much here that needs
making."

"…So aren't you bored?"

Eikon smiled a little at the question. It wasn't a mocking smile. It
was an amused one.

"…It's been a while since I've heard the word bored."

I found the answer strange. It didn't sound like she didn't know what
bored meant — it sounded like she simply hadn't had occasion to hear
the word.

"…Did you used to know?"

"…Yes. Before I came here."

"…And now?"

Eikon looked up at the sky for a moment. Not a face searching for
something — more like a face just resting its eyes.

"…I guess now there's no reason to be bored."

*I turned that answer over late into that night. No reason. As if she
meant boredom, and being tired of things, were feelings that only
arose when there was a reason for them. Outside, you got bored even
with no reason. Here, it seemed, without a reason nothing arose at
all.*

"…Want to try a sip?"

I opened the cap and held it out. Eikon took it and drank. One sip.

"…How does it taste?" I asked.

Eikon made a face like she was thinking for a moment.

"…Just like water."

"…Isn't it strange, though? Isn't this the first water you've had in
seven years?"

"…What would be strange about it. Water's just water."

I didn't know what to say to that. It wasn't the answer someone who
hadn't drunk water in seven years should give. More precisely, it was
the answer of someone who'd never needed to.

Eikon handed the flask back.

"…Why do you keep carrying that flask around anyway? There must be
times the ration station's closer."

I wasn't ready with an answer to that.

"…I don't know. I just feel better holding on to it."

*It wasn't a lie. But it wasn't the whole answer either. I'd never once
thought of throwing the flask away. That the water jar my mother had
filled the night before she left kept coming back to me — that, I
didn't tell her that day.*

---

"…What about your family?" I asked. "Are they here?"

"…Yes. Both my parents."

"…Do you see them often?"

"…Sometimes."

"…Don't you miss them?"

Eikon's face looked at the question for a moment, as if peering into
it. She wasn't angry. The question itself seemed foreign to her.

"…Why would I miss them when I can just go see them?"

I was at a loss for that answer. Outside, missing someone arose
whether or not you could see them. Here, the two seemed bound
together into one thing. If you could see someone, there was nothing
to miss.

"…I couldn't see mine for seven years." I said. "So I kept missing
them the whole time."

"…Ah, that would be different, then."

Eikon said only that much and didn't go on. I was grateful for the
brief acknowledgment, and also let down by it. It was true that it was
different. But exactly what that difference was, she made no attempt
to find out.

*I didn't feel, at the time, that she was dismissing me. It was the
opposite, actually. She honestly marked what she didn't know as not
known. Everyone in that city was like that. They didn't pretend to
know what they didn't. I thought that was kindness. Later I would
learn how far away that kindness came from.*

Eikon tapped the flask lightly with her fingertip.

"…What are you going to do once you drink all this, though? Are you
going to be here a while?"

"…I don't know. Not yet."

"…Did you stop partway through writing your form?"

I looked at her, startled at how she knew. Eikon shrugged.

"…You have the face for it. People here get it sometimes too. The
face of someone who stopped in the middle of deciding something."

"…People here have that too, sometimes?"

"…Very rarely. There isn't much to decide in the first place, so it's
not common."

I didn't let that pass unnoticed. That there isn't much to decide.
There seemed to be something important folded into that sentence, but
that day I couldn't catch hold of what it was.

---

The sun had tilted a little. In this city that, too, felt like an
exact clock. The shadows always stretched at the same angle.

"…I have to go." Eikon stood.

"…Where to?"

"…Just. There's somewhere to be."

I didn't ask what that meant. I felt I didn't yet have the right to
ask.

"…See you again." Eikon said. It wasn't a parting courtesy — it was
said in a tone that meant it truly would happen.

"…Okay."

She walked off. I sat on the bench and watched her back. Her walk was
light. There was nothing hurried in it.

*Watching that back, I had this thought for the first time. Among the
people I'd met in this city, she was the first who hadn't looked at me
with pity. The Curator, the ration-station clerk — everyone had, in
some way, handled me carefully. Eikon didn't. She just treated me as
someone she was curious about.*

*At the time, that ease felt good. Later I'd learn why that was so. She
had no reason to be careful with me. A person who lacks nothing has no
need to pity anyone else either.*

---

On the way back to the lodging I passed the ration station. The clerk
saw me and gave a nod of recognition. Everyone recognized me once they
saw the flask.

"…Filling up again today?"

"…No. I've still got some left."

The clerk nodded and went back to their work. That person, too, didn't
look at me strangely. Only it was a different kind from Eikon's. That
person knew I was from outside and so didn't trouble themselves over
it. Eikon had simply been curious, regardless of who I was.

*I didn't know the difference then. I know it now. One was a kindness
that acknowledged an exception. The other was the attitude of someone
for whom the very concept of an exception didn't exist.*

I got back to the lodging and looked out the window. The city was
still quiet. A quiet with no sound of water in it. I'd started to grow
a little used to that quiet. That was what frightened me. Growing used
to it.

I didn't take the form back out that night. Instead I turned over the
things she'd said. To drink. What would be strange about it. Water's
just water. I guess now there's no reason to be bored.

Those words stayed with me strangely long. Each one, taken alone, was
true. But gathered together, something felt missing. What it was, I
couldn't put into words that day.

*That day I noticed, for the first time, that she always paused
briefly before she began to speak. It wasn't that she wasn't in a
hurry. It seemed more like there was simply no reason to hurry in the
first place. I hadn't been in this city long, and already, without
noticing, I'd begun to talk the same way.*
