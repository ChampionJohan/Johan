# Chapter 32. Back Up

> _Previously_ — He asked me: "…Do you think these can live again?" I
> couldn't answer. He didn't fault me for that. "You're the first." By
> afternoon I decided to go back to the city.

The road up felt longer than the road down had been.

The same slope, the same rocks. And yet my feet kept feeling heavy. I
couldn't tell whether that was my body's exhaustion, or something else.

I used the rope a few times. The knots came together in one try now. My
hands seemed to have fully relearned that work. I smiled a little,
watching them. The twelve days of shoveling hadn't gone to waste.

*Climbing that slope, I kept turning that question over the whole way.
Do you think these can live again. The question was under my feet the
entire climb. Every time I set a foot down, it asked itself again. I
decided not to look for an answer. As he had told me to, the night
before.*

---

Around the six-hundredth step, I looked back.

The valley lay down there below. Wide, quiet, dry exactly as it had been
the day before. I tried to find him in that view. I couldn't. Either he
was too far off, or he'd gone behind some fold of ground.

*I felt a strange disappointment then. It hadn't even been a full day,
and already it felt natural to me that he would keep being there. I
would go through that disappointment again many times, later. I'll write
more about it in Chapter 47.*

I turned and kept walking.

---

By the time I reached the city's edge, the sun had passed a little
beyond its height.

I caught my breath. I brushed off the dust. There was white powder from
the valley on my hands and my clothes. I didn't brush all of it off. For
some reason, I didn't want to.

The city spread out before my eyes.

The buildings, the square, the distribution station, the Curator's
building far off. All exactly as it had been. I confirmed that with my
own eyes. Nothing had changed. Not the roof colors, not the width of the
streets, not the way people walked.

Even the light was different. The valley's light had come filtered
through white powder; whatever it fell on lost a shade of its color. The
city's light was yellow and clear. I stood under that light and looked at
my own hand. The white powder caught in the back of it stood out
strangely. Even the air was different. The valley's air had been heavy
and dry; here it was light, and smelled newly washed.

And yet it looked different.

*I tried, for a long time, to rewrite this sentence. I wanted to explain
exactly how it looked different. After rewriting it many times, I finally
accepted that this sentence was the most accurate one there was. Nothing
had changed, and yet it looked different. Beyond that, I have no
explanation.*

I stood there and tried to count the city. How many roofs there were, how
many alleys. I'd done this several times before. The numbers had always
come out the same. I counted again, that day. The roofs came out the
same number as ever. So did the alleys, and the square.

*Only after confirming that not one of those numbers had changed did I
understand why I'd felt uneasy. I think I had secretly hoped something
would have changed. Seeing that nothing had, I felt, if anything, even
more like a stranger here.*

---

At first I thought it might be the light.

I wondered whether my eyes, having grown used to the valley's white
light, were simply taking in the city's familiar light differently. But
as I walked I learned that wasn't it.

I passed the distribution station. The attendant recognized me and
nodded. The same greeting as always. And yet, receiving it, I felt it as
something strange. This man had greeted me exactly the same way
yesterday, and the day before that. I noticed it, freshly, as if for the
first time.

I passed the square. There were the benches. A few people sitting on
them. I looked at them. They all sat quietly. No one running, no one
hurrying.

*I must have seen that scene dozens of times. It had been over a month
since I'd come to this city. And yet that day the scene looked strange
to me, as if I were seeing it for the first time. To eyes that had just
come from the valley, this quiet read differently.*

One person was standing in front of a flowerbed. A small flowerbed. One
of the few in this city — planted not with flowers, but simply with dry
grass. That person was holding a watering can. Giving it exactly the
regulation amount of water. I watched that motion for a long time.

*That flowerbed wasn't receiving water because it needed it. It was
receiving it because that was the set procedure. I noticed that
difference for the first time that day. There was no one watering
anything in the valley. No one had set a schedule for it there. And yet
I'd felt something alive there. This flowerbed, receiving its exact water
every single day, gave off no such feeling at all.*

Passing in front of the Curator's building, the clerk at the front desk
recognized me and gave a light greeting. Receiving it, I thought of my
mother and my brother. At this very moment, the two of them were still
in that room, in the same posture as yesterday. My steps slowed a little
at that thought. Then I kept walking. I didn't want to go that way,
today.

---

I walked along the channel.

Speon's channel. It was running all the way to the city's edge now. I
walked listening to it. The sound of water was exactly the same as
yesterday. And yet even that sound was heard differently.

*I had a thought for the first time then. It wasn't the sound that had
changed. It was my ears. I had heard true silence, in the valley. A
silence with no sound in it at all. Coming back to the city, even this
small sound of water rang loud.*

Walking, I heard, freshly, sounds I'd never before picked out one by one.
The click of the distribution station's scale. The sound of someone
opening and closing a door. Footsteps far off, all of them small and
careful. It had been over a month since I'd come to this city, and even
having lived that long among these sounds, I had never once listened to
them separately, one by one.

I went to the pit. Speon was there, smoothing the soil.

"…Back," he said, setting his shovel upright. It wasn't a worried face.
But there was something in the eyes that looked at me.

"…Yes."

"…All right?"

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…I don't know," I said. "Whether I'm all right or not."

Speon nodded at that answer. He didn't press further.

---

"…What was down there?" he asked.

I didn't know how to answer that question. Bones, traces, and a person. I
didn't know which of those to say first.

"…I met someone," I said.

Speon stopped shoveling.

"…Someone?"

"…Yes. Inside the valley. Walking among the bones."

Speon looked at me for a long time. It was a surprised face, though not
enormously surprised.

"…What kind of person?"

"…I don't know. He didn't tell me his name."

"…What did he say?"

I stopped for a moment at that question. I wasn't sure I could carry that
question over to Speon — do you think these can live again.

"…He asked me one question," I said. "I still haven't answered it."

Speon didn't press further at that. He just nodded.

"…There are questions you don't answer," he said. "Not everything has
to have an answer."

Strangely, that was a comfort to me.

---

On the way back to the dormitory, I stopped in front of the tub of water
they kept for washing. A large tub. The water lay still inside it.

I looked at my own face reflected on it.

My face had tanned a little over the past few days. Dried dust still lay
in a thin film on my cheeks. My eyes looked a little more sunken than
before. I looked at that face for a long time.

*It was then I understood, exactly, for the first time, that the city
hadn't changed at all. The city hadn't changed one bit. What had changed
was me. A face that had spent a day in the valley simply looked
different to eyes that had come back to the city. But knowing that didn't
make the strangeness go away.*

I scooped water in my hand and washed my face. The dust washed down. The
water clouded and then cleared again. I looked into that tub a long
while before I moved on.

---

I walked the city the whole afternoon.

No particular purpose. I just wanted to walk. As I walked I kept
comparing things. The valley's soil against the city's streets. The
valley's silence against the city's small sounds. The valley's scattered
things against the city's ordered people.

*I knew that comparison wasn't fair. One was a land of failures, and the
other was a city of successes. And yet I couldn't stop making it.*

Passing in front of the Curator's building, I stopped walking a moment. I
thought of my mother and my brother. I thought about going to visit.
But my feet wouldn't move that way.

*I didn't know the reason then. I learned it later. I didn't want to see
my mother while I still hadn't found an answer to that question. The
question and my mother had, somewhere along the way, become bound
together as one.*

I saw the Curator climbing the steps to his building. He didn't seem to
notice me. He was climbing with a folder tucked under his arm. His pace
was slower than usual. I watched his back for a moment.

*I saw something in that back, though I couldn't put a name to it then.
A weight different from mere tiredness. I would only come to know what it
was in Chapter 34. Writing this passage now, I find myself wanting to
count how many times a day he climbs and descends those six steps. I've
never counted it.*

---

That evening I met Eikon.

By chance. She was in the spot where she always walked. The moment she
saw me, her expression changed slightly.

"…You're back," she said. "You're still you?"

I laughed a little at that question. I remembered what she'd said before
I went down to the valley. Don't come back a different person.

"…I don't know," I said, honestly. "I'm not sure I know myself well
either."

At that answer, Eikon's face took on a slightly strange look.

"…Isn't that the same as becoming a different person?"

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…What was down there?" she asked.

"…Bones. Traces. And there was a person."

"…A person? In that valley?"

"…Yes."

Eikon's face stiffened slightly.

"…There can't be a person there."

I said nothing to that. I wanted to ask why she was so certain, but I
didn't.

---

"…What did that person say?" Eikon asked.

I hesitated a moment. I wasn't sure I could carry that question over to
her either.

"…He asked me — do you think these can live again."

Eikon's face showed nothing at all, hearing that. After a long while, she
said this.

"…That's a strange question."

"…Why?"

"…The answer's obvious. Bones don't live."

I wanted to say something to that, but the words wouldn't come. I
couldn't call her wrong. But I didn't want to say she was right either.

"…Is that what you think?" I asked.

"…Of course. It's right that they don't live."

I envied that certainty. At the same time, it frightened me a little.

"…Why couldn't you answer, Anemos?" Eikon asked. It wasn't a mocking
tone.

"…I don't know either. I didn't want to say yes, and I didn't want to
say no."

"…Why not?"

I thought about that question for a long time.

"…If I say no, it feels like admitting those people are really over.
I couldn't do that."

"…And if you say yes?"

"…That feels like a lie. Not something I said because I really believe
it — just because I wish it were so."

Eikon looked at me steadily for a moment, hearing that.

"…That's strange," she said. "I've never once had to think about that."

"…Why not?"

"…Because for me there's only one answer. There's nothing to think
about."

Listening to that, I felt that in her world there was no place at all
for not knowing. I felt both envy and sadness at that. I couldn't gauge,
then, which was larger.

*I kept turning that conversation over on the way back. Eikon had said no
without a moment's hesitation. I couldn't. I would come to understand a
little more where that difference came from, in the next chapter.*

---

That night, I lay in the dormitory looking up at the city's familiar
ceiling.

The night before, there had been the valley's stars. Tonight it was just
a ceiling. I turned that difference over for a long time.

*That was the first night I didn't feel this city was holding me
prisoner. Instead I felt this city was putting something off. I couldn't
put into words, then, what it was putting off. I can now. This city was a
place that deferred the question. The valley was a place that kept
asking it.*

I looked at my palms. I could feel the thin callus that had settled over
the old blister marks. I started to count how many times that hand had
touched the city's walls that day, and gave it up. The days I gave up
counting were growing more frequent.

I lay down with my head on the flask. Water sat half-full inside it. I
thought about where that water had come from. The distribution station.
The valley's water hadn't reached that far yet.

*I didn't know, then, that this water would one day run all the way to
that valley. If I had known, I wouldn't have slept as easily as I did
that night.*

Before falling asleep, I brought that question to mind one last time. Do
you think these can live again. This time too, I made no answer to it.
Instead I simply set the question down beside me and closed my eyes.

*I dreamed that night. A dream in which the valley and the city ran
together. My mother was walking across the white soil, and my brother was
holding the water flask. I didn't remember all of the dream when I woke.
Only one scene stayed with me clearly. My mother turned to look at me and
said something, but no sound came.*
