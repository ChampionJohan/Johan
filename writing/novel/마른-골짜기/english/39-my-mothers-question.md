# Chapter 39. My Mother's Question

> _Previously_ — I came to understand, myself, why I had come to this
> city. Not only for my family, but to go back to the time when I
> wasn't alone. With that share acknowledged, I applied for another
> visit the next day.

In the morning my brother came looking for me.

Lately he often came and waited in front of the room where I was
staying. Not a visit — just there in the yard. He never came inside the
room itself. I didn't ask why.

"…Brother, are you going again today?" my brother asked.

"…Yeah."

"…Should I go too?"

"…No." I said. "I'll go alone today."

My brother nodded at that. No sign of hurt. Eight years old doesn't
hold onto hurt for long.

"…Tell Mom I said hi." my brother said.

*Hearing that, I smiled a little. Well-being. I heard the word I would
write on the form that day from my brother, before I thought of it
myself.*

---

I wrote well-being in the purpose box on the form.

Before, it had taken me forever to fill in that box. This time I didn't
hesitate. Well-being. That was the exact word. I wasn't going there
today to get anything.

The clerk took the form and looked at me.

"…You filled that in quickly today."

"…Yes."

I didn't explain further. The clerk didn't ask further either.

---

Sitting in the waiting room, I went back over what had happened the day
before.

Speon's words, the seventeen funerals, the hill when I was nine. I knew
all of it now. But I had no intention of laying it all out for my
mother today.

*I couldn't have put that resolve into exact words, at the time. I can
say it now. That day, instead of going to say something, I wanted to go
to listen to something.*

The clerk called my name.

---

My mother welcomed me as she always did.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes."

"…You've come again."

I knew a different answer to that greeting now. But I held that answer
back too. I just sat down.

My mother studied me for a moment.

"…Your expression is different again, today."

"…Different from yesterday too?"

"…Yes. Yesterday it was a face that had learned something. Today it's
just a face that's here."

*Hearing that, I felt all over again how long this person had been
watching me. Someone who notices that my face is different every time.
I'd taken that for granted, all this time. It wasn't a thing to take
for granted.*

---

I didn't talk about the valley, or about the water.

Instead I asked,

"…What did you do today?"

My mother paused at the question. Her face looked as though she'd been
asked something strange.

"…The same as always." she said. "Getting up, sitting, waiting for
you."

"…Is waiting something you do?"

"…Well." My mother smiled. "I'm not sure about that."

I liked that answer. If it had come before, I would have found it sad.
Today I just liked it. It had been a long time since my mother had said
I'm not sure that lightly.

---

I passed on my brother's message.

"…My brother said to tell you hello."

"…So he knows how to use words like that." my mother smiled. "Who
taught him?"

"…Me, probably."

"…That's not a phrase you used to use either."

I couldn't answer that. It was true. I had almost never used the word
well-being, all this time. I'd never had anyone whose well-being I
needed to ask after.

---

We sat for a while without saying much.

The room was always the same. The walls, the chair, the position of the
window. My mother had sat in that same spot in that room for seven
years now. With a face that was neither in pain nor bored. I wasn't
unfamiliar with that face anymore.

The silence wasn't uncomfortable. That struck me as strange. For seven
years, in this room, silence had always been a blank I had to fill.
Today it wasn't.

*I still remember that silence exactly. Inside it, for the first time,
I was at ease with nothing prepared. That I could be here even with no
words ready, I learned that day, for the first time.*

Through the window came the faint sound of water. The ditch Speon had
dug had reached this far by now. My mother listened to it for a moment.

"…That sound, it's often here lately." she said.

"…Yes. It keeps spreading."

"…There was a sound like that when I was young, too." my mother said.
"The well-bucket sound. Do you remember?"

I stopped at that. It had been a long time since my mother had brought
up her own childhood too.

"…I remember." I said. "My brother was scared of that sound."

"…That's right. And you used to let the rope down slowly on purpose."

"…That was a mean thing to do."

My mother laughed at that.

"…But he liked it later, didn't he."

"…Because when that sound came, it meant I was there."

Saying that, I surprised myself a little. I'd thought I had realized
that a long time ago, alone. And now here I was, saying it to my mother.

---

My mother looked at me again. That look in her eyes must have been the
difference the Curator had mentioned yesterday. I still couldn't have
named it, at the time.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes."

My mother was quiet for a moment. Her face was one of choosing
something. That was a face I'd never seen before, too. This person
always answered right away. She never chose her words.

*I can still count that brief silence, even now. It wasn't long. A few
breaths' worth. But seven years fit inside those few breaths.*

---

"…How have you been?"

I couldn't say anything at all, hearing that.

My mother was looking at me. Not a face asking a question back at me.
A face wanting to know.

*In seven years I had come to this room more than thirty-two times.
In all those thirty-two visits, my mother had never once asked me that.
If I asked why, she asked why back. If I said I missed you, she said
me too. She always handed back whatever I'd said, unchanged. But not
today. Today she asked, herself.*

---

"…I've been fine." I managed to say.

My voice came out strangely. I didn't know why, at the time.

"…Liar." my mother said. Not an angry tone.

"…What?"

"…I can tell from your face. That's not the face of someone who's been
fine."

I didn't know how to answer that. My mother didn't press for an answer.
She just waited.

---

"…It's been hard." I finally said. "A lot."

"…How."

Faced with that question, for the first time, I wanted to say it as it
was, not summarize seven years.

"…I was alone." I said. "That was the hardest part. Watching people
die was hard too, but harder than that was always having to walk home
alone afterward."

My mother listened to that quietly. She said nothing. But this time the
silence wasn't a blank to be filled. It was just a listening silence.

"…I learned to dig wells too." I went on. "At fourteen. From a
quiet old man. He stopped too. I started counting from his funeral, the
ninth. It's seventeen, up to now."

"…Seventeen." My mother repeated the number. "You've been counting all
of that?"

"…Yes. Counting became a habit."

"…So that's how you endured the hard parts." my mother said. "By
counting."

I couldn't answer that at all. It was the first time my mother had seen
my counting habit as a way of enduring. I had never called it that
myself, even to myself.

---

"…I'm sorry." my mother said, after a long while.

That surprised me.

"…For what."

"…For leaving you alone."

"…It's not your fault. You weren't sick."

"…Even so." my mother said. "Sorry is sorry."

*I held onto that sentence for a long time. I had never once seen this
person apologize, all this time. The preserved don't feel regret. They
have no lack to regret. But my mother apologized, that day.*

I didn't know how to answer that, so I just fiddled with the flask.

"…What about your brother." my mother asked. "Was it hard for him
too?"

"…He was younger than me, so he probably didn't understand much."

"…No." my mother said. "Even an eight-year-old understands. He's just
pretending not to."

Hearing that, I remembered my brother waiting for me in the yard. Tell
her I said hi. I felt, for the first time, that I understood a little
of what had been inside those words.

---

Near the end of visiting hours, my mother asked again,

"…So, how are you, now?"

"…Now?"

"…Yes. Today. This moment."

I thought about that for a moment.

"…I'm all right." I said. "Because I'm here."

Saying that, I felt, this time, that it was really true. It wasn't
words I'd prepared, and it wasn't said to reassure my mother. It was
just exactly what I felt in that moment.

My mother smiled at that. Not a large smile. But I could tell that
smile was different from before. Before, the smile had simply been
there. This time it had come from something.

---

Standing to leave, I gathered the flask back up.

"…I'll come again."

"…All right." my mother said. Then she added, "Tell me more about
yourself, next time."

I stopped at that.

*Tell me more about yourself, next time. I repeated that sentence to
myself again and again, walking out the door. This person had never
asked anything of me in seven years. She'd had no reason to ask. But
today, for the first time, she asked.*

At the door I turned back to look at my mother once more. She had
already turned her eyes to the window. Toward where the sound of water
was coming from.

*I remember that back for a long time. The face looking out the window.
I had never once, all this time, seen my mother look out a window that
long. She'd had no reason to look. But that day she was looking at
something.*

---

I ran into the Curator in the hallway. This time he didn't ask first.
He just saw me and nodded.

"…My mother asked me something today." I said first.

The Curator stopped walking.

"…What."

"…How I'd been."

He said nothing at all for a long while, hearing that. I saw something
surging, large, inside that silence.

"…I've never seen a record like that." he finally said. "I've done
this work a long time. This is the first time I've ever heard of one of
the preserved asking something, first."

"…What does this mean?" I asked again.

The Curator closed his eyes for a moment, then opened them. A face
choosing an answer.

"…I don't know exactly, myself." he said. "But if there's one thing
I've learned doing this work, it's that asking something means not
knowing something, and not knowing is something that doesn't happen to
the people here. That's the whole of what I know."

He left it at that and adjusted his grip on the papers. I felt that he
wanted to say more. He didn't.

---

I walked to the edge of the city, carrying those words with me.

The sound of the channel was running. I sat there, and went back over
my mother's question again. How have you been.

My brother was waiting for me. Crouched at the edge of the yard,
watching the channel.

"…Did you tell her?" my brother asked.

"…Yeah."

"…What did Mom say?"

I thought for a moment about how to answer.

"…She asked." I said. "How I'd been."

My brother tilted his head at that.

"…Doesn't she usually not ask that?"

"…Right. She usually doesn't."

My brother's face didn't understand why that was special. I started to
explain, then gave it up. It wasn't something I could explain to an
eight-year-old.

"…Should I ask you too?" my brother said. "You."

That made me laugh.

"…Sure. Go ahead."

"…How was your day, brother?"

"…It was good." I said. Not a lie.

My brother nodded, satisfied with that answer, and went back to
watching the channel. I sat beside him for a while.

---

That evening I told Speon about it.

He stopped mid-motion, putting the shovels away.

"…She asked, you said." he asked, as if to confirm.

"…Yes. For the first time."

Speon said nothing for a long while. Then, looking toward the ditch, he
said,

"…It's like this too, when water first starts moving underground. No
sound at all. Just a very small change of direction."

I started to ask exactly what he meant, then didn't. I felt I already
knew, without asking.

"…So what comes next." I asked.

"…I don't know that either." Speon said. "A change of direction
doesn't necessarily mean it makes it all the way here. But if the
direction doesn't change, it never comes at all."

He shouldered the shovel again.

"…Let's stop here for today." he said. "You should rest too."

---

*I write down that question the most carefully of anything in this
record. I didn't fully understand, that day, what it meant. I only
knew: to be curious about something means something is lacking. And my
mother, for the first time in seven years, had come to know that there
was something about me she didn't know.*

*That night I tried not to think about it any further than that. If I
let it weigh too heavily, it felt like it might break. I decided just
to hold the question, quietly.*
