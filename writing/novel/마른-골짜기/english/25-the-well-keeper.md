# Chapter 25. The Well-Keeper

> _Previously_ — an unfamiliar sound came from outside the city. I knew it
> at once, because I'd grown up outside. The sound of a bucket going down,
> a rope paying out, and at the end, a splash. Someone was drawing water.
> This city had no well.

I followed the sound.

I left the dormitory and walked east. In this city, you almost never have
to follow a sound while you walk. There's so little sound to begin with.
Footsteps, sometimes a door opening and closing — that's all. So an
unfamiliar sound was easy to track, even from far away.

On the way I passed three people. All walking the same direction. No one
hurried, and no one looked at me. I noticed that none of the three so
much as turned toward the sound. They must have heard it. In a city this
quiet, there was no way they hadn't.

*I thought it was strange, at the time. I understood later. It wasn't
strange. People in this city simply don't listen to sounds they don't
need. Not that they can't hear them — they choose not to. It took me a
while to see the difference.*

It was at the eastern edge of the city, well past even the distribution
station. There had originally been nothing there. I'd passed that stretch
of ground on my first day here. Dry soil, a few weeds — that was all.

Now there was a pit.

About waist-deep on me. Wide enough that two people standing across from
each other would touch shoulders. Beside it, the dug-out earth was piled
like a small mountain, and a bucket and rope hung over the mound.

The water flask at my waist swung against my hip. My uncle had given it
to me before he left. "No water there, they say." I hadn't known what
that meant, then. I only understood once I reached the city. There really
was no well. Only the distribution station.

I had never once emptied that flask all the way. I'd drink half, then
refill it at the station. But in that moment, for the first time, the
flask felt heavy. I didn't know why.

There was a man in the pit.

Tall, broad-shouldered. Old sun on his forearms. Even from a distance I
could see the thick calluses on the hand gripping the shovel. His clothes
weren't the clothes of this city. The color had faded, and the knees had
been patched twice over.

I stood there a long while, watching.

He gave no sign of having seen me and kept digging. Drive the shovel in,
step on it, lift the earth, throw it up. There was a rhythm to it.
Unhurried, but never stopping.

*I know that rhythm. I learned it at fourteen. There are two kinds of
digging. Digging in a hurry, and digging built to last. The hurried kind
wears out in an hour. The lasting kind goes all day. This man was the
lasting kind.*

"…If you're just going to stand there watching, pour some water."

The voice came up out of the pit. It took me a beat to realize he was
talking to me.

"…What?"

"You've got a flask. That one."

He meant the flask at my waist. I was the only person in this city who
carried one around. The preserved didn't need water, and the living took
what they needed from the station, as they needed it. I was the only one
who kept a flask on me all the time.

"…I'm going to drink this."

"…I know. Just give me a little. The dirt's too dry to dig."

I unhooked the flask and went to the edge of the pit. The man set his
shovel down for a moment and held out his hand. I passed it over. He
poured half of it onto the dirt floor and struck the spot with the shovel
a few times. I watched the earth go soft.

"…Thanks." He handed the flask back. "Speon."

"…Anemos."

"…Anemos." He repeated the name once. Then he picked the shovel back up.
"Wind."

I didn't answer that. It was the first time I'd met someone who knew what
my name meant. No one in this city had ever asked. They had no reason to.

*I let that one line pass by, at the time. I understood later. Speon
wasn't a man who said what a name meant carelessly. When he said it,
there was always a reason. It was only much later that he told me what
the reason was.*

---

I kept standing there.

I looked down into the pit. The soil was red on top and gray farther
down. I knew what that meant — the old man had taught me. Red earth is
the dry layer, gray earth is the trace of where moisture once passed
through. Gray means water crossed this ground at some point.

"…Has water gone through here before?" I asked.

Speon looked up at me, still holding the shovel. For the first time his
face showed a little surprise.

"…How'd you know that."

"…The color of the dirt."

"…Someone taught you."

"…Yes. An old man." I paused, then added. "He's gone now."

Speon nodded. He didn't say anything comforting. He just nodded. I found
that easier, somehow. In this city, people didn't know how to say
comforting things anymore. There was nothing left to be sad about. Speon's
nod was better than any comforting word.

*I wrote earlier that I counted that old man's funeral as the ninth of
seventeen. I'll set it down again here. He died in winter. On the last
day he called me over and had me hold his hand. A man who rarely spoke
said a little more that day. "…One of the wells you dig is sure to come
up dry." "…Why?" "…So you'll have a reason to dig the next one." I didn't
understand it then. I thought success every time would only be better. I
know now. A person who only succeeds stops. You need one failure mixed
in to keep digging.*

"…There was a water channel down here." Speon started digging again as
he said it. "It got blocked, a long time ago. Earth built up."

"…Why did it get blocked?"

"…Because no one used it. Unused things get blocked. People and water
channels are the same that way."

"…There's no well here," I said.

"…I know. That's why I'm digging."

"…Did someone ask you to?"

Speon stopped digging and looked up at me. His eyes were a light brown.
His face showed no irritation at all.

"…No."

"…Then why dig?"

He drove the shovel into the earth pile. Then he straightened his back.
It made a sound. It meant he'd been bent over a long time.

"…Water that just sits rots." He said it. "It has to flow to stay
alive."

I didn't understand what he meant. This city didn't even have anywhere
for water to sit still in the first place. There was no well. And yet
Speon spoke as if he were talking about water that was already sitting
somewhere, stagnant.

"…This city doesn't have water," I said. "There's nothing to sit still."

"…There is." Speon said. "It's not only water that's missing."

Then he picked the shovel back up. He didn't explain any further. I
didn't fully understand what he meant, not that day.

*I know now. The water he meant wasn't well water. This whole city was
standing water. People sitting exactly as they were seven years ago.
Things that never changed. That was what Speon was talking about, and I
was still listening as if it were only about a real well.*

---

I started counting the mounds of dirt.

Each swing of the shovel brought up one heap. I have a habit of counting
things. Somewhere around the hundredth swing, I asked.

"…Have you dug a lot of these?"

"…A lot."

"…How many?"

Speon's face went thoughtful for a moment. He didn't look like a man who
kept count. And yet his answer was exact.

"…Thirty-seven."

"…Did they all work?"

"…No. Thirty-one came up with water. Six didn't."

"…What about the ones that didn't?"

"…Left them as they were. Thought about filling them back in, but I
didn't."

"…Why not?"

"…Someone might come dig further, someday. Water I couldn't find, someone
else might."

I liked that answer. A man who admitted failure without sealing it back
up afterward. I'd seen very few people like that. In this city, failed
things went down into the valley. They weren't left standing so someone
could try again.

"…Where are you from?" I asked.

"…Here and there." Speon gave a short laugh. "Once, in some city, I saw
a bridge that only had half of itself built. I had three friends then,
and one of them helped finish that bridge."

"…And now?"

"…Scattered. Everyone went their own way. I'm a well-digger, so I keep
moving."

*I didn't ask who those three friends were, that day. I had no reason
to. It sounded to me like an ordinary old story of Speon's, and I still
didn't know then what he'd done before he came to this city. Later I
would hear that bridge story again. That belongs to the last pages of
this record.*

---

Still, I couldn't make myself leave. I wasn't sure why, even to myself. I
just liked the sound of the digging. Earth being lifted, the occasional
knock against a stone, the low sound of Speon's breath going out. It
wasn't the sound of water, but it was a sound for water's sake.

"…How many people have come?" I asked. "To watch you dig here."

"…You're the first."

"…No one's come at all?"

"…A few passing by. They looked, then moved on."

"…Didn't they say anything?"

Speon lifted a shovelful of earth as he answered.

"…Nothing to say, I guess. No need for it."

I heard that and looked back toward the city. Far off I could see the
Curator's building. People walked by, quietly. That was how everyone in
this city moved, always — quiet, unhurried, only as much as was needed.

*Every time I look back on this moment, I feel a strange unease. I
expected the city to get angry. A well was, after all, a thing this city
had declared it didn't need. But no one got angry. That was somehow
heavier. If they'd been angry, there would have been something to fight.
With no anger, there was nothing to fight at all.*

---

The sun began to set. Speon kept digging.

"…What about food?" I asked.

"…I've got some." He tipped his chin toward a small sack beside the
earth pile. "Dried stuff."

"…Water?"

"…I'll have to take a bit more off your flask."

I laughed. It was the first time I'd laughed since coming to this city.
It lasted a few seconds. But it was a real laugh.

*I counted those few seconds afterward. In seven years I never counted
the days I laughed. There was nothing much to count. Coming to this city
gave me a reason to count — laughter had grown rare. I wrote earlier that
counting had become a habit of mine; from that day on, laughter became
one more thing I counted.*

I unhooked the flask again and handed it over. Speon drank a little and
gave it back.

"…Will you be back tomorrow too?" I asked.

"…I'm going to be here the whole time."

"…How long is that?"

"…Until the water comes."

"…When's that?"

Speon pressed his palm flat against the dirt floor. Then he closed his
eyes for a moment. He looked like someone listening for something. I
didn't know, at the time, what that gesture was.

"…Don't know." He opened his eyes. "Water comes out when it comes out."

"…You can't rush it?"

"…Rush it and all you get is a collapse."

I heard that and looked at the walls of the pit. They really were packed
firm and solid. The mark of digging that hadn't been rushed.

I found myself wondering again about the gesture I'd seen earlier.

"…You put your hand down and closed your eyes before. What was that?"

"…Listening."

"…To what?"

"…Where the water wants to go."

I didn't know what that meant, so I stayed quiet. Speon motioned for me
to put my own hand down and try. I crouched and did it. The dirt was just
dirt. Cold and hard.

"…I don't hear anything."

"…No one does, at first." Speon said. "It's not listening, really. It's
waiting. If you keep your hand there long enough, the earth forgets what
it's been hiding. Then you hear a little."

I couldn't say I understood that. But strangely, the words frightened me.
Waiting until the earth forgot what it was hiding — somehow, that sounded
like this city's story.

*I didn't see the connection then. This city, too, had been hiding
something for a long time. It wasn't that the people wanted nothing —
they had forgotten how to want. What Speon said to the earth, I would
later hear him say to the whole city.*

---

That day I didn't go back to the city. I stayed sitting beside him a
while longer.

Speon didn't say anything more to me. He just kept digging. I watched. It
had been a long time since I'd had anything to watch. There wasn't much
worth watching in this city. Everyone moved exactly the same way.

*Walking back to my assigned room that night, I remembered something my
mother once said to me. When my brother was afraid of the sound of
water, she told me, "Getting used to something and not being afraid of
it aren't the same thing." I wanted to hand that line back to Speon that
day. This city had gotten too used to living without a well. But that
didn't mean it didn't need water.*

*I didn't say it that day. I couldn't say it the next day either. It
would be a long time before I could say it at all.*

On my way back to the dormitory I looked back three times. The sound of
digging still came from the pit. It was growing dark, and still Speon
didn't stop.

Listening to that sound, I had a thought for the first time.

*There's someone in this city who keeps going, even when he's told he
isn't needed.*

That thought stayed in my head all that night. I still didn't know what
it meant. I only held onto it, for a long time.

*This was around my twenty-third morning in the city. Every day since
I'd arrived, I'd counted something — the number of visits, the time I'd
spent standing at the window, the count of sentences my mother had said
identically. But that night, for the first time, I counted something
else. I couldn't count how many times Speon swung the shovel, but I
counted exactly how many times I laughed. Once.*

*One. But that one was a different kind of number from the ones I'd
counted before. Not a number that only went down. A number that could go
up.*

Just before the sun finished setting, one person passed by.

A girl. A year younger than me, walking slowly — the same unhurried walk
everyone in this city had. I knew her face.

It was Eikon.

She stopped for a moment in front of the pit. She looked at Speon, then
at me, then back at the pit. She said nothing. Her face showed almost
nothing. Not surprised, not curious — just a face that was looking.

I thought she'd say something. But she said nothing at all and started
walking again. A few steps on, she turned back exactly once.

*I remember that retreating figure for a long time afterward. Not
surprised, not dismissive. Just the back of someone who had looked at
something that had nothing to do with her. I didn't know it that day, but
that would become how this whole city treated Speon. Not angry, not
stopping him — just regarding him as something that had nothing to do
with them.*

"…Someone you know?" Speon asked.

"…Yes. Fifteen years old. Preserved seven years ago."

Speon drove the shovel in once more.

"…This city must have a lot of people like that."

"…Yes."

"…Does everyone look at it like that?"

I couldn't answer right away. Thinking about it, it really was true. No
one got angry, no one was curious. Everyone gave it exactly that much
attention — the attention you'd give something that had nothing to do
with you.

"…Yes. Everyone's like that."

Speon heard that and paused his hand for a moment. Then, as if it were
nothing much, he went back to lifting earth.

"…Guess I'd better keep digging, then." He said it. He didn't explain
why. I didn't know the weight of that line yet, either.

Once the sun had fully set, stars came out. Almost no one in this city
looks at the stars. The preserved don't go out at night without reason,
and the living go to sleep at their set hour. But that night I sat by the
pit and looked at the stars for a long time.

Outside, I'd looked at stars every night. The old man taught me
directions by the constellations. In the settlement, that had been
useful knowledge. In this city, it was useless. No one here ever needed
to find their direction at night. Wherever you went, the path was
already set.

*That night I found myself thinking, after a long while, about useless
knowledge. The names of constellations, the knots the old man taught me,
how to read the color of soil. Since coming to this city I'd thought all
of it had become useless. Sitting beside Speon that night, I felt for the
first time that it wasn't that. It hadn't become useless — it had simply
had nowhere to be used. Speon was making a place for it to be used
again.*

---

The next morning I went back to that spot. On the way, I didn't prepare
any words to explain why I was going. I couldn't remember the last time
I'd gone somewhere that didn't require me to have words ready.

The pit was a little deeper than the day before. Speon was already down
inside it. He saw me and, without any greeting, said this.

"…Flask."

I laughed and unhooked it.

That day I went by way of the distribution station. In front of it, as
always, a short line stood waiting. Only the living stand in that line.
Standing in it, I had a strange thought for the first time. The station
hands out water. Exactly as much as is needed. A well doesn't hand
anything out. It just comes up. Whoever needs it draws it themselves.

*I thought about that difference for the first time that day. Rationing
is handing out something fixed. A well is simply something that's there.
One is given by whoever manages it; the other is given by the ground
itself, for nothing. This city only had the first kind. Speon had brought
the second.*

The station attendant looked at my flask and asked.

"…Refilling again?"

"…Yes."

"…You filled it yesterday too."

"…I shared it."

The attendant tilted his head. His face said he didn't understand what
sharing a portion meant. Here, no one ever had reason to give their share
to someone else. Everyone needed only exactly their own.

I took the water and walked back toward the pit. On the way I passed the
spot where Eikon had turned back the day before. No one was there. Only
a couple of footprints left in the dirt.

I looked at those footprints a moment. Then I kept walking.

When I reached the pit, Speon was already down halfway again. The depth
had grown by a hand's width since the day before. I sat down beside it
and checked the color of the soil. Still red. Gray was still a long way
off.

"…How much more do you have to dig?" I asked.

"…Don't know." Speon said. "Until the red earth ends."

"…How much is that?"

"…You find out by digging."

I liked that answer better than any answer I'd heard in this city. This
city knew everything in advance. Visiting hours, ration amounts,
procedures. There wasn't a single thing done without already knowing how
it would turn out. Speon was doing this without knowing. And even so, he
never wavered.

*I couldn't put that difference into words then. I can now. This city
moves knowing the outcome already. Since the outcome is fixed, there's
nothing new to learn. Speon moves without knowing the outcome. So every
day is a day of learning something new. I wanted to stay beside that
kind of day.*
