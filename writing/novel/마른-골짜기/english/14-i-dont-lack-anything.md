# Chapter 14. I Don't Lack Anything

> _Previously_ — I met Eikon in the plaza. Preserved at fifteen seven
> years ago, she was still fifteen. When I asked if she wasn't curious
> about the taste of water, she said it was just the taste of water.

Three days later I met her again.

In those three days I hadn't done much of anything. I went back and
forth between the lodging, the ration station, and the plaza. I walked
past the window twice, but I didn't go in. The third application, sitting
in that drawer, kept pulling at me, but I couldn't work up the nerve to
ask for it back.

*I can count those three days. It was exactly three. But what I
thought about during them, I don't remember well. I think I was
probably trying not to think about anything at all. Trying doesn't
make a thing so, though.*

I hadn't gone looking for her. I was sitting in the same plaza, on the
same bench, and Eikon came to me first. She walked up as though she'd
known I'd be there.

"…Carrying the flask again."

"…Yes."

"…Where are you off to today?"

I had nowhere in particular to go. I hadn't gone back to see my mother
and my brother since the second visit. The third application was
still sitting in the window's drawer.

"…I was just walking."

"…Want to come with me? I've got something I'm doing today."

I went along without asking what it was.

---

What Eikon did wasn't much of anything. More precisely, I wasn't even
sure I could call it doing something.

We passed a garden first — a place inside the city planted with
flowers and trees. Eikon met two people she knew there and talked a
while.

One was an older-looking woman. Looked older was the accurate way to
put it. Since she was preserved she couldn't actually have aged, but
her face gave that impression. The other was a boy who looked younger
than me.

"…This is Anemos. He's from outside." Eikon introduced me.

The woman looked at me and bowed her head slightly.

"…Here for a visit?"

"…Yes."

"…How lovely for you."

There was no malice in it. She meant it, honestly — that it would be
lovely. I didn't know what to say, so I just nodded.

*I would hear that phrase a few more times after that day. How lovely
for you. That there was someone to visit — that there was still,
even, someone waiting — was a thing people in that city envied. I
hadn't known it was something to envy.*

The boy didn't say much, just waved and went on. Eikon watched his
back and said,

"…He's never had much to say."

"…Are you close?"

"…Yes. I've known him a long time."

"…How long?"

"…I don't know. Five years, maybe?"

I was a little startled by how easy the answer was. It was the first
time I'd heard someone start a sentence about a five-year friend with
"I don't know."

After that we went somewhere like a library. There were a lot of
books. Eikon pulled one out, flipped through a few pages, and put it
back.

"…Going to read it?" I asked.

"…No. Just looked."

"…Not interesting?"

"…It's not that it's not interesting. I just won't read it today."

After that we just walked. It wasn't a walk with a destination. When
there was a road, we walked it; when there was a bench, we sat; then
we walked again.

While walking we passed a fountain. Watching the water shoot up and
fall, Eikon paused a moment.

"…Do you like that?" I asked.

"…Yes. It's nice to look at."

"…So you watch it every day?"

"…Often."

"…Doesn't it get old? Watching the same thing every day."

"…It's the same water, but the shape's different every time. Look."

I looked at the fountain again after she said that. It really was.
The water shot up in exactly the same direction each time, but the
shape it broke into was slightly different every time. I'd never
noticed that before.

*I don't forget that moment. It was the first time I felt, concretely,
that the way she saw the world was different from mine. I saw
repetition as tedium. She found something different, every time, even
inside the repetition. Which of us is right, I still don't know.*

*I counted how many places we'd passed that day. One garden, one
library, two fountains, five benches. But not one of them had been a
destination. I'd never before seen a person walk for five hours with
no destination.*

---

"…Aren't you bored?" I finally asked.

Eikon stopped walking and looked at me.

"…By what?"

"…This. Living like this every day."

"…It's fun, though."

"…What's fun about it?"

Eikon thought for a moment. It wasn't a face avoiding the answer. It
was a face genuinely searching for one.

"…Walking. Meeting people. Looking at books. All of it."

The answer was so simple I found it hard to believe.

"…But it's the same every day, isn't it? Was yesterday like this
too?"

"…Yes."

"…The day before too?"

"…Yes."

"…All seven years?"

This time Eikon actually laughed.

"…I don't remember every single day of the seven years, but they were
probably about like this."

---

I turned the answer over as we walked. Outside, every day had been
different. One day the well ran dry, one day it rained. One day
someone died, one day someone was born. If a day wasn't different, it
hardly felt like being alive.

There was none of that here. And yet Eikon wasn't bored.

"…Have you never wanted to do something new?" I asked.

"…Something new?"

"…Like, wanting to try something today you didn't do yesterday."

I could see Eikon taking the question seriously. She seemed to have
caught that I wasn't asking to tease her.

"…Not really. There's plenty to do today — why look for something
else?"

"…Just, out of tedium, I mean. Usually."

"…You'd need something tedious first, to be tedious."

I stopped at that. You'd need something tedious first, to be tedious.
It sounded like it didn't add up, and yet it was strangely exact.

*That day, for the first time, I felt in my body that time moved
differently for people in this city than it did for me. My time was
headed somewhere. The next visit, the next decision, the next day.
Eikon's time went nowhere. It just was.*

---

We sat again on a garden bench. The sun had passed noon.

"…What do you lack, Eikon?" I asked.

This time I wanted to ask more directly. The earlier questions hadn't
gotten me an answer.

Eikon gave the question a truly thoughtful look. She thought about it
a long time. I took that silence as a silence that was searching for
something.

"…I don't think there's anything."

"…Really?"

"…Yes. I thought about it, and there isn't."

"…Friends? Isn't there anyone you'd like to see more?"

"…If I want to see someone, I can just go see them."

"…What about someone you can't see?"

"…Is there such a person? Here?"

I was stopped short by the counter-question. There wasn't. Inside this
city, anyone you wanted to see, you could see. Not being able to see
someone was a thing that only happened to a person like me, from
outside.

"…What about your parents? Don't you ever think you'd like to live
longer, or stay with them longer? Nothing like that?"

"…If I want to be with them, I go see them. I'm not sure what it would
even mean to want to live longer here."

"…Growing up. Becoming an adult. Aren't you curious about that at
all?"

For the first time, Eikon looked a little amused.

"…What's good about becoming an adult?"

I couldn't answer. I genuinely didn't know. I'd been taught it was
good, but I'd never thought about why. All that came to mind was
getting taller, getting stronger, being able to decide more things on
your own. But trying to explain why that was good, I found myself at
a loss.

"…See?" Eikon said. "Even you don't know, Anemos. So why should I be
curious about it?"

*I couldn't argue with that. I still can't. Why becoming an adult is
good — I didn't know then, and I don't know exactly now. I've only
lived believing that it was. Eikon didn't hold that belief at all.*

---

"…Aren't you lonely?" I asked. It seemed like it might be more precise
than saying stifled.

"…Why would I be lonely?"

"…You have time alone too, don't you?"

"…Is being alone the same as being lonely?"

I was stopped short by that question for a moment. They weren't the
same. I'd had a lot of time alone too, but at some point I'd started
to feel lonely. I'd never separated the two, so I couldn't find an
answer.

"…I don't think they're the same."

"…Then why'd you ask me?"

"…Just, I've seen people sitting alone on benches when there aren't
many people around here. They looked lonely."

Eikon thought about that for a moment, then shook her head.

"…They're alone because they want to be. If they didn't want to be
alone, they wouldn't be."

"…Does it always go the way you want it to?"

"…Yes. Here it does."

I felt something like a wall in that answer. It wasn't a wall meant to
pick a fight. She was simply stating a fact as it was, but to me that
fact felt like a wall.

*That day, for the first time, I thought it might be possible for
there to be no lonely people in this city at all. Loneliness is born
in the gap between what you want and what you have. A person with no
such gap has no room in them for loneliness to grow.*

"…Doesn't it feel stifling?" I asked again. This time with a different
word.

Eikon looked straight at me. There was no defensiveness in her face.
It was a face that was truly curious.

"…By what?"

I couldn't answer.

If she asked me back what exactly was stifling, I didn't know what
I'd say. She wasn't shut in a small room. No one had locked her away.
She went wherever she wanted, as much as she wanted. She saw whoever
she wanted, whenever she wanted. She wasn't enduring anything.

*I realized, in that moment, that I myself didn't know what I'd been
trying to ask. Stifling is a word you use when something is blocked.
For her, nothing was blocked. Asking someone with nothing blocked
whether they felt stifled was a question that didn't even hold
together as a question.*

"…Never mind." I finally said. "I was just asking."

Eikon didn't press further. She just nodded and looked back up at the
sky.

After a long while I spoke again.

"…When I was outside, something was short every single day. Water was
short, and even sleeping I always wanted something more. It was hard.
But thinking back on it now, I think that was what kept me moving."

"…So lacking something is good?"

"…Not that it's good. Just, that it's what made me do things. Going
to draw water, learning a trade. I moved because I lacked."

Eikon seemed to take that seriously. Then she said,

"…So I don't need to move, then."

There was no sarcasm at all in that. It was simply a conclusion. I
couldn't find anything else to say in front of that conclusion.

*I would bring that conversation back up again, even years later. I
wanted to tell her she was wrong. But I couldn't find where, not then
and not fully even now. I had no grounds to tell someone with no
reason to move that they should move.*

---

On the way back to the lodging I made that question over again,
several times. Should I have asked what she lacked? Should I have
asked what she missed? Whatever word I put in, it felt like the same
answer would come back.

By what.

*I would go on remembering those two words for a long time. In writing
this book, it's a line I return to again and again. She wasn't asking
to deceive me. She genuinely didn't know, and that's why she asked.
And that was what unsettled me longest of all.*

I filled my flask passing the ration station. The clerk asked,

"…Did you walk a lot today?"

"…Yes."

"…Must have been tiring."

I didn't answer that. I wasn't sure whether it had been tiring or not.
My body was fine, but something kept pulling at me, and my steps felt
heavy.

On the way to the lodging I passed the preservation building where my
mother and brother were. I hadn't taken that road on purpose. It was
just the road I always walked.

I stood in front of the building and looked up at the window for a
moment. I didn't even know which window. I was always shown to the
same visiting room, but I'd never thought about where in the building
that room actually was.

*That day, in front of that building, I asked this question for the
first time. Did my mother, too, lack nothing, the way Eikon did? Was
she not bored, not lonely, with nothing left to want? After I asked
that question, I stood there for a long while.*

Back at the lodging, I opened and closed the flask's cap. That day I
did it maybe thirty times. Even counting was no use. My thoughts kept
going back to that same spot.

By what.

Out the window I could see the city's lights. All the same brightness.
Not a single one flickered. Outside, lanterns flickered often — from
the wind, from running out of oil, from a wick burning short. The
lights here were steady, as if they had no such reasons to falter.

Watching that steadiness, I thought of Eikon. She was like that light,
too. Not flickering because there was no reason to flicker.

*I slept badly that night. That was strange — nothing had happened
that day that should have been especially hard. I understood it
later. I'd been searching, inside her answers, for something missing,
and no matter how I searched, I couldn't find it, and that was why I
couldn't sleep. That nothing was missing — that itself was what kept
me awake.*
