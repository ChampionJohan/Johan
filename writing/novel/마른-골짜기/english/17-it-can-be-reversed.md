# Chapter 17. It Can Be Reversed

> _Previously_ — I asked the Curator what release was. He warned me
> that once I heard the explanation, I couldn't go back to not having
> heard it. I said I wanted to hear it anyway, and the Curator began
> to speak.

The light in the room had turned a little more yellow than before.
It was the only proof that time was passing.

The Curator took his hand off the folder. And looked at me directly.

"…Release is possible."

He said that first. I held my breath and listened.

"…The procedure isn't difficult either. You fill out the form, wait a
few days, and undergo the operation. Nothing about it is blocked."

I read that sentence over to myself, several times. Possible. Not
difficult. Nothing blocked.

"…Really?" I asked. My voice shook.

"…Yes."

---

I nearly stood up out of my seat. Something inside me came loose all
at once. For seven years I hadn't known any way to "wake" my mother
and my brother — and there was a way. And not one that was blocked,
not one with a difficult procedure either.

In that moment I felt like I was back at nine years old. That day
I'd sat behind the hill until the sun went down. Back then there had
been nothing I could do. But now it was different. There was
something I could do. And it wasn't even hard.

My grip tightened on the flask. My hand opened and closed without my
noticing. The habit of counting something didn't come this time.
There was nothing to count. There was only the feeling that
everything was already settled.

*I didn't know, then, exactly what to call this feeling. I know now.
It was hope. The first hope I'd felt in a concrete shape in seven
years. And how thin that hope was, I wouldn't know until I sat in
this same room again, a few days later.*

"…So I can apply right away, today?"

"…The application itself, yes, that's possible."

The Curator's tone was strangely careful. I didn't notice that
carefulness at the time. I was too happy.

"…What's the procedure? Where do I get the form?"

"…Wait a moment, Anemos."

The Curator raised a hand to stop me.

"…Before I go over the procedure, there's something you need to know
first."

---

I stopped short at that. Something that had just come loose tightened
back up again.

"…What is it?"

"…You need to know exactly what release reverses, first."

"…Isn't it reversing preservation? That's what you just said."

"…That's right. But what that exactly means — everyone gets it wrong,
at first."

I didn't know what that meant. Reversing something. Going back to a
state without preservation. I didn't understand what was complicated
about that.

"…A preserved person, in their current state, speaks, remembers,
recognizes you, Anemos." The Curator said. "That's true. It isn't a
lie."

"…Yes. I know."

"…Release takes that person out of that state. Back, into a living
body again."

---

"…That's a good thing." I said. "It's living again."

The Curator looked at me for a moment. There was something complicated
in his eyes. I couldn't tell whether it was sorrow or carefulness.

"…Yes. It's living again."

He said it while looking down at his hands. That gesture looked to me
like someone measuring something — how much to say, how far to go.

"…If my mother lives again," I said, "she'll eat beside me, sleep
beside me, grow older. That's what happens, isn't it?"

"…Yes. That's what happens."

"…That's what I wanted. That's exactly what I've wanted for seven
years."

The Curator nodded at that. Even nodding, his eyes didn't look away
from me. But what was in those eyes wasn't only agreement. I felt that
difference in the moment, but I couldn't put a name to it.

He said that much and stopped. I waited for what came next. But the
next thing didn't come right away.

"…But?" I pressed.

"…But I'll tell you that in the next chapter."

*I didn't find that sentence strange at the time. I thought he was
just tired and sparing with his words. Looking back now, the Curator
was, in that moment, being careful with me. I think he believed that
if he told me all of it at once, I wouldn't be able to bear it.*

---

Unable to stand the silence, I asked again.

"…Where do I get the application form, anyway?"

"…You can get it here. Only —"

"…Only what?"

The Curator opened a drawer and took out a single sheet. He didn't
hand it to me — he set it down in front of himself.

"…The application itself isn't difficult. Name, relationship,
subject's information. That's all there is to it."

I looked at the paper. It really did look simple. It had only a few
fields.

Name. Relationship to the subject. Subject's full name. Subject's
preservation number. And a single signature line at the bottom. That
was all.

I traced each field with my eyes, one at a time. Writing my own name
in the name field wouldn't be hard. In the relationship field I could
write "son." In the subject's name field I could write my mother's
name. And I thought I'd need to write one more of these, with my
brother's name in it.

*Looking at that form, I thought this for the first time. Could a
sheet of paper this short really hold the weight of seven years?
Looking back now it's a laughable question. The paper was never meant
to hold the weight. It was only a road it let something pass along.*

"…Is that all? I just write this?"

"…Procedurally, yes."

I let that phrase, procedurally, pass by unnoticed at the time. Later
I would learn it was the heaviest phrase in this whole chapter.

---

"…Why doesn't anyone do it?" I asked. "When it's this simple."

The Curator didn't answer that question right away. He smoothed the
edge of the form with his fingertip.

"…What's simple is the procedure. The decision isn't simple."

"…What decision?"

"…I'll tell you that later."

I felt a small stifling at that answer. But hope was bigger than that
stifling. The fact that there was a procedure, that it wasn't blocked,
was so large that I could hardly hear anything else.

*Writing this passage now, I want to grab that earlier self and shake
him. Why didn't I press harder? Why did I let "the decision isn't
simple" pass by like that? The answer is simple. I had just seen hope
for the first time in seven years, and hope makes a person hurry. It
doesn't make a person doubt. At the time, that seemed only natural.*

"…If the procedure is this simple," I asked again, "why is there no
one in this city who's been released? From what I've heard, anyway."

The Curator's expression showed a small surprise at that question.

"…It's not that there's no one. It's just extremely, extremely rare."

"…How rare?"

"…In all my time in this seat, you could count them on one hand."

I wanted to ask for the exact number, but I was afraid to ask. I
didn't know, at the time, where that fear came from. I know now. My
body already knew, before I did. That the number wasn't a good
number.

"…I want to write it today, even." I said.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes?"

"…Don't be in a hurry."

---

That struck me as strange. I'd waited seven years already. I thought
I had every right to hurry. This man didn't know my seven years. He
didn't know the three days I'd gone without drinking water, the
seventeen funerals, the promise I'd broken — "I'll come back later" —
and never kept.

But in that moment, I didn't say any of that out loud. I had a vague
sense that it wasn't because he didn't know those things that he was
telling me not to hurry.

"…Why shouldn't I hurry? If it's something that can be done, isn't
it better to do it quickly?"

The Curator looked at me for a moment, then said this.

"…This isn't the kind of thing that's better done quickly."

I didn't fully understand that, at the time. Not understanding it,
some corner of my mind kept holding on to the good news anyway.
Possible. Not difficult. Nothing blocked.

"…Have you seen a release, Curator?" I asked. "Actually seen one."

The Curator closed his eyes for a moment, then opened them.

"…I have. Several times."

"…What was it like?"

"…That's hard for me to say right now."

"…Why?"

"…Because you don't know the whole of it yet, Anemos. If I tell you
only part of it, you might misunderstand."

I felt a little stifled by that answer, but I didn't press further.
I knew he wasn't doing this to toy with me. No one in this city ever
lied. That concealing and lying were different things — I sensed that,
vaguely, in that moment.

The Curator held the form out to me. His fingertips lingered on it for
a moment.

"…You can take it with you. Just don't write on it today." The
Curator said.

"…Why not?"

"…Because it won't be too late to write it once you've heard the
rest."

I nodded, obediently, at that. I'd been told not to hurry again, but
in that moment it didn't bother me much. I took the form and tucked
it into my coat. Its weight was so light I could hardly feel it was
real.

*I was still smiling as I walked out of that room. The Curator never
once smiled with me. I didn't notice that difference at the time. I
should have. I didn't.*

---

"…Come back next time." The Curator said. "I'll tell you the rest
then."

"…What's the rest?"

"…What release truly means."

I nodded at that. I was curious, but for now, what I'd heard today
was already enough to make me happy. I stood and said my goodbye.

"…Thank you."

The Curator nodded. There was no smile on his face.

Walking out of the room, I looked at the copy of the application in my
hand. Name, relationship, subject's information. Looking at those
fields, I imagined writing my mother's name into them.

*I smiled the whole afternoon that day. I smiled at the ration-station
clerk, smiled greeting people I passed in the plaza. I thought I
should tell Eikon this news when I saw her. Thinking that, my steps
were unusually light that day.*

*That lightness disappears completely in the next chapter.*

---

I went straight to the garden from there. I looked for Eikon. Luckily
I found her sitting on a bench.

"…Eikon."

"…Your face is different today."

"…I have good news."

Eikon looked at me with an interested expression.

"…What is it?"

"…Release is possible. The procedure's simple too. The Curator told
me himself."

I said that expecting she'd be glad the way I was. But Eikon's
expression barely changed.

"…That's already known."

"…What?"

"…That it's possible. That's not new."

I felt the air go out of me all at once. To me it was news that
overturned the world. To her, it was something she'd already known
all along. That the same sentence could carry such different
temperatures felt strange to me.

"…Then why hasn't anyone told me?"

"…Because you never asked. People here don't tend to bring up things
that aren't needed."

---

The sun slanted in through the garden trees. In that light I looked
at Eikon's face again. There was no change in her expression. Not
joy, not surprise, not disappointment — just her usual face.

"…Aren't you glad about this news?" I asked.

"…Why would I be glad? It has nothing to do with me."

"…It's glad news for me."

"…I know. I can tell from your face."

She said that, looking at me closely. There was something like worry
mixed into her eyes. It was a look I hadn't seen before.

"…But I'm a little worried." Eikon said.

"…About what?"

"…The Curator didn't tell you everything, did he? Just the
procedure."

I was at a loss for words at that question. She was exactly right.

"…How did you know?"

"…He always does that. He never tells you everything at once."

"…Why?"

Eikon shrugged.

"…I don't know either. But he must have a reason for doing it that
way."

*I let that comment pass by unnoticed that day. It was only when I sat
in that room again a few days later that I learned the reason. And
once I knew the reason, I came to understand, too, why Eikon hadn't
been as happy as I was that day.*

Eikon pointed at the flask.

"…Give me a sip."

I opened the cap and held it out. She drank a sip and handed it back.

"…How does it taste?" I asked. The same question as last time.

"…Just like water."

"…Same answer as before."

"…Same water."

I laughed at that. She laughed too. That laugh was the easiest moment
between us that whole day.

---

On the way back to the lodging I took the copy of the application out
again. A fold had already formed in it, from being held too long in
my hand.

I imagined writing my own name into the name field. Son, in the
relationship field. My mother's name, in the subject's name field.
Just imagining it made my heart race.

*I slept well that night. It was the most comfortable night's sleep
I'd had since I came to that city. When I opened my eyes the next
morning, I felt like the world had changed. I didn't open and close
the flask's cap once that day. There was nothing to count, nothing to
bear. How short that feeling would turn out to be, I couldn't have
imagined then.*
