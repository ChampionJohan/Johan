# Chapter 36. It Runs Down to the Valley

> _Previously_ — Water came up in Speon's pit. People gathered. No one
> drank it. Only the distribution station attendant dipped in a
> fingertip. That night even the water in my own flask felt different.

The water cleared in three days.

I stopped by the pit every day through those three days. The first day
it was muddy. The second day it had settled a little. By the third day
it was clear enough to see the bottom. That day, for the first time,
Speon scooped up a mouthful and drank it.

"…How is it?" I asked.

"…Tastes like water," he said, laughing.

I tried a mouthful too. Cold, with a faint trace of earth still in it.
Close to the well water I used to drink outside. That familiarity
surprised me. Every drop of water I'd had since coming to this city had
passed through the distribution station. Exact, regulation amounts.
This water was just water. Untouched by anyone's hand.

I noticed his answer was exactly the same as Eikon's had been. Tastes
like water. But this time it sounded different. Eikon had said it
because there was nothing surprising in it. Speon said it though there
was. The same words held something different inside.

---

On the fourth day, Speon set up a small stone marker at the edge of the
pit.

"…What's that?" I asked.

"…I'm going to run the channel from here."

I watched him point his hand out. Not toward the city's inside. Toward
the outside of the city — more exactly, downward.

"…Where's it going?" I asked.

"…The valley."

I stopped for a moment at that.

---

"…Shouldn't it go toward the city first?" I asked. "The people are
here."

"…The city already has it," Speon said. "There's the distribution
station. Regulation amounts, but it's there."

"…But the valley has none at all."

Speon planted his shovel in the soil and looked at me. Something a
little mischievous showed in his eyes.

"…Well, there's your answer, then."

I liked that answer. But I still wanted to ask more.

"…Still, shouldn't the city come first, in order? It's closer."

Speon thought about that for a moment. Then he said this.

"…Up there isn't dry."

---

I didn't understand that right away.

"…What do you mean?"

"…No one in the city dies of thirst. There's the distribution station.
It's not that the water's not enough — it's that they don't need water.
That's different from being dry."

"…And the valley?"

"…The valley is really dry. Not because there's no water — because
water's never once gone there."

I turned that distinction over for a long time. The city wasn't dry
because it had no need. The valley was dry because water had never
reached it. On the surface, both places had no water. But Speon saw the
two as different.

*I didn't fully understand that, that day. I know now. The city was a
place that had forgotten thirst. The valley was a place that had never
even had thirst to forget. Speon knew exactly which of the two was more
urgent.*

---

That afternoon, Eikon passed by and stopped walking.

"…Where's it going?" she asked, looking at the stone marker Speon had
set.

"…The valley," I said.

Eikon's expression stiffened slightly.

"…Why there?"

"…Speon says up here isn't dry already."

Eikon turned that over a moment.

"…There's no one there. No one who needs water."

"…Seems that way."

"…Then why send it there?"

I looked at Speon at that question. He didn't stop shoveling, and
answered in my place.

"…You don't only give water to people who need it," he said. "You can
make a need, too."

Eikon's face showed she didn't understand.

"…What does that mean?"

"…I don't know either," Speon said, laughing. "It just feels that
way."

Eikon didn't ask further at that answer. She looked once more at the
pit and the channel, and walked off without a word. Watching her back, I
saw, for the first time, that she wasn't certain of something. Only for
a moment.

*I couldn't properly read that brief unsteadiness, then. I know now. In
that moment, for the first time, she'd seen something her own logic
couldn't explain — sending water to a place that had no need for it. In
her world, there was no place for a thing like that.*

---

I stood there and turned to look back at the city.

The distribution station, the square, the Curator's building. All
quiet. That quiet looked different to me, this time. Before, that
quiet had looked like peace. That day it looked like something being
held back.

"…Won't the city's people feel hurt?" I asked. "That it's not coming to
them, and going down instead."

"…I asked," Speon said.

I was surprised at that.

"…Asked who?"

"…The distribution station attendant, that official in gray, a few
others."

"…What did they say?"

"…All of them said it didn't matter. That what they had was already
enough."

That answer wasn't strange to me. This city's people always said that.
That it didn't matter. That it was enough. And that wasn't a lie.

---

That evening, the Curator heard the news and came by as well.

"…I heard it's going to the valley," he said.

"…Yes."

He stood a long while in front of the marker. I tensed for a moment,
thinking he might object. The valley wasn't a light place for him
either.

"…Are you all right with this?" I asked.

"…Yes," he said. "If anything, I think this should have happened long
ago."

"…Why?"

"…I've been going down there alone, all this time," he said. "Without
water. Now I won't be alone anymore."

Something in me stirred at that. This man was receiving a single
channel of water as company for his own sorrow.

"…You won't stop it?" I asked.

"…There's no reason to stop it," he said. "This is the first thing I've
ever not wanted to stop."

He left with only those words. I looked at Speon. Speon said nothing
and picked up his shovel again.

---

We began cutting the channel together.

Starting from the city's edge. We dug the soil shallow to make a
ditch, and packed the edges with stone. The water began to flow slowly
along it.

I found the work similar to, and yet different from, the digging I'd
done going down into the valley. Back then it had been downward, narrow,
cut deep. This time it was sideways, long, cut far. The blisters on my
palms broke open again. I felt the callus already sitting there and
picked up the shovel again.

"…Is it all right to dig this shallow?" I asked. I felt uneasy that we
were taking the soil so thin.

"…Shallow is what keeps it from collapsing," Speon said. "Dig deep and
it goes faster, but it collapses just as fast. The more you rush a
channel, the less it lasts."

Hearing that, I remembered what the old well-digger had once told me. That
you don't find water — you make it a way. I passed those words along to
Speon.

"…That's exactly right," Speon said, laughing. "I wish I'd known that
old man."

Standing there, I felt strangely steadied that two people who'd never
once met were saying the same thing.

I watched the water flow. At first it was slow. The soil was drinking it
in. But after a day or two, the soil grew wet and packed, and the water
ran faster.

*Digging that ditch, I thought of the twelve days I'd spent going down
into the valley. Back then I'd dug down and down. Now the water was
running along that same direction. I wanted to believe that wasn't a
coincidence. I'm still not sure.*

The channel reached the city's edge in three days. From there the ground
grew steep. I gathered stones and built up embankments. It had become
work my hands knew by now.

Over those three days I measured the channel's length in my own steps.
The first day, two hundred and thirty steps; the second, four hundred
more; and on the third, it reached the city's edge. Writing those
numbers down again, I remembered the steps I'd counted going down into
the valley. Back then I'd counted my own steps going down. This time I
counted the water's steps, not my own. The direction was the same.

*Digging this channel, I found myself remembering something Speon had
told me once before. That in some city he'd seen a bridge only half
finished, and that he'd had three friends, and one of them had helped
finish that bridge. I hadn't asked, then, where that bridge led. Watching
this channel now, I thought this channel and that bridge might be the
same kind of thing. Not something for one city alone, but something that
joins city to city. I'm not certain. I only set the thought down here.*

---

At the city's edge, in front of the slope that led down into the valley,
Speon stopped walking.

"…From here on," he said, "we have to go slow."

"…Why?"

"…The slope's steep. Rush it and the channel collapses. You have to
cut it a little at a time, to make it last."

I listened to that and packed the soil down evenly. Speon set stones as
supports. We worked on that the whole day.

The first tier we cut collapsed. The water surged in all at once and
overran the earthen bank. Speon didn't get angry seeing that. He simply
rebuilt the bank. This time he cut the tier lower and narrower. It took
time for the water to fill that tier before crossing to the next. Rush
it and it collapsed; wait and it crossed over. I learned that rhythm with
my hands, and with my whole body.

Every time the water crossed to the next tier, a short, broken sound
came. A brief trickling, then a stop, then trickling again. Every time I
heard that sound, I could count one more tier finished. By evening I had
heard that sound eleven times.

By evening, the water had reached the first tier of the slope. I stood
there and looked down.

The valley was down there. Still wide, still dry. I thought that
somewhere in that landscape, he must be there. Walking among the bones,
even now.

---

"…How long will it take?" I asked. "To reach all the way down there."

"…Don't know," Speon said. "Won't know till we get there."

That answer was familiar to me. Speon always said that. And it was
always right.

"…But it will reach, won't it?"

Speon looked at me.

"…It'll reach," he said. Not a voice full of certainty. Just a voice
stating a fact.

"…How do you know?"

"…Water goes down. As long as nothing blocks it."

That sentence was simple, and yet it felt heavy to me. As long as
nothing blocks it. This city hadn't been blocking the water, all this
time. It had just never made a way for it to go. Speon had made that
way. Now the water was only going where it had always meant to go.

---

*Standing there, I thought of something I'd heard from Book Two. Something
Speon had once said, they told me. Water rots when it sits still. It
lives when it flows. I'd taken that, at the time, as only talk about a
well. That day, standing in front of the slope, I understood that it had
been a much larger story than that.*

I listened to the water running down. Still a small sound. The sound of
it crossing from the slope's first tier to the next. A trickling sound,
but one that didn't stop.

"…When this water reaches down there," I asked, "what changes?"

Speon slung the shovel over his shoulder.

"…Don't know," he said again. "Won't know till it reaches."

I laughed at that answer. It was an answer no one in this city ever
gave. This city knew every outcome in advance. Speon never knew, right
to the end. And he was never ashamed of not knowing.

---

The sun went fully down. We sat side by side there and listened to the
water.

"…Cutting more tomorrow?" I asked.

"…Yeah. One tier at a time."

"…I'll help."

"…I know," Speon said. "You don't have to ask."

I laughed at that. I knew things like that about him now too.

*I couldn't fall asleep for a long time that night. I imagined the day
the channel would reach the valley. Whether he would see that water.
Whether the bones would really live again, once it reached them. I still
had no answer to that question. But this time, not having one didn't
weigh on me.*

I went back over everything that had happened these past three days, in
order. Meeting him in the valley. The question I couldn't answer.
Coming back to the city and finding the view looked different. Eikon's
logic, that sentence I couldn't argue against. The Curator's sorrow, the
wet eyes he'd shown me for the first time. And the water. That night no
one drank it, and one person dipped in a hand.

*Laying those four things side by side, I felt for the first time that
everyone I'd met in this city was, in the end, standing in front of the
same thing. He had said it was all right not to know the answer. Eikon
had said she needed no answer because she lacked nothing. The Curator
had grieved even while he knew the answer. I belonged fully to none of
those three, and stood somewhere between all of them.*

The sound of water came through the window all through the night. I
listened to it and turned that sentence over again. Up there isn't dry
already. I turned it over a long while, and fell asleep with it.

*I still don't know the day this water will reach the valley. But
writing this record now, I know this much: that water does, in the end,
reach it. And when it reaches, I come to stand again in front of the
question I put off longest in this whole book. That is not Part Three's
story. It is Part Four's.*

Before I closed my eyes, I looked at my hands, last of all. Blisters and
calluses mixed together on them. Tomorrow I would dig soil with these
hands again. And the day after that too. These hands would carry this
water all the way to the valley. I decided to believe that.
