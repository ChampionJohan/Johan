# Chapter 19. Why

> _Previously_ — I heard from the Curator what release required. Once
> released, a person becomes a body that can die again. And it
> requires that person's own consent. He told me that when you ask
> someone who lacks nothing for their consent, most of them ask back,
> "Why?" I couldn't say a word.

After I walked out of that room in Chapter 18, for days I heard only
those two syllables — *why*. When I fell asleep, when I opened my
eyes. They sat on top of every other thought I had.

*I still don't know what to call those days. Too frightened to call
them days of preparation, and yet I kept preparing something, too
frightened to call them only days of fear. Probably they were both. I
meet that kind of time more than once in this book.*

It took me three days to file for a visit.

To be precise, I could have filed that same day. The three days were
the time I spent standing at the counter with the form in my hand and
turning back.

*I didn't count those three days. My habit of counting didn't help me
that time. I learned then, for the first time, that there is a kind
of fear that can't be turned into a number.*

During those three days I saw Eikon once. Not in the garden — in
front of the ration station. I was filling the flask when she came
up.

"…Haven't seen you around lately," Eikon said.

"…I've had some things to think about."

"…What things?"

I didn't answer right away. I didn't want to talk about the
application. But Eikon didn't press. She just looked at my flask.

"…You've been opening and closing that a lot lately," she said. "I
can tell by the sound."

That surprised me a little. I hadn't even noticed I was doing it.

"…I guess so."

"…People trying to decide something do that. People here don't have
much deciding to do, so most of them wouldn't know. I learned it
watching you, Anemos."

I couldn't smile at that. Eikon studied my face and went on.

"…Are you going to ask her? Your mother."

I nodded at that.

"…When?"

"…Day after tomorrow, probably."

Eikon chose her words for a moment. It was a different kind of
silence from her usual one. She usually put a thought straight into
words. This time she was choosing something.

"…What are you going to ask her?" she finally said.

"…Whether she wants to do it."

"…That's all?"

"…There's more, after that. I've been preparing it for seven
years."

Eikon heard that and said nothing back. I couldn't tell if that
silence was worry or indifference.

"…Why aren't you answering?" I asked.

"…No reason," Eikon said. "I was going to say I hope it goes well. But
I'm not sure anymore what that would even mean."

Something cold moved through me at that. If even this girl didn't
know what "going well" meant, then what, exactly, was I walking
toward?

*I didn't hold on to that question properly, at the time. I should
have. If I had, I might have prepared a little differently the next
day. But I let that coldness pass through me and go. Going back over
the words I'd prepared, in order, felt more urgent.*

On the fourth day, I finally filed the form. My hands weren't
shaking. That itself felt strange.

---

The visiting room was the same room as always. One wooden table, two
chairs, one window. I had the grain of that table's wood memorized by
then.

The door opened and my mother came in.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes, Mother."

My mother smiled. The same smile as the last three visits. A smile
full of gladness, without a flaw in it.

"…You're looking well."

"…So are you."

This exchange, too, was identical. I'd grown used to the sameness by
then. But that day the sameness reached me differently. For the first
time, some part of me tried to guess what lay behind that smile.

I unhooked the flask from my belt and set it on my knees. I touched
the cap with my fingertips. I didn't open it. I just needed something
to hold on to.

*I've already written, more than once in this book, that moment of
holding the flask like that in that room. I did the same thing in
front of the Curator. Writing this account, I counted how often that
habit shows up. Already several times, in this book alone. When I'm
afraid, I hold something in my hand. It was the only method I knew.*

My mother saw me fiddling with the flask and said nothing about it.
She just waited. A waiting that didn't hurry, the way people in this
city wait. That day I found the waiting both a comfort and a fear. I
would have to say it eventually.

---

We sat down. My mother spoke first.

"…How have you been lately?"

I didn't answer the way I usually did. Usually I just said I was
doing fine. That day was different.

"…Mother." I said. "There's something I need to tell you."

My mother's expression shifted, slightly. Not a worried expression.
Just an attentive one.

"…What is it?"

I tried to bring out the words I'd prepared. Seven years' worth of
words. But they lost their order in my mouth.

"…I — Mother, you can." I stumbled over it. "You can be turned back
into a living body again."

---

My mother heard that and said nothing for a moment. I was afraid of
that silence.

"…What do you mean?" my mother asked.

"…There's something called release. It reverses preservation. Then
you'd really be alive again, Mother. With me."

Saying that, I felt my own throat close up.

My mother looked at me for a moment. There was surprise in her eyes.
Not a bad surprise. Just the surprise of hearing something
unexpected.

"…So there is such a thing," my mother said. "I didn't know."

*That was the first moment I felt something strange. My mother hadn't
known, either. Seven years in this city, and she hadn't known there
was a way to turn her own body back. Was it because no one had told
her, or because it made no difference to her whether she knew?*

---

"…Would you want to do it?" I asked.

This question was the point I'd prepared for. I'd hovered outside a
counter for three days for this question, waited seven years for it.

My mother heard the question and looked at me steadily. Not an angry
face, not a sad one. Just a curious one.

"…Why?"

I lost my breath at that single word.

It was different from the Curator's "Why?" That one had been a
businesslike question. My mother's "Why?" was gentle. A tone that was
truly asking out of curiosity. That made it more frightening.

---

"…Why would I need to become that again?" my mother asked again.
"There's nothing wrong with me right now."

"…But," I said, "this isn't really living, is it."

"…Isn't it?" my mother asked back. "I'm talking with you right now,
like this. I see your face, I hear your voice. If this isn't living,
then what is?"

I couldn't answer that question. Just as I hadn't been able to answer
in front of the Curator, this time too I said nothing at all.

*I knew, in that moment, that I'd hit the same wall a second time.
The first time, in front of a stranger. This time, in front of my
mother. The same wall, and the second time hurt more.*

My mother watched my silence and reached out to touch the back of my
hand. Her hand was warm. As warm as it had been seven years ago.

"…Anemos. I'm not in pain right now. I'm not afraid, I'm not sad. You
know that too."

"…I know."

"…So why does that become a problem?"

I couldn't answer that question either. I'd felt that it was a
problem, and yet I couldn't explain, right there, why it was one. The
more I searched for words to explain it, the more those words sounded
like they were about my own situation, and not hers.

---

"…Mother." I finally managed to say. "I waited seven years."

"…I know."

"…I want you to live with me again. Eat meals together, sleep under
the same roof. Just an ordinary life."

My mother heard that and closed her eyes for a moment, then opened
them.

"…That means a lot to me. It really does."

"…But?"

"…But I already see you like this, don't I. Not often, but I can
always come."

"…That's not the same as living together."

"…What's different about it?"

I lost my words again at that question. I'd thought I knew what was
different, but when it came time to actually say it, nothing came
out.

---

"…Anemos." My mother said my name. Not with anger in it. Like that
morning — a voice that was asking something of me.

"…Yes."

"…Why do you want this so much?"

I hadn't expected that question. My mother wasn't asking why for her
own sake — she was asking why for mine.

"…I," I said, "want you to be alive again."

"…That's not an answer. That's what you want. It isn't a reason."

I had nothing to say to that. She was right.

---

"…I'll think about it," my mother finally said.

That surprised me a little. It wasn't a refusal.

"…Really?"

"…Yes. I'll think about it. But I can't promise anything."

"…Why?"

This time it was I who used those two syllables. The question I'd
heard from the Curator, I threw it back at my mother this time.

My mother smiled faintly at that.

"…I don't know that yet myself. I'll have to think about it to find
out."

My mother turned her eyes toward the window for a moment. Past that
window there was always the same tree. I'd already seen that tree
many times. Even on windless days, its leaves stirred slightly. I'd
thought that strange once, but that day the thought didn't come.

"…Are you going to ask your brother too?" my mother asked, turning
back to me.

I hadn't expected that question.

"…Yes. I mean to ask him."

"…He's only eight."

"…I know. But it's his own matter, so I have to ask him."

My mother looked at me for a long time at that. A look I couldn't
tell apart — worry, or something like pride.

"…What did you know, when you were eight?" my mother asked.

I lost my words at that question. I thought of myself sitting behind
the hill at nine. What had I known, back then? Even now I don't know
exactly.

"…I don't know."

"…Neither do I," my mother said. "That's part of why I'm a little
worried."

"…About what?"

"…About what you'll do, once you hear his answer."

I had no answer to that either. My mother was already worrying about
a conversation I hadn't even had yet. I didn't find that frightening
so much as strangely comforting. For the first time in seven years,
my mother was worrying ahead of time, for my sake.

"…You don't have to worry," I said. There was no basis for it.

"…That's the kind of thing people say without any basis," my mother
said, smiling.

That smile put me a little at ease. As long as there was a smile,
nothing had collapsed yet — or so it seemed.

---

A notice came that the visit was ending. We said goodbye the way we
always did.

"…I'll come again," I said.

"…Yes. Come again."

My mother smiled and waved. The same gesture as the last three
visits. Watching that gesture, I felt that nothing had changed. The
content of our conversation had been entirely different, but the way
we parted was exactly the same.

Leaving the visiting room, I felt the strength go out of my legs. I
leaned against the wall for a moment.

*I stood there a long time. I didn't count how long. I didn't want
to. I just wanted to feel the coldness of that wall, nothing else.*

*My mother hadn't refused me. That, somehow, made it heavier. If it
had been a refusal, I would have grieved and it would have been over.
"I'll think about it" was a sentence that let me neither grieve nor
be at ease.*

*That night I took out the words I'd prepared, again. Seven years'
worth of words, piled up. That night I finally admitted, properly,
that not one of them had been of any use.*

Back at the lodging, I took out the copy of the application again. I
smoothed the crease with my fingertip. In the name field there was
still only my name. The signature line was empty.

*I looked at that blank line for a long time. My mother's "Why?"
seemed to hover over it, still. I wanted to fill it with something.
But there was nothing to fill it with.*

I folded the form back up. And for the first time, I decided to count
the words I'd prepared over seven years, one by one. I counted them
off on my fingers. Five. Five sentences.

*Let's go home now. I missed you. This isn't really living, is it. I
want you to be alive again.* And the last one, held so long that it
had stopped being a sentence and become something closer to a weight.

*I counted all five of them that night, but I couldn't bring myself
to take any of them out. To take one out I'd have had to hold it in
my hand, and I felt certain that the moment I did, it would break. I
decided to take them out one at a time, the next day. Even if I had
to watch them break, I had to watch.*

Outside the window, that same steady light was still burning. I
didn't count it. That night, counting anything was no use. I've
already written that once, earlier in this chapter. It's worth
writing again here, because that night truly was like that.

I fell asleep with the five words still hooked on my fingers. The
next morning those fingers were still folded the same way. It seemed
that even in sleep, some part of me had gone on counting. Five
fingers, five sentences. I met that morning without having properly
looked at even one of them yet.
