# Chapter 38. Why I Came

> _Previously_ — I went to my mother without trying to persuade her, and
> only talked about the valley and the water. Her expression was
> different from usual. That night I asked myself whether I'd really
> gone wanting nothing at all. The answer wouldn't come.

I couldn't sleep that night.

I lay with my head on the flask, but my eyes kept opening. I looked at
the ceiling. This room was one the Preserve set aside for people like
me. No furniture in it. No one who needed any.

*In that room, that one night, I thought more than I had in the sum of
all my seven years' nights. I still think so now. Some nights are
longer than others.*

I closed my eyes and counted back from the beginning again. From the
day I left the settlement, from the day I reached the city after four
days' walk, up through the thirty-second visit.

Somewhere among those numbers there seemed to be one I'd missed. I
didn't know which. It just felt like it was there.

*I was a person who counted things. But that night, no matter how much
I counted, no answer came. I learned then, for the first time, that
there were things counting alone could not reach.*

---

Toward dawn I fell asleep briefly and dreamed.

In the dream I was nine years old. Sitting behind the hill. But this
time I wasn't alone. My brother was sitting beside me. Still eight
years old.

"…Brother." my dream-brother said. "Why are you sitting alone."

I couldn't answer. I opened my eyes.

*I still remember that dream exactly. The moment I woke from it, I
knew. The nine-year-old me who had sat behind that hill hadn't been
there because he wanted to be alone. He had been there because he was
learning, for the first time that day, how to be alone.*

---

In the morning I went to Speon.

He was already at the ditch. He saw me and handed me a second shovel.

"…Looks like you didn't sleep well." he said.

"…How could you tell."

"…I can tell by the face. Same when you're digging."

I took the shovel and dug at the earth. I'd thought using my body might
help. It didn't.

"…Speon." I said. "That old man, before — he said water isn't
something you find."

"…He said you make it a way."

"…What did that mean, exactly."

Speon stopped digging and looked at me. It was a look I hadn't seen
before.

"…Why are you suddenly asking that."

"…I don't know. No reason."

---

He went back to digging. The answer didn't come right away.

"…Water is everywhere." he said, after a long while. "Under the
ground. You think it isn't there because you can't see it, but it's all
there, really. Digging a well doesn't mean finding it. It means making
a place for water that's already there to come out."

I turned that over.

"…Then what about me." I said. "Did I come here to find something, or
to make a place for it?"

Speon looked at me again. Longer, this time.

"…That's not a question I can answer." he said. "You have to dig it up
yourself to know."

---

*Hearing that, I set the shovel down. I stood there a long while, just
like that.*

For seven years I'd thought I had come to find something. My mother.
My brother. To find what I'd lost.

But my mother had never been lost. She'd been in the city the whole
time. I could see her whenever I wanted. I'd kept looking for someone
who didn't need to be found.

*So what had I been looking for?*

I followed that question through to the end, for the first time.

---

The seventeen funerals came back to me again.

I'd written down many times that the old man's had been the ninth. He
was the one who'd taught me to dig wells. A man of few words. For a
while after he stopped, I kept digging that field alone.

At the other eight funerals there had been other people. Relatives,
neighbors, a child I'd played with. Each time I'd watched the people
crying beside me. I'd cried too. But every time, once the crying ended,
I walked home alone.

*I counted that fact for the first time that night. Seventeen funerals,
and after them, seventeen roads walked alone. Every time I lost someone,
I left that place alone, every time.*

---

I thought back to that day when I was nine again.

The day I sat alone behind the hill. The day I stayed until the sun
went down. The day I came back to find everyone already gone.

From that day on I was always alone. I grew up in my relatives' house,
watched seventeen funerals, and in not one of them did anyone who cried
beside me stay for long.

*I'd written many times that counting was how I'd endured those seven
years. That's true. But it wasn't the whole of it. Even while I
counted, I was alone the whole time. Counting is something you do
alone.*

I remembered the years my brother used to follow me. When I went for
water, when I went for firewood. The years he followed so closely it
annoyed me. I hadn't known, then, that I wasn't alone in those years.

---

I remembered too, what my brother had said, looking up at me.

Brother, you got tall.

I had turned sixteen and my brother was still eight. I hadn't properly
felt, at the time, how large that one sentence was. It meant seven
years of growing up alone.

*I had been alone the whole time I was growing. While I got taller,
while I got stronger, while I learned how to go to a funeral. No one
beside me.*

I hadn't wanted to undo those seven years. To be precise, the fact that
my mother and my brother were still exactly as they'd been seven years
ago was the force that had dragged me all the way here. If they hadn't
changed, it seemed like I might be able to go back to before, too.

---

I pushed that thought all the way through. For the first time, I didn't
run from it.

*When I came to the city, I believed I had come to wake my family. That
much wasn't wrong. But hidden inside that sentence was another sentence.
I had come to go back to the time when I wasn't alone.*

*Waking someone and going back are different things. I saw that
difference for the first time there at the edge of the field, shovel
still in hand.*

It was a shameful thing. That's how it felt. Inside the work I'd
believed was for my family's sake, I was there too. My own share of it.

---

Eikon came to mind, all at once.

I don't lack anything. Why would I need more? She'd said that. I hadn't
been able to answer it. I couldn't refute it.

*There at the edge of the field, I admitted for the first time that
there was a lack in me too. I lacked something. I lacked someone beside
me. Eikon didn't have that lack. So she had nothing left to want. I
did. That was why I'd come all this way.*

I looked straight at that difference for the first time. Eikon's logic
was hard to refute. You can't tell someone with nothing lacking to want
more. But I lacked something. My lack had carried me this far. Without
it, I would never have come to this city at all.

*In that moment I felt, for the first time, one of this book's
questions become my own. Is what is preserved different from what is
alive. I didn't know the answer. I only knew, that day, that I lacked
because I was alive, and that I had come because I lacked.*

---

Speon came over again. He must have noticed I hadn't been holding the
shovel long.

"…Dig anything up." he asked.

The question made me laugh. A strange laugh.

"…Yeah." I said. "I thought I came here for my mother."

"…Weren't you?"

"…I was. No — that part's true too, but it wasn't the whole of it. I
came to go back to the time when I wasn't alone."

Speon wasn't surprised at that.

"…What's wrong with that." he said.

"…It's wrong. Because I said it was for my mother, when really it was
for me."

---

"…It can be both." Speon said. "When I run a channel, I do it for
whoever's thirsty. But it's also true I do it because I like the sound
of water. Both are true. Does that make it a flaw?"

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…Your share being mixed in doesn't make the love for your mother
fake." he said. "But now that you know it, now that you know, you can't
go on pretending you don't."

*I remember that as the most exact thing he ever said. When I didn't
know my own share was mixed into my love, I was a person who
persuaded. Once I knew, I couldn't be that anymore.*

"…Then what am I supposed to do now." I asked.

Speon shrugged.

"…I don't know either. I'm a well-digger. Not a heart-digger."

"…You just dug one pretty well, though."

"…That was luck." he laughed. A laugh I hadn't seen before. "There's
only one thing I can tell you. Once you've made the way, you have to
wait and see whether the water comes. Digging doesn't make it come
right away."

I held onto that for a long time.

*I learned that day, for the first time, that waiting and doing nothing
are different things. I'd thought of waiting as doing nothing, all this
time. It wasn't.*

---

At midday I ran into the Curator. He was carrying papers, headed
somewhere.

"…Your expression is different from yesterday." he said.

"…How's it different."

"…Yesterday it was a face asking something. Today it's a face that's
learned something."

I was struck by that observation. This man had truly spent a long time
reading faces.

"…I learned something." I said. "Why I came here."

"…What is it."

I hesitated for a moment, then said,

"…Turns out I came for myself. I'd thought it was for my mother."

The Curator stopped walking. He looked at me for a long while.

"…It takes many people a long time to learn that." he said. "I learned
it too, doing this work. Even I'm not certain whether I became a
Curator purely for other people's sake."

I couldn't answer that at all. He left it there and walked on.

*That was the first time I felt I understood a little of where this
man's sorrow came from. Every day he read, in people's faces, the same
thing I had seen in myself that day. A love with his own share mixed
into it. And even reading it, he kept doing the work.*

---

I picked the shovel back up.

Moving my body helped a little. The earth grew familiar to my hands
again. The channel stretched a little farther downward.

*From that day on, I never went to my mother again as a person trying
to persuade her. To be precise, I couldn't. Persuading is pretending to
act for someone else's sake while really driving them toward the
outcome you want. I now knew what I wanted. So I could no longer call
it something done for my mother's sake.*

*From that day I tried to become someone who went with her, not someone
who persuaded her. I would test the difference between those two, for
the first time, at my next visit.*

---

That evening I met Eikon. She was sitting by the channel.

"…What did you dig up today?" she asked. She remembered what I'd said
the day before.

"…Myself." I said.

"…You dug up yourself?"

"…Yeah. Something a little shameful came out."

Eikon looked at me steadily.

"…What was shameful?"

I laughed for the first time at that.

"…I'll tell you later."

"…Is it a secret?"

"…No." I said. "I just haven't put it into words yet."

Eikon didn't find that answer strange. She didn't much understand why
a person would hide something. She had nothing to hide, herself.

"…Me." she said. "There isn't much I'm curious about. But I'm a little
curious what you dug up."

"…Why?"

"…I don't know." she said. "No reason."

I let that no reason pass without thinking much of it. Eikon said that
often. Being curious without giving a reason. I only thought it was a
turn of phrase she had.

Eikon stood up. She was about to go on her way, then turned back
suddenly.

"…Anemos." she said.

"…Yeah."

"…Wanting to take your mother with you — is that a bad thing?"

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…I don't really know." she went on. "I was just curious what it's
like, wanting something. Looking at you, you always seem like someone
who wants something."

"…It's not bad." I said. "But there's some of me mixed into it. I
learned that today."

"…So will you stop?"

"…No." I said. "That's not a reason to stop."

Eikon listened to that quietly. She didn't ask further and walked away.

---

I was left alone by the channel.

The sun had fully set. Only the sound of water remained. Listening to
it, I went back through, in order, everything I'd learned that day.
Speon's words, the seventeen roads walked alone, the hill when I was
nine, Eikon's logic and my own lack.

*It's still strange to me that I learned all of that in a single day.
Maybe I had already known it, long before. It's only that the courage
to look at it came to me, that day, for the first time.*

I opened the flask and drank a little water. It was still water I
couldn't give my mother. But that day the fact didn't weigh on me the
way it had before. For the first time, I simply let myself believe that
someday I would be able to give it to her.

*That night, listening to the sound of the channel, I fell asleep for
the first time with my own share acknowledged. The shame was still
there. But the shame didn't stop me. The next day, I applied for
another visit. And I already knew what I would write in the purpose
box on the form.*
