# Chapter 18. They Become Able to Die Again

> _Previously_ — I heard from the Curator that release was possible.
> The procedure was simple too. I took the application and was happy.
> The Curator said he'd tell me the rest next time.

Two days later I sat in that room again.

In those two days I took the application out and put it back about
three times. It wasn't that I was wavering over whether to write on
it. I just wanted the paper in my hand. I even traced the spot where
my mother's name would go, with my fingertip.

*I still remember those two days exactly. They were among the
happiest two days of my life. And that they were also the last time I
would ever be happy in so simple a way — that, too, I know now.*

I didn't wait long in the waiting room this time. The receptionist saw
my name and showed me in right away, as though she'd known I was
coming.

The corridor leading to the room felt long that day. It wasn't more
than a few steps, but each step felt like it was preparing me for
something. I didn't know what.

The Curator was sitting in the same seat as last time. He nodded when
he saw me. He didn't smile.

"…You're here."

"…Yes. I came to hear the rest."

The Curator pulled a folder toward himself. This time he opened it.

---

"…I told you last time. That release means returning that person to a
living body."

"…Yes."

"…What that means, exactly — I'll tell you today."

I set the flask on my knees and wrapped both hands around it. The
same posture as last time.

"…A released person becomes, again, a fully living body. They
breathe, blood moves through them, an injury hurts, they age. They go
hungry, they go thirsty, and when they're tired, they sleep."

"…Yes, that's a good thing, isn't it."

The Curator looked at me for a moment. In his eyes I felt something
heavy approaching.

"…And from that moment," the Curator said, "they become able to die
again."

---

At first I didn't understand what he meant, in a strange way. To die.
Wasn't that obvious? If you're alive, someday you die. I'd known that
since I was very small.

"…Right. That's just natural, isn't it. Everyone who's alive is like
that."

"…Anemos." The Curator said. "The people here right now don't die."

I looked back over that sentence.

"…They don't die?"

"…No. In the preserved state, they don't die. They don't get hurt,
they don't fall ill, they don't age. That's what Perpetuity does."

I already knew that. I'd already seen it — that my mother and my
brother hadn't changed one bit in seven years. But that this connected
to the words don't die — I felt that, truly, for the first time, right
then.

"…So my mother right now," I said slowly, "is in a state where she
can't die?"

"…Yes."

"…But if she's released?"

"…From the moment she's released, she becomes a body that can die
again."

I repeated that sentence silently to myself. Becomes a body that can
die again. It was a strange sentence. Becoming able to die sounded
like gaining something, and yet at the same time I already knew it
was a loss.

---

The room went quiet. In that silence I heard that sentence again and
again in my head. They become able to die again.

*I've gone back to that moment countless times since. Even now, writing
this book, I can still hear that sentence. They become able to die
again. I couldn't admit, right away, that it was bad news. Admitting
it took time.*

"…So," I said. "If I get my mother back, I could lose her again?"

"…Yes. That's right."

"…How soon? Can she live a long life, at least?"

"…No one knows that. She'd become the same as everyone else. She
could have an accident, she could fall ill, she could live a long,
long life. She'd become just the same as what you saw outside,
Anemos."

Something in me caved in at that answer. There wasn't a single
certainty left. My mother, right now, was certain. Nothing had
happened to her in seven years, and nothing would happen to her going
forward. Release would make that certainty disappear.

I thought of the seventeen funerals I'd counted. The old man, the
neighbor's child, my father's distant relative. All of them had been
alive, and then they'd died. That was what being alive meant. It meant
that someday you joined that list.

*In that moment I asked myself this question for the first time.
Whether I truly wanted to put my mother on that list. That I couldn't
immediately answer yes to that question is something I'm ashamed of,
even now, thinking back. Ashamed, and yet an honest hesitation.*

---

"…Even so," I said. My voice cracked. "Even so, she'd be alive. She'd
really, truly be alive."

"…That's right."

"…Then I still want to do it."

The Curator nodded at that. There was no anger in his face, no attempt
to stop me.

"…Why are you so certain, Anemos? That your mother would think the
same way?"

"…That's," I started to speak, and stopped. Had I really been
certain? I asked myself that question for the first time. "…I don't
know. I just assumed, of course, that she would."

"…I understand. That feeling. But I still haven't told you
everything."

"…There's more?"

"…Yes. The most important part is still to come."

I drew a deep breath. My body knew, before I did, that something
heavier was coming.

The Curator poured a glass of water and drank it. Watching that
motion, I felt that even he found this conversation hard. He must
have done this many times, and yet each time didn't seem to get any
easier.

"…Anemos, everything I've told you so far has been the procedure.
From here it's the real reason. Why no one ever manages to see an
application through to the end."

---

"…Release," the Curator said, "requires the consent of the preserved
person themselves."

"…Consent?"

"…Yes. You can write the application, Anemos. Because you're family.
But that alone isn't enough. The subject themselves must consent
before the procedure can go forward. One more signature is needed.
Not your signature — the subject's signature."

That didn't surprise me much.

"…Isn't that only natural? It's their own life."

"…That's right. It's only natural."

The Curator said that and paused for a moment. He looked down quietly
at his hands on the desk.

"…But here's why that becomes a problem."

---

"…A preserved person," the Curator went on, "lacks nothing. They
aren't sick, aren't hungry, aren't lonely, aren't afraid. There's
nothing they need."

"…Yes, I know. I've met someone like that too. She told me she
lacked nothing."

"…Then try asking that person for their consent. Do you know what
they'll say?"

I couldn't answer.

"…When you ask for their consent," the Curator said, "most of them
ask you this.

Why?"

---

I heard that and couldn't say a word.

I truly couldn't say anything at all. I tried to open my mouth, and
nothing came out.

*I've tried to rewrite this scene more times than any other in this
book. I wanted to write it more dramatically. I wanted to write that I
argued back, that I said something. But that would be a lie. In that
room, I truly said nothing at all.*

I didn't know how much time had passed. There was no clock in the
room. I only gauged the time by the feeling in the fingertips holding
the flask. I sat there until my fingertips went numb.

The Curator waited for me. He didn't press.

"…Why, they ask?" I finally managed.

"…Yes. Why should they become a body that can get sick again? Why
should they become a body that can die again? It's good now — why?"

---

I tried, hard, to find something to argue back with that question.
The harder I tried, the less came out.

"…That's," I said. "That's not living, is it. That's not really
living."

"…That's true too," the Curator said. "But it doesn't land, for the
person themselves. They already feel that they're doing fine. They
speak, they remember, they meet people. If they ask you what living
is, then, if that isn't it — I can't answer that either."

"…And," the Curator added, "if you say that in front of them, they'll
answer you like this. Then what am I now?"

I imagined that question turned on me. My mother, looking at me, asking
that. Just imagining it closed up my throat.

"…I can't answer that question either." I admitted.

"…It isn't only you, Anemos. No one can."

I tightened my grip on the flask. My knuckles went white.

"…So," I said, "no one is ever able to be released? If none of them
give consent?"

"…That's right. That's why there are many applications and almost no
completions. Most stop right in front of the subject's consent."

---

"…What do you think, Curator," I asked, "the people who made this —
why do you think they built it this way? Why did they make consent a
requirement?"

The Curator thought about that a long while.

"…I think it was respect. At the start, at least."

"…Respect?"

"…Yes. It's their body. Family shouldn't be able to change a person's
body just as they please. That's probably why consent was made
mandatory."

I nodded at that. It made sense. And at the same time, it felt more
frightening for making sense.

"…The people who first made this can't have meant harm." I said. It
was as much a thing I was saying to myself.

"…That's right. They didn't mean harm. It was probably a very good
intention, if anything. Meant to keep anyone from reversing a person
carelessly, to make sure that person's own will was respected."

"…But that respect," the Curator went on, "ended up becoming a door
that, in the end, no one walks through. Asking for the consent of a
person who lacks nothing is the same as leaving a door open while
already knowing no one will walk out of it."

---

I sat there a long while, unable to say anything.

*I think that was the day, in that room, that I finally saw this city
clearly for the first time. Up until then I had suspected the city was
hiding something. That it was fake, a trick. But it wasn't. This city
hid nothing at all. It had simply arranged things so that no one had
any reason to leave. And that was far more complete than hiding
anything ever could have been.*

"…So what can I do?" I asked.

"…You can ask. Your mother. Your brother. I can't promise you an
answer."

"…And if they refuse?"

"…Then the application stops there. There's no way to force it
forward."

"…What if it's not a refusal, just — no answer at all? If they just
keep asking back, 'why'?"

"…That's a refusal too. Not answering is the answer."

That sounded cruel to me. But I knew he wasn't saying it to be cruel.
It was simply a fact.

"…I'm still going to ask." I said.

"…Of course. You should ask. I'm not stopping you. This is something
you have to go through yourself, Anemos."

"…Then why are you looking at me like that?"

The Curator paused for a moment at that question.

"…Like what?"

"…Like you're worried."

The Curator heard that and smiled faintly. It was a smile I hadn't
seen before. A sad smile.

"…That's right. I am worried. But that's not a reason to stop you.
I don't have the right to stop you, either."

---

My legs wavered slightly as I stood. I didn't know if it was from
sitting too long, or for some other reason. At the door, the Curator
said one last thing.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes."

"…This isn't me telling you to give up. I just want you to go in with
your eyes open."

I nodded at that. What exactly I should have been braced for, I didn't
know at the time.

As I left the room I looked again at the application in my hand.
Name. Relationship. Subject's full name. Subject's preservation
number. The signature line.

That signature line looked different to me now. It wasn't a field
for my signature — it was a field that needed my mother's.

*That day I didn't go to the ration station, didn't go to the plaza. I
went straight to the lodging. I shut the door and sat looking at that
form for a long while. Why. Those two syllables kept circling in my
ears.*

*Until then I'd thought I'd spent seven years preparing. That day I
learned that all of that preparation had been for a different
question entirely. I had prepared for how to find my mother. That my
mother might not want me — for that, I had never once prepared at
all.*

---

Outside the window, the city's lights came on, one by one. I started
counting them and gave up again. That day, counting anything was no
use.

I opened the flask's cap. I drank a sip of water. The water felt
unusually cold. This was what being alive was. Feeling cold as cold.
And, someday, that feeling stopping too.

That night, for the first time, I became afraid of seeing my mother
again. Until then there had only been longing. From that night on,
fear grew alongside it. Fear that I would ask, and hear that answer:
why. Fear of not knowing what would become of me, if I heard it.

*I go into the next chapter without having overcome that fear. I
didn't overcome it and go. I simply carried it with me. Looking back
now, carrying that fear with me was, itself, the best I could do at
the time.*
