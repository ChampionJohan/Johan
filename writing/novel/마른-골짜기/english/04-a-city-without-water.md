# Chapter 4. A City Without Water

> _Previously_ — Anemos reached the Preserve after four days. Everyone he met on the road left him with the same words: "You'll know once you go." The moment he crossed the gate, the sound of water outside cut off completely. He pushed open the door of the reception office and went in.

Inside the reception office it was cool.

One person sat there. Their age was hard to guess. They could have been about thirty, or about forty.

"…Is this your first time here?" that person asked.

"…Yes."

"…Your name and your purpose, please."

I gave my name. When I tried to say my purpose, I paused a moment, not knowing how to put it.

"…My mother and my brother are here. They were preserved seven years ago."

That person wrote something down on paper. Not surprised, not wearing a look of sympathy. Just wrote it down.

*I felt that blank expression as coldness, at first. Now I know. It wasn't coldness. In this city, that story was nothing to be surprised at. Everyone had come here to say exactly that.*

There was a nameplate on the desk. It read only "Reception Officer." No name.

"…Do you have a name?" I asked.

The person looked up. A face a little taken aback.

"…I do."

"…Then why does the plate only say—"

"…We use only titles here. It's a rule of this place."

I thought that was strange. In our settlement, people were called by name. Even the village head had a name, and everyone knew it.

*I would learn later that officials in this city were generally called only by their titles. It wasn't that they had no names. Names were simply not used. I thought a long time about why, but by the end of this book I never got a certain answer.*

---

"…How many days do you plan to stay?" the person asked.

I couldn't answer that question. I hadn't come with any notion of days. I had come only with the thought of bringing them out.

"…I don't know."

"…That's fine. We'll assign you lodging. There's no set period."

"…What about money?"

"…This city has no money."

I thought I'd misheard.

"…What?"

"…There's no currency. Whatever you need, you receive as a ration."

*I felt relieved hearing that. Outside, there had been many things I couldn't do for lack of money. Here there was no such thing at all — I only took it as good news.*

*I would learn later that it was good news and something else at the same time. Having no money also meant there was no way to have more of what you wanted. Here everyone received the same. Because no one wanted more.*

---

After registration, that person guided me to the ration station.

On the way, I asked.

"…Where's the well here?"

The person stopped walking and looked at me.

"…A well?"

"…Yes. To draw water."

"…This city has no well."

I stopped where I stood.

"…None at all? Then how does water—"

"…Whoever needs it receives it at the ration station."

"…Living people need water."

The person looked at me a moment, then answered.

"…Yes. That's what the ration is for. Those who have been preserved don't need it."

*I turned that sentence over many times. Those who have been preserved don't need it. I didn't know exactly what it meant, then. I know now. It meant there was a kind of body that could be fine without drinking water.*

---

On the way to the ration station, I saw, up close for the first time, a person of this city — that is, someone who had been preserved.

A woman. Her age was hard to guess. Her walk was a perfectly even pace. Neither fast nor slow. I greeted her without thinking, as I passed.

"…Hello."

The woman looked at me and smiled. An exact smile. Neither too wide nor too small.

"…Yes, hello."

That was all. The woman walked on. I watched her back. It was a fairly strong afternoon sun, but there was no sweat on her forehead. Her face wasn't flushed either.

*At the time I just took it as a difference in constitution and let it go. It was only later, asking the reception officer again, that I understood. A preserved person doesn't sweat. Not because they don't feel heat, but because their body has no need to use water that way.*

The ration station was a building like a small storehouse. There weren't many people.

Inside, I had the feeling that standing there holding a flask was the one strange thing about me. No one else was carrying a flask.

The station attendant saw me and asked.

"…Here for water?"

"…Yes."

"…How much do you need?"

That question flustered me too. Outside, since there was a well, there had never been a need to decide how much was needed. You simply drew what you needed.

"…A day's worth?"

"…I'll give you a day's worth."

The attendant scooped water from a jar and filled my flask. The exact amount. Neither overflowing nor short.

*I admired that exactness at first, then soon found it strange. Well water isn't exact. The amount was different every time you drew it. Sometimes it overflowed, sometimes it fell short. That unevenness was the very nature of well water. This water had come out measured. Water with no room to be uneven.*

"…And tomorrow?" I asked.

"…Come again tomorrow and you'll get more."

"…I have to come every day?"

"…Yes."

I thought that was a bother. If there were a well, you could draw a great deal at once and set it by.

*I learned later that this way of rationing wasn't about being a bother. In this city there was almost no one, besides me, who had any reason to store water. Everyone needed only to receive what they needed for that day. Storing was something only people who worried about the future did.*

---

Leaving the ration station, I walked the streets.

Buildings lined the roads between the roofs. People passed to and fro. Everyone walked quietly. No one ran.

I looked around for something like a well or a washing place. There was none. No stream either. Not even the sound of running water.

*I walked the city for about three hours that day. In all that time I never once heard the sound of water flowing. I don't think anyone who has only ever lived in an outside settlement can fully grasp how strange that is. The sound of water had always simply been there, like air. That day was the first time I ever walked through a place where it had disappeared.*

Walking on, I passed a small plaza. A stone structure stood in the middle. Round in shape, hollowed in the center. Glad to see it, I went over.

It was a fountain. A fountain with no water.

The stone floor was bone dry. Not even a trace of moss. I set my hand on the edge of the structure. Not a speck of dust — perfectly clean.

I asked a passerby.

"…Did water used to come out of this?"

The person stopped and looked at the fountain. Looked it over a moment, as if seeing it for the first time.

"…Not sure. I've never heard anything like that."

"…Then why was it built?"

"…Because the shape is pretty?"

That was all the person said before walking off. I stayed behind, looking the fountain over more.

*I would learn later that this fountain had never had water in it from the start. This city had a number of things that only kept the shape of flowing water without actually letting anything flow. That day, at the fountain, was the first time I saw it. And later I would see the same shape in something much larger.*

There was no one hanging washing out to dry, either. Every garment was clean. I found that strange too, but soon guessed the reason.

*A preserved person's clothes don't get dirty. The living were a small minority, and their laundry, it seemed, was handled some other way. I never did see that method, to the end. It was probably a way that used no water.*

There was little dust either. Outside, in the settlement, when the wind blew, dirt lifted into the air. Shoes were always grimy. Here, only my shoes were dirty. That evening I looked a long while at the soles of my own shoes.

---

The lodging was a short walk from the reception office. Inside that building were several rooms, and I was assigned one of them.

On the way in, the door of the next room was open, so I could see inside. A man was unpacking his things. He looked to be in his mid-twenties.

"…First time here?" he asked, looking at me first.

"…Yes."

"…I came the day before yesterday too. Who did you come to see?"

"…My mother and my brother."

The man nodded.

"…I have a brother. It's been three years."

I understood right away what that "it's been" meant. I was already, little by little, learning the way people used that word in this city.

"…Do you go back and forth often?" I asked.

The man's hands stopped their unpacking.

"…This is my third time."

"…Three times in three years?"

"…Yes."

I thought that was few. I didn't say it aloud. The man must have read my expression, because he added this.

"…At first I came every month."

He said only that and went back to unpacking. I asked nothing more and went into my own room.

*That night I thought together about that man's "it's been," and about how his visits had shrunk from every month to three times in three years. I still couldn't connect the two, then. The connection wouldn't come until later, until I had stood in that place myself.*

As night deepened, I looked out the window. A few lamps were lit along the street. The light was fine, even. It didn't flicker. I had watched lamp-flames flicker in the wind my whole life, but not once did a lamp in this city flicker.

*I would learn the reason for that too, later. For now I only note that it was strange. That night I was counting a list of strange things in my mind. No sound, no well, no color, no flicker. Four. I didn't know, then, how much longer that list would grow.*

It was a small room. One bed, one table. I sat in that room and set the flask down on the table.

I suddenly thought of the people I had passed that day. No one had carried a flask. No one had a water bottle at their hip. Only I did.

*That night, turning the flask over in my hands, I thought: this object had become the thing that marked me here as someone from outside. When my uncle gave it to me, it must have meant survive. Here that meaning had turned into a different meaning. It was like a mark that read: the only person in this city who needs water.*

I drank a little water that night. About half of the day's ration.

*From then on I began to conserve water. Even knowing there would be a ration every day. A habit worn into the body doesn't disappear just because it's explained.*

---

The next morning, I went back to the reception office to find out the procedure for a visit. On the way I passed the ration station from the day before.

A line stood there. Only three people. I looked closely at those three. They all looked, like me, like people who had come from somewhere else. Their clothes were a little different from the city people's.

*I tried, then, to count how many people in this whole city drank water. I couldn't count it. Instead I arrived at this: everyone who drinks water is someone who came from outside. No one was born or grew up in this city. Everyone here had come from somewhere.*

*Whether that was a correct conclusion, I would only confirm later. For now I set down only the guess I formed that morning.*

On the way, I ran into the man from the next room again, the one from the night before. He was already coming back from finishing his visit.

"…That was fast," I said.

"…If you apply early in the morning, it happens that same morning. There aren't many people."

"…How was it?"

The man looked at me a moment. His face searching for an answer.

"…Good."

There was something strange in the way he said it. The word "good" held very little good in it. I didn't notice that at the time. I only exchanged goodbyes and we parted.

*I would recall that expression again later. Once, coming back from my own first visit, when someone asked me "how was it," I found I had answered with that same tone — "good."*

Reaching the reception office, the same person from yesterday recognized me.

"…Here to apply for a visit?"

"…Yes."

"…I'll walk you through the procedure. You have to see the Curator first."

That was the first time I heard that title. The Curator. I heard the word with the flask still in my hand.

"…What kind of person are they?" I asked.

"…A kind person. You don't need to worry."

That answer was reassuring. But one thing the person added afterward stayed with me.

"…Though you may have to wait a while. They've been rather busy lately."

"…Are there a lot of people applying for visits?"

"…No, it's not that, but…"

The person didn't finish the sentence. I asked nothing more. It seemed like asking wouldn't bring an answer anyway.

*I turned that unfinished sentence over all that night. Busy, but not because there are many applicants. Then busy with what? I would learn the answer in Chapter 6. Here I only set down that I wondered.*

Leaving the reception office, I shook the flask again. Still about half left. The ration hour hadn't come yet.

*In that moment I got some sense of the shape a day would repeat in, in this city. The ration station, the reception office, the lodging. A day spent going back and forth among them. No one else's day had a flask in it. Only mine did.*
