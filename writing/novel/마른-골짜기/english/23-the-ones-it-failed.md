# Chapter 23. The Ones It Failed

> _Previously_ — For the first time I stood at the city's edge and
> looked down into the Dry Valley. No one ever went down there. Not
> because it was blocked, but because there was no reason to go. I
> couldn't fall asleep with that landscape still in my eyes.

The next day I went back to that edge.

I hadn't decided to go. I'd set out to fill the flask at the ration
station, and my feet turned that way on their own. I decided not to
fight the direction my body wanted.

When I reached the railing, someone was already there.

It was Eikon.

---

I was a little surprised.

"…Do you come here often?" I asked.

"…Sometimes."

"…Why?"

Eikon shrugged at the question.

"…No particular reason. I just end up here if I walk long enough."

I didn't quite believe that at face value. There weren't many places
in this city people went to without a reason. But I didn't press
further.

We leaned against the railing side by side. The valley was the same
as yesterday. The dry color, the sparse trees, the dark, stained
marks.

"…I saw you standing here yesterday," Eikon said. "From a distance."

"…Why didn't you come over?"

"…You looked like you wanted to be alone."

"…But you came today."

"…Today you didn't look like that."

That distinction made me smile. This girl always read people that
way. Accurate, in a way that was a little frightening for being so
accurate.

"…Who did you meet here yesterday?" Eikon asked.

"…A man. Carrying a basket."

"…Ah, him." Eikon seemed to know who I meant. "He comes here often
too. Like me."

"…Why?"

"…I don't know. I never asked. People don't ask that much, in this
city."

Hearing that, I realized for the first time that I wasn't the only
one who stood at this low railing. It seemed the people of this city,
too, sometimes walked out here, for no reason at all.

---

"…What's that?" I asked. The same question I'd asked the man
yesterday.

Eikon didn't hesitate at all with that question.

"…The valley."

"…I know that. I mean what's down there. Those dark marks."

Eikon looked down for a moment. Then she spoke, in a tone as
unaffected as ever.

"…Traces."

"…Traces of who?"

"…Of the people whose preservation failed."

I heard that and my body went stiff. Eikon's tone hadn't changed in
the slightest. The exact same tone she'd use to talk about the
weather.

---

"…Failed?" I asked back. "It can fail?"

"…Very rarely. It isn't something that happens often, to begin
with."

"…How does it fail?"

Eikon thought for a moment before answering. A face choosing her
explanation, not a face of discomfort.

"…Preservation starts with registering the person who's brought in.
Their name, their record, who they are. If that registration doesn't
finish properly, the preservation doesn't finish properly either."

"…What happens if it doesn't finish?"

"…It simply doesn't happen. That person doesn't remain in the
preserved state."

"…Then what? What happens to them?"

"…Just — as they were before. A body that gets sick, grows old,
stops."

Hearing that answer, I felt the strength go out of my knees.

"…How often does that happen?" I asked. "That kind of failure."

"…I don't know exactly. But it's supposed to be very rare. Rare
enough that most people here go their whole lives without hearing of
it even once."

"…But you know about it."

"…I heard it as a child. From my parents. Just that such a thing
exists."

"…Weren't you scared? Back then?"

Eikon looked into that question for a moment.

"…I was a child too, back then. Not so much scared — I just thought,
so there's such a thing in the world too. Most people here take it
that way. Like bad weather. Not common, but not something that never
happens either."

That comparison sent a chill through me. Bad weather. This city
called a person's complete disappearance something on the order of
weather.

---

"…Sometimes records get damaged, too," Eikon went on. "Among people
registered a very long time ago, sometimes the record ages until it
can't be read anymore. If that happens, the preservation tied to that
record ends along with it."

"…When does that happen? At registration, or later?"

"…Mostly at registration, they say. Damage happening later is
supposed to be almost unheard of."

That answer gave me a very small measure of relief. It didn't apply
to my mother and brother. Their records had been intact for seven
years.

"…So," I asked again, "do all those failed people end up down
there?"

"…Yes. More precisely, their traces do. Whether it's the body, or
whatever's left."

"…Why there, of all places?"

Eikon couldn't answer that question.

"…I don't know. It's just always been that way. I only know what
I've been taught."

---

"…Doesn't it make you sad?" I asked. A different version of the
question I'd already thrown at her once, in Chapter 14.

Eikon looked genuinely puzzled at that.

"…Why would it be sad?"

"…A person died. That's what failing means, in the end, isn't it."

"…It failed. No one did anything wrong. The registration didn't go
through, and that's just what happened."

That distinction felt foreign to me. Outside, failure and fault
often sat in the same place. When something failed, people went
looking for someone to blame. In this city, that link itself seemed
not to exist.

"…Have you ever been down there?"

"…No. There's no reason to go."

Hearing that answer, I heard the man's words from yesterday layered
over it. There's no reason to go. It felt like this whole city had
memorized the same sentence.

---

In that moment a number came to me.

Eleven.

Seven years ago, eleven people came from our settlement to this
city. They were the sick. I'd received word that nine of them had
been preserved. What had happened to the remaining two was written
nowhere in that bulletin.

I stood there and counted that number again. Eleven minus nine.
Two.

"…Eikon." I asked. My voice shook. "If — say a person came to this
city, and no word ever came about whether they were preserved or
not. What would that mean?"

Eikon looked at my face for a long time at that question. A face
that had noticed something.

"…That usually means they failed."

---

I heard that answer and had nothing to say.

I unhooked the flask and opened and closed the cap. My hands were
shaking. I gripped the flask harder to try to stop the shaking.

"…It's not certain, though," I finally said. "That no word means
failure. It could be some other reason."

"…What other reason could there be?"

I couldn't answer that question. No other reason truly came to mind.
I'd simply hoped there was one.

"…What did the bulletin say, exactly?" Eikon asked.

I still had that sentence memorized. A sentence I'd read over and
over.

"…'The subject has been stably preserved, and a visit may be
requested at any time.' That's what was written for the nine. For
the other two, nothing at all was written."

Eikon heard that and nodded. There was no hesitation in that nod.

"…Then they failed. There was nothing to write, so nothing was
written. If it's good news, this place writes all of it down."

I wanted to argue with that certainty, but I had no words to argue
with. I think I'd already known it myself. I'd only not wanted to
admit it.

*I set this down beforehand, in Chapter 1. That of the eleven, nine
were preserved, and what became of the remaining two was never
written. And I wrote that I would learn later where those two had
gone. This is that place.*

*I don't know those two people's names. I don't know their faces. I
know nothing about them, beyond that they were carried away from our
settlement together. But in that moment I knew that somewhere down
there, there might be traces of those two people whose faces I'd
never known.*

"…Is there any way to confirm it?" I asked. "Whether those two are
really down there."

Eikon shook her head.

"…Without a name, there's no way to confirm it. They were never
registered here to begin with."

I felt that answer as cruel. Eikon's tone was still not cruel at
all. Only the fact was.

---

We stood there in silence for a moment. The wind blew up from below
again. I couldn't tell whether it was the same wind as yesterday, or
a different one.

"…Are you all right?" Eikon asked. For the first time, something
like worry showed on her face as she looked at me.

"…I don't know."

"…Did I say something wrong?"

"…No. You just told me the truth."

Eikon looked a little relieved at that. Watching her face, I
confirmed again that she hadn't meant to hurt me. She'd only said
what she knew.

*That day, for the first time, I saw Eikon's composure differently.
Up until then, I'd only thought that composure came from having no
lack. That day it was different. That composure existed, too,
because death itself had already become a very distant thing in this
city. The preserved don't die. The failed aren't even registered.
This city had never had a place set aside for grief, from the start.*

"…Are you going to go down?" Eikon asked.

I couldn't answer that question right away.

"…I don't know."

"…What would change, if you went down? You don't even know their
names."

I couldn't answer that question either. She was right. Going to look
for two people whose names I didn't even know made no sense. And yet
in that moment I felt some corner of my heart already leaning that
way.

Eikon stood quietly beside me. She didn't hurry me. She just looked
down with me.

---

That afternoon I went to find the Curator. There was something I
wanted to confirm.

"…You said nine of the eleven sent word back," I said. "Why did the
other two never send any?"

The Curator took out a folder and leafed through it. It took a
while, as though the record were an old one.

"…This is a record from seven years ago. Here it is." He set his
finger on a page. "Nine people, registration completed. The
remaining two…" He paused a moment. "…it says here, registration
incomplete."

"…What does that mean?"

"…Exactly what Eikon told you. It means the preservation procedure
never finished going through."

"…What were those two people's names?"

The Curator looked over the page again. He shook his head.

"…Without registration, there's no name either. I'm sorry."

That "I'm sorry" put me at ease, oddly enough. It was the first time
anyone in this city had apologized to me. Everyone else had only
stated facts. This man stated the fact and was sorry about it too.

"…Are they down in that valley too?" I asked.

"…Probably. I can't promise you that. But it's by far the most
likely thing."

I heard that answer and rose from my seat. There was nothing more to
ask. There had been — but it felt like nothing more would change the
answer.

At the door, the Curator called me back once more.

"…Anemos."

"…Yes."

"…Not many people come to ask about this kind of thing."

"…Why not?"

"…Most people just remember the number nine. It's easier to forget
the two."

I had no answer to that. I too had nearly forgotten those two, for
seven years. I'd lived content with the number nine.

---

I went back to that edge again. Eikon was already gone.

I looked down alone again. The dark marks were still there. I
couldn't tell which of them, if any, were the traces of the two
people who'd come from my settlement. It seemed I would never be
able to tell.

*I didn't go down into the valley that day. It wasn't yet the time.
But from that moment on, what lay down there was no longer, for me, a
strange landscape. Down there might be the trace of someone I knew.
No face, no name, but someone I knew all the same.*

Walking back to the lodging, I counted those eleven people again. I
didn't remember their faces exactly. I'd been nine. But a few pieces
remained. Someone's cough, a child carried on someone's back, an old
woman waving from a doorway.

*I couldn't tell which of those fragments belonged to those two
people. I probably never will. I found it strangely troubling that I
couldn't write their names in this account. I had never learned how
to mourn someone with no name.*

That night, for the first time, I felt relief that my mother and
brother had been among the nine. And I was ashamed of that relief.
The boundary between nine and two had been pure chance. I couldn't
tell whether I should be grateful for that chance, or sorry for it.

*That day, for the first time, I saw a small gap in this city's
perfection. Perpetuity has never once broken its promise. The
preserved truly do not disappear. But that promise applied only to
those whose registration was finished. For those never registered,
there had never been a promise to begin with, to keep or to break. I
would only understand later that this gap wasn't a flaw in this
city, but proof that this city was honest. This city never made a
promise it couldn't keep.*
