# Chapter 15. A Sickness

> _Previously_ — I spent a day walking with Eikon. I asked whether she
> didn't lack anything, whether she didn't feel stifled. She asked me
> back, "By what?" and I couldn't answer.

The next time, I went to find her first.

That morning I went to look at the fountain again. Something she'd
said had stayed with me. It's the same water, but the shape's
different every time. I stood in front of it a long while and watched
the shape the water broke into. It really was a little different each
time. But that only made me feel more stifled. She had found something
inside it, and to me it still looked like nothing but repetition.

*Standing in front of that fountain, I thought this for the first
time. Maybe it wasn't that I couldn't see it — maybe I was looking for
something that never needed to be seen in the first place. That
thought didn't leave me all day.*

I waited in front of the garden. I didn't know whether Eikon would
show up or not — we hadn't made any promise to meet. But about an hour
later she came walking up. There wasn't the slightest surprise in her.

"…Here again."

"…Yes. There's something I wanted to say."

"…You look serious today."

I didn't know what my face looked like. I touched it lightly with my
hand, though it wasn't as if that would fix my expression. I just did
it anyway.

"…Not serious, exactly. I just had a few things I was curious about."

Eikon tilted her head and studied me. Her eyes really were curious. In
front of that gaze I felt the words I'd prepared come apart. I hadn't
meant to be angry, or to press her — but looking at her face, there
was nothing left to do but speak plainly.

Eikon sat down beside me. She didn't press me to get on with it. I was
grateful for the waiting, and also weighed down by it.

"…Last time I asked you something strange. Whether you felt stifled."

"…Yes, I remember."

"…I couldn't answer back then, but I think I understand now. What I
was asking."

---

"…I," I began. "When I was outside, I always wanted something more.
When it didn't rain, I wanted rain. When it rained, I wanted it to
stop. Whenever I got something, I wanted something else after that."

Eikon listened quietly.

"…That was hard. But I can't really imagine what it would be like
without it. A life with nothing to want."

"…That's me." Eikon said. Her tone was even.

"…Yes. So I wanted to ask. Do you really never want anything? Not
even once?"

This time Eikon thought for longer. I felt that this silence was
different from the ones before. The earlier silences had been
silences trying to understand a question. This one felt like a
silence choosing whether or not to bring something out.

"…Once, I saw someone like that." Eikon said.

"…Like what?"

"…Someone who'd been here as long as I had. One day, that person
started wanting something."

---

"…What did they want?"

"…I don't know exactly. They couldn't really explain it either. They
just said something was missing. That what was here wasn't enough."

I leaned forward.

"…And then?"

"…That person got stranger and stranger. Didn't seem to sleep well,
wasn't the same even when they met people. Kept trying to be alone."

"…Were they sick?"

"…No. It wasn't sickness. It just seemed like they kept looking for
something. Something that wasn't there."

Eikon's voice stayed even the whole time. An evenness with no grief
mixed into it. I found that strange.

"…What about that person now?" I asked.

"…They applied for release."

---

That word reached my ears for the first time. Release.

"…What is that?"

"…Reversing preservation. Going back to how you were before."

"…Before?"

"…A living body."

It took me a moment to understand what she meant. Once I did, my heart
sped up.

"…Is that possible? Reversing it?"

"…Yes. It's possible. I heard it's not even that difficult."

"…Then why doesn't anyone do it? Everyone just stays here."

"…Because no one wants to."

I didn't understand that answer right away.

"…No one wants that? To become a living body again?"

"…Think about it. Here, no one gets sick. No one goes hungry. No one
dies. So why would you give that up and go back to a body that can get
sick again?"

I couldn't answer that question at all. As logic, it was correct.
And yet something kept catching at me.

"…So what happened to that person, then? Where are they now?"

Eikon gave a small shrug.

"…I don't know. There's been no word since they applied."

"…No one knows? What happened to them?"

"…Right. Word doesn't really travel from the people here to begin
with. Once you're released, you're off this city's roster."

There was something chilling in that answer. A whole person had
vanished completely, and it was explained this calmly, as if it were
nothing at all.

Outside, I'd watched people disappear many times. They disappeared by
dying. That was a sad thing, and everyone treated it that way. But the
disappearance I'd just heard about was different from that. This
person hadn't disappeared by dying — they'd disappeared in order to
live. And yet no one knew what had become of them.

*In that moment I thought, for the first time, that in this city the
direction of disappearing ran opposite to outside. Outside, you
disappeared by dying. Here, you disappeared by living again. Whether
those two disappearances could carry the same weight, I couldn't
decide, then.*

---

"…Isn't that sad?" I asked.

Eikon looked genuinely puzzled.

"…Why would it be sad?"

"…No one even knows what happened to them."

"…That person went because they wanted to. They applied themselves.
It's not something to be sad about."

I wanted to argue with that, but I didn't know where to begin. Nothing
in it was wrong.

"…I think that," Eikon went on, "is a sickness."

"…A sickness?"

"…Yes. Wanting something when you lack nothing. That's a sickness."

---

"…But why is it a sickness? It's just wanting something."

"…Wanting comes from lacking something, doesn't it? But here, there's
nothing lacking. And if you still want something anyway, isn't
something wrong with your body or your mind? Like someone who isn't
sick feeling as if they are."

I turned that logic over. The logic itself wasn't strange. It was, if
anything, clean. If you feel a lack when nothing is lacking, then
isn't the feeling itself what's wrong? Outside, I'd been taught the
opposite. That it's the person who can't feel lack who's strange. A
person who's full and keeps eating, a person who has plenty and wants
more. Grown-ups clicked their tongues at people like that.

Here, that standard was flipped entirely on its head. Feeling a lack
was the sickness.

*I couldn't find an answer to that logic in that moment. I still
can't, fully, now. Even now, writing this book, I don't have a
sentence that truly beats Eikon's.*

"…Then could I be sick too?" I asked. "The things I was just saying."

Eikon looked at me closely. There was worry mixed into her eyes. It
was an expression I hadn't seen before.

"…You're a bit different, Anemos."

"…How am I different?"

"…You're not from here. You said you really did lack things outside.
That's not a sickness. That was just a fact."

"…What about now, though? I'm here now."

Eikon didn't answer right away. She looked at me for a moment, as
though studying me.

"…I'm not sure, right now. If you keep wanting something even here, I
can't really judge that either."

I couldn't tell whether that answer was comfort or warning.

"…Did you think that person was sick too?" I asked again.

"…No. That person didn't think it was a sickness. They thought they
were right."

"…What about you?"

Eikon looked at me and said, evenly,

"…I think that person was pitiable. They caught a sickness they never
had."

---

"…Pitiable?" I repeated. "But you just said it wasn't something to be
sad about."

"…Yes. Both are true. Pitiable, but not sad. They went because they
wanted to."

I couldn't understand that distinction. How could something be
pitiable and not sad at the same time.

*I would come to understand that distinction only later. To Eikon,
sadness was a feeling that came from losing something. But that person
hadn't lost anything. They had only gone to get something they wanted,
something they'd chosen themselves. Nothing lost, so no reason to be
sad — that was her arithmetic.*

Eikon studied my face and asked,

"…Why do you look so serious?"

"…No reason. It's just — that story sounded a little frightening."

"…What's frightening about it? A story about someone who got sick?"

I couldn't answer that question. I didn't even know exactly what was
frightening about it. Only that evenness, that word sickness, kept
pulling at me.

"…Have you never once had that happen to you?" I asked. "Wanted
something more."

Eikon shook her head.

"…No."

"…Not even once? In seven years?"

"…Yes. You keep asking, but really, no. It's not a lie."

I knew it wasn't a lie. I'd already confirmed, several times over,
that no one in this city lied. That was exactly what made it more
frightening. Because it wasn't a lie.

"…Then maybe that person was like you, at first." I said. "Before they
wanted something."

Eikon paused for a moment at that. It was the first hesitation I'd
seen in her.

"…Maybe so."

"…Then someday, could it be you too?"

"…I don't know. That, I don't know either."

In that short answer I heard, for the first time, something that
wasn't certainty. Until then, every answer she'd given had been
certain. This one wasn't.

*I remember that moment for a long time after. It felt like I'd seen a
tiny crack in something that had looked whole. Hardly enough to call a
crack — no bigger than a fingernail mark. And yet it was the largest
thing I saw that whole day.*

---

The sun began to set. The garden's shadows lengthened.

"…I should get going." Eikon said, standing.

"…Okay."

"…Be careful not to catch the sickness, Anemos."

She said it and laughed. It was a joke. A real joke, meant as a joke.

I couldn't laugh.

*I kept turning that line over that night. Be careful not to catch the
sickness. She really believed it was a sickness you could catch. And
I, the moment I heard it, thought that maybe I already had.*

Walking back to the lodging, I repeated that word to myself several
times. Release. I'd heard it for the first time that day. I didn't
know, then, that it would become the heaviest word of everything I
would hear in this city from then on.

---

I couldn't sleep even after reaching the lodging. I thought about my
mother and my brother. And I thought about that stranger. The one
who'd caught the sickness. The one who had felt a lack in a place with
nothing lacking.

*I didn't know, then, who that person was. Not their name, not their
face, not their age. And yet that person kept overlapping with me. I
hadn't been in this city long myself, and already I kept wanting
something.*

I lay in bed fiddling with the flask. Opening and closing the cap. I
counted to about twenty-something and stopped. Counting was no use
that night either.

That night I had this thought for the first time. That I wanted to
give my mother a body again. But the moment I thought it, Eikon's
words followed right behind it. Because no one wants that.

My mother might not want it either. That possibility entered my head
for the first time that night. I tried to push it away. It wouldn't
be pushed away.

It was the same with my brother. I'd never once asked an eight-year-old
what he wanted and what he didn't. I'd just assumed I could take him
along however I wanted. That assumption felt strange, that night, for
the first time.

*I hardly slept that night. The moment I opened my eyes the next
morning, I'd decided one thing. To go find the Curator and ask exactly
what release was. That became the next chapter of this story.*

Out the window the city's lights were still lit, steady as ever. I
tried to count them and gave up. They weren't countable. They all ran
together, so I couldn't tell where one ended and the next began.

I paced the room a few times. It was a small room; a few steps and
you hit a wall. I circled, and circled, and circled again. Walking
wasn't going to produce an answer, but it was better than sitting
still.

*That night, for the first time, I felt this city was frightening. Up
until then I'd only felt it was strange. Quiet, without water, with
everyone looking so alike. But what I felt that night was a different
kind. This city wasn't a bad place. That was exactly what made it
frightening. No one here wanted to leave, and I felt like the strange
one, for finding that strange.*

I only closed my eyes around dawn. I slept briefly, then got up and
filled my flask. That day I walked not toward the ration station but
toward the Curator's office. That road felt shorter than any road I'd
walked until then. I hadn't quickened my pace, but I arrived in what
felt like no time at all.
