# Chapter 34. The Curator's Sorrow

> _Previously_ — Eikon stated her logic plainly. "I don't lack anything.
> Why would I need more?" I couldn't argue against it. She said she
> wasn't shaking. I felt something else behind that certainty.

The next day I went to see the Curator.

I had no particular business. It wasn't a visit application either. I
simply couldn't stop thinking about that back I'd seen climbing the
steps the day before.

The clerk at the front desk looked a little puzzled to see me.

"…Not a visit today?"

"…No. I just came to see him."

The clerk went off to ask inside. I sat on the long bench and waited.
I didn't count the chairs, always twelve of them. I felt no need to
count.

---

The Curator's room was exactly as it had been.

One desk, two chairs. The walls still bare. The stack of papers looked
a little taller than before.

Every time I walked into that room, I felt anew how completely
unchanged it was. The first time, the second time, today. I used to
think this was simply his taste. Now I know better. It wasn't that he
had no reason to change it — it was that he had no time to. The stack of
papers in that room had never once gotten smaller.

The varnish had worn off the corner of the desk, at the spot where he
rested his hand. One armrest of his chair shone unusually smooth on one
side only — the side, it seemed, he leaned on when he signed. Looking
at those spots, I felt, for the first time, as though I had actually
seen with my own eyes how long this man had sat at this desk.

The Curator saw me and rose slightly from his seat.

"…Anemos. It's been a while."

"…Yes."

"…I heard you went down to the valley."

I was startled at that.

"…How did you know?"

"…There's a record that you filled your flask twice at the distribution
station." The Curator smiled slightly. "This city keeps a record of
even that."

I felt that smile was a little different from his usual one. I didn't
know then what was different about it.

---

"…How was it?" the Curator asked.

I couldn't answer that right away. I didn't know where to begin.

"…There were a lot of bones," I said. "And I met someone."

At that the Curator touched the desk with his fingertips. A very brief
motion.

"…Someone?"

"…Yes. He was walking there."

The Curator said nothing for a moment. I felt that silence resembled
that gap I'd seen in him before — that instant, while explaining a
procedure, when his eyes had drifted somewhere else, very briefly.

"…Did you know?" I asked. "That there's a person down there."

The Curator nodded. Then he spoke very slowly.

"…I knew. I've known for a long time."

---

"…Who is he?"

"…I don't know either," the Curator said. "I've never asked him his
name."

"…Why not?"

"…I thought he wouldn't answer, even if I asked."

I laughed at that. It matched exactly what I'd experienced in the
valley.

"…Have you been down there?" I asked.

The Curator looked out the window a moment. Only rooftops were visible
through it. Not a direction that looked toward the valley.

"…Yes," he said. "Many times."

I was startled by that. I'd heard that no one in this city went down.
There was no need to.

"…Why do you go down?"

The Curator didn't answer that right away. He squared the corners of the
stack of papers, though they were already square.

"…Because it's my work," he said.

---

"…Is that your work? Going down into the valley?"

"…No one assigned it to me," the Curator said. "I just thought someone
had to know. What's down there."

I turned that over for a long time.

"…What is down there?" I asked. "As you've seen it."

This time the Curator was silent for a long while. I nearly started
counting that silence. Then I stopped myself. For the first time,
counting something in front of this man felt like rudeness.

"…Failure," he said finally.

Something in me went rigid at that word.

"…Are the ones who failed, down there?"

"…No," the Curator said, shaking his head. "It isn't the people who
failed."

---

"…Then what failed?"

The Curator looked straight at me. For the first time, I saw something
unhidden in those eyes.

"…I did," he said. "I'm the one who failed."

I had no answer to that.

"…What do you mean?" I managed. "What did you fail at?"

The Curator laced his fingers together. He sat like that for a moment.

"…When I first came to this post," he said, "I thought preservation was
a completion. Keeping a person from pain, keeping them from
disappearing. I believed that was the best thing we could do."

"…You don't now?"

"…I still think that. Preservation really is a good thing. No one is
in pain, no one disappears. That's not a lie."

"…But?"

"…But I learned, later, that believing that was the whole of it — that
was my failure. That not being in pain and being alive are different
things — I didn't know that. Not for a very long time."

"…Perpetuity doesn't fail," the Curator went on. "That's a fact.
Preservation is exact. It does exactly what it promises. No
disappearing, no losing memory, no pain. It has never once broken that,
not a single time."

"…But?"

"…But believing that was the whole of it — that was my failure."

*I didn't fully understand that sentence at the time. I know now. This
man wasn't doubting the system. The system worked perfectly. What he
doubted was himself — himself, who had believed a perfect system to be
only that: perfect.*

---

"…What's down there," the Curator said, "isn't something preservation
failed to produce. It's a place that shows what preservation left out."

"…Left out what?"

The Curator opened his hand, then closed it again.

"…I don't know exactly, either. That's why I go down. To find out."

I felt that answer was strangely honest. This man never seemed ashamed
to say he didn't know. I'd sensed before that he didn't tell me
everything he knew. But that wasn't him hiding something — it was that
there really were things he didn't know.

"…The first time I asked you," I said, carefully, "when you told me
release was possible. You didn't look glad."

"…No."

"…Why not?"

The Curator looked at me a long while at that question.

---

"…While I'm in this post," he said, "my work is to explain this
procedure exactly. As it is. Without hiding anything, without
exaggerating anything. That's my job."

"…Yes."

"…But every time I do that work, I know exactly what I'm saying."

I hadn't yet felt the full weight of those words.

"…What do you mean?"

For the first time I saw that his eyes were wet. He wasn't crying. Just
something heavy around them.

"…Every time, I recommend preservation to people," he said. "Because
it's my work. And I know what that means for that person. They will
never be in pain again. They will never disappear again. And they will
never, ever again, laugh or cry."

I couldn't breathe at that.

"…Knowing that," the Curator went on, "I do that work every day."

"…How many times a day?" I asked.

"…Usually three or four," he said. "Sometimes more than ten."

I was startled by that number. This man repeated words of the same
weight ten times a day. I tried to count how many years that had
accumulated over, and gave it up. It seemed a number too large for
counting to mean anything.

---

"…Then why do you keep doing it?" I asked. I didn't mean it as an
accusation. I was truly curious.

"…If I don't, someone else will," the Curator said. "And I — at least if
I'm the one doing it, I can do it without hiding anything."

"…Does that comfort you?"

"…No," he said, right away. "It's no comfort. It's just the best I can
do."

*I couldn't say anything in front of that answer. This man didn't look
for comfort in his own work. He didn't justify his own work either. He
simply knew the work was sad, and didn't stop because of that sadness.*

---

"…What about kids like Eikon?" I asked. "Do you know them too?"

"…Yes. I know all of them."

"…How do you feel, seeing them?"

The Curator looked out the window a moment.

"…At first I was glad," he said. "It was proof that preservation was
working well. No pain, not forgotten. That was the goal of my work."

"…And now?"

"…I'm still glad," he said. "And at the same time, I'm sad."

I started to ask how those two feelings could exist together in one
person, and gave it up. I already knew the answer. I'd heard something
like it from Speon. Worried, but that doesn't mean I can go in your
place. Hard, but that doesn't mean I can't bear it. This man was holding
two things together in the same way.

---

"…I'm not going to quit this work," the Curator said. "That's the most
honest answer I can give you."

"…Why not quit?"

"…Because if I quit, someone who doesn't grieve as much as I do will do
this instead."

My throat tightened at that. This man was using his own sorrow like a
resource. He seemed to believe that if someone who grieves does this
work, the work becomes, if only a little, less cold.

*I came to see this man differently, that day. At first I'd seen him as
a kind man. Then I'd seen him as a tired man. That day, for the first
time, I saw him as a sad man. And I saw that his sadness didn't stop him.
I think the last of those three is the heaviest.*

*Elsewhere in this record, I've heard about other officials in other
cities. One believed the system so completely he never doubted it. One
didn't believe it, but followed it anyway for lack of any alternative.
Another didn't even know what shame was. Every time I hear those
stories, I think of this man. He was unlike all three. He believed the
system — because the system really was exact. And at the same time,
knowing exactly what that exactness meant for a person, he kept doing the
work every day. I think that is the hardest place to stand. Believing,
and grieving.*

From the stack of papers on his desk, he pulled out one sheet, then put
it back without showing it to me. I didn't ask what it was. But
watching that gesture, I remembered what Speon had said — that this man
wrote every document himself instead of leaving it to anyone else. That
he staked his own name on anything he decided.

"…Did you write that yourself?" I asked.

"…Yes. I write every document I sign, myself."

"…Why? You could leave it to someone else."

"…If I don't write it myself," the Curator said, "it feels like I'm not
taking responsibility for it."

My throat tightened again at that.

I looked down at the floor by the window. It was worn far more than the
rest of the floor. It looked like the spot he stood in every time he
looked outside. I knew that kind of wear didn't come in a few days. It
takes a long time, a very long time of standing, to make a floor look
like that.

---

"…Thank you," I said. I wasn't sure exactly what I was thanking him for.

"…For what?"

"…For being honest with me."

At that the Curator smiled, very slightly, for the first time. A sad
smile.

"…Anemos, you're the first person I've said this to."

I asked, startled.

"…You never told anyone?"

"…No. No one ever asked."

That sentence rang, to me, like Eikon's had. It's been a while since
I've heard the word bored. No one in this city ever asked each other
things like this. They were people with no reason to ask.

---

"…Can I ask one more thing?" I said, standing to leave.

"…Yes."

"…You said you'd seen someone released. The first time. Can you tell me,
now, what that was like?"

The Curator looked at me for a long while.

"…I trembled," he said. "Seeing someone who could die again. But that
person smiled, for the first time. I still remember that face."

I couldn't ask anything to that.

"…What happened to that person afterward?" I asked.

"…They lived. A few years," the Curator said. "And then they stopped."

I recognized that word. It was a word I'd used outside. The word for
someone who passes after a long illness. It was the first time I'd heard
anyone in this city use it.

"…Where did you learn that word?" I asked.

"…That person taught it to me. After the release. They asked me to use
it, when they died."

"…Why?"

"…They said they wanted to leave it behind as proof they had lived."

I caught my breath at that.

"…Was that smile a good thing, or a bad thing?" I managed.

"…Both," the Curator said. "I still don't know the answer."

*Leaving that room, I thought again of the day I'd first felt sorry for
this man. That day I couldn't explain why. I can now. I think I was
already sensing this man's sorrow, even then. It just took twenty-eight
chapters to give it a name.*
