# Chapter 7. Applying for a Visit

> _Previously_ — Anemos met the Curator for the first time. A person versed in procedure, courteous, yet somehow worn. "The feelings you've prepared may not all get used" — the meaning of those parting words, Anemos does not yet know. He handed in his application. Now only the waiting was left.

The officer was a young woman.

"…This way, please," the woman said. "I'll call you as soon as it's ready."

I followed her to a small waiting room. There were a few chairs, and no one else there but me.

"…How long will it take?" I asked.

"…About half an hour. An hour at the most."

Hearing that span of time, my heart felt strange. I had waited seven years, and now it hadn't sunk in that only half an hour remained.

*I still remember exactly how I spent that half hour, sitting in that waiting room. I spent it counting. There was nothing like a clock on the wall. I gauged the time by the sound of my own heartbeat.*

*I would count one breath in and one breath out and call it one. Counting up to fifty that way felt like about five minutes had passed. That afternoon I counted this way six times. On the sixth, I lost the number and had to start again from the beginning. That was the first failure I experienced that day.*

---

The moment I sat down, I took a piece of paper from my pocket.

The paper where I had written out the words I'd worked over on the road. I unfolded it and read it again.

First, the greeting.

*I had meant to open with "I've missed you." Then something like "Have you been well." I had practiced this order the whole four days of walking.*

I had reworked that greeting many times too. At first, worried that if I just said "Mom" I wouldn't be able to go on, I'd considered opening with something else instead of the word itself. In the end I decided to simply say it.

*I stopped counting how many times, in that waiting room, I silently rehearsed that single word, "Mom." I gave up somewhere around twenty. Counting had lost its meaning. That day I learned, for the first time, that some words come out sounding like the first time no matter how many times you practice them.*

I also looked at the small note I'd written in the margin. "While smiling." I had written those two words down since I was nine years old. I mustn't meet her crying, nine-year-old me had thought.

*Seeing that note, I laughed a little in the waiting room that day. It felt like homework nine-year-old me had left for sixteen-year-old me. While smiling. I had no confidence, then, that I could keep that homework.*

Second, everything that had happened since.

*My uncle's household, Gaon, learning to dig wells. I had meant to tell it all in order, thinking my mother would want to know.*

I had broken that second item down in more detail. The story of Gaon coming to think of me as family. The story of the first time I cried aloud, when the old man stopped. Whether to include the story of my friend who stopped in eight days, I had agonized over for a long time.

*In the end I decided to include it. It felt like something my mother should hear. I thought that only by knowing everything I'd endured would she understand the words I meant to use to persuade her to come with me.*

*Looking back now, it was a strange calculation. The calculation that if I told her everything I'd endured, she would do something for me because of it. I didn't even know, then, that it was a calculation.*

I had even numbered the order on the paper. One, Gaon. Two, the old man. Three, my friend. Four, how to dig for water. I had changed that order about three times over the four days on the road.

At first I had meant to tell the old man's story first. I wanted my mother, above all, to hear what he had told me — that you don't find water, you make it a way. On the second night I changed the order. It seemed wrong to start with something sad, so I decided to tell Gaon's story first.

*At the time I thought reordering it that way was consideration for my mother. Looking at it now, it wasn't consideration — it was fear. I was afraid that if I brought up something sad first, she would grieve, and I'd never get to the main thing I meant to say.*

On the third night I changed the order again. I meant to cut my friend's story out entirely. It felt too heavy. But on the morning of the fourth day I decided to put it back in.

*Without that story, there was no explaining why I had come this far to fetch my mother. It was only after watching my friend die that I understood the weight of the word preservation. Without that story, I thought then, the main thing I meant to say would lose its force.*

Third, the main thing.

*Let's go together now. Five words.*

I wrote in Chapter 1 that I first settled on those five words that night when I was nine. After that, this line nearly changed several times. At twelve I considered switching it to "I've come to bring you back." At fourteen I thought of adding "I'm grown now too." In the end I returned, every time, to the original words.

*I couldn't explain the reason then. I know now. All the other words were words that explained me. "Let's go together now" was the only one that didn't explain me at all. It was a phrase aimed wholly at them. That's why I never changed it, to the end.*

I folded and unfolded that paper again and again. My hands grew damp.

---

Through the waiting room window I could see the yard. A few small trees stood there. Though the wind was blowing, the leaves barely stirred.

*I found that strange too, at the time. The same kind as the unflickering lamps I noted in Chapter 4. I didn't know the reason, that day. I know now. This city simply doesn't have strong wind to begin with. Not by chance, but because of the terrain. The valley drew all the wind down into itself.*

I watched that tree and counted time. How many times the leaves stirred. When I reached twenty, the officer came back.

"…A little longer, please."

"…Is something wrong?"

"…No. It just takes some time, procedurally."

I believed that. I had reason to. I had already learned that the people of this city don't lie.

*I still believe those words. From that day on, I never once heard a lie in this city. Only, the truth wasn't always the shape I wanted.*

---

The waiting room door opened once and closed again. It seemed someone had come into the wrong room and left. In that brief moment I saw a person standing in the corridor. An older man. His face was pale.

I would come to remember that face for a long time. It had no expression to speak of. Neither sad nor glad — just a face that looked entirely used up.

*I didn't know, then, what had caused that face. I couldn't have guessed it was the face of someone just finishing a visit. It was only late that afternoon, looking into a mirror, that I found my own face had come to look like that man's.*

I also saw what he held in his hand. A small handkerchief. The hand holding it was not the least bit wet.

*I didn't find that strange, at the time. I simply couldn't imagine, then, that a handkerchief used to wipe tears could stay dry.*

---

While waiting, I thought about other things too.

*Then, for the first time, I tested myself on whether I remembered my mother's voice exactly. I closed my eyes and tried to bring her voice to mind. It came. Exactly, it came. That was a relief to me.*

*I tried to bring my brother's voice to mind too. He had been eight then, so I didn't know how much his voice would have changed. I tried and gave it up. Only the eight-year-old's voice came to mind. I couldn't imagine what it might sound like now.*

*I didn't know, then, what that small snag meant. I know now. My brother's voice really was exactly the eight-year-old's voice, still. It wasn't a failure of my imagination. My memory had been exact.*

While at it, I counted other things too. The four days it had taken to get here, the three settlements I'd passed through in those four days, the four people whose names I'd heard on the road. The numbers grew the more I counted.

*That was strangely reassuring. What could be counted was still something I could hold on to.*

I thought about something else too. Would my mother recognize me. I had gone from nine years old to sixteen. I had shot up in height, my voice had deepened, my face had changed. My uncle, a few months before, had looked at me and said, "I wouldn't have known you."

*That was the first time I took that worry seriously, in the waiting room. For seven years I had thought only of my mother staying the same, and had hardly given a thought to the fact that I had changed. That was strange. I simply hadn't felt the change, because I saw myself in a mirror every day.*

*That day, in the waiting room, I asked myself that question for the first time. What if I'm the only one who changed. I would learn the answer soon.*

---

About an hour in, I heard sound from the next room.

Not voices. Sounds like a door opening and closing, footsteps. I listened closely.

*I would learn later that this room was the passage leading to the visiting room. That day I counted how many times that door opened and closed. Three times. Three people, it turned out, had gone before me.*

The officer came again and said,

"…Almost ready. Just a little more."

"…What preparation is being made?"

The woman looked at me a moment, then said,

"…It takes time, bringing her in."

"…Bringing her in? From far away?"

"…Not that far."

I sensed something vague in that answer. But I didn't press further, then. My mind was elsewhere.

*I would only understand what that vagueness was much, much later. I'll write that story later in this book. Here I only note that I failed to notice it that day.*

Once the officer left, I was alone again. The room was quiet. So quiet that my own breathing sounded loud.

*In that quiet I noticed something strange. Ever since arriving in this city, I had always felt my own breathing, my own footsteps, unusually loud. With every other sound so low, only my own sound stood out. There were times it felt like proof that I was the only living person in this city. It was an illusion. Everyone was alive. Only I was noisy.*

I closed my eyes and brought my mother's face to mind again. This time not the voice, but the face. The set of her eyes, her nose, the corners of her mouth. I called up that face piece by piece, as if fitting it together.

*I thought I remembered that face exactly. But when I opened the door and actually saw that face, I found there was a very fine difference between the face I remembered and the real one. What that difference was, I'll write in Chapter 9.*

---

I looked over the paper again.

The third item, the main thing. Let's go together now.

*I practiced those five words aloud, for the last time, in that waiting room. Very quietly, so only my own ears could hear.*

"…Let's go together now."

The words sounded strangely awkward. Words I had practiced for four days — no, for seven years — and yet, spoken aloud, they felt foreign.

*I thought, at the time, that the strangeness was from nerves. Later I would learn there was another reason. Those words had been written with a nine-year-old's heart. Spoken from a sixteen-year-old's mouth, they simply didn't fit.*

I meant to say it once more but gave it up. I had thought a second try would improve it, but it seemed it would only feel more foreign.

*I folded that sentence up as it was and put it away inside myself.*

---

I fidgeted with the flask. Opened and closed the cap.

*I drank exactly one mouthful of water in that waiting room. I learned that day, for the first time, that your throat goes dry when you're nervous. I was probably the only person in this city who felt thirst.*

Looking at that flask, I had a thought too. Should I show it to my mother. If I told her my uncle had given it to me and I'd carried it all the way here, would she be glad.

*I secretly added that as a fourth item. I didn't write it on the paper. Only in my mind. I didn't know, then, that it would turn out to be the most useless item among all the words I'd prepared.*

Through the window the sun had tilted a little further. I gauged the length of the shadow. A little past noon. On my second day in this city, at the end of a road I'd walked for four days, I had finally arrived at that hour.

The officer opened the door and came in again.

"…It's ready."

I sprang up at those words. I folded the paper again and put it inside my shirt. My hands trembled.

"…This way, please."

I followed her out into the corridor. My steps felt strangely heavy. Steps I had waited seven years for, and yet now that I actually walked them, my legs wouldn't quite obey.

*I can still picture that corridor exactly. It wasn't a long corridor. There were three doors. I stopped before the middle one.*

The other two doors were also closed. I wondered whether someone like me was behind them too. Maybe someone else stood there clutching a flask like me, folding and unfolding a paper like me.

*I learned later that my guess wasn't wrong. Those three doors in that corridor always held people carrying a similarly weighted burden. I didn't know that, that day. I thought I was the only one carrying this weight.*

The officer pointed at the door.

"…Here. Go in."

I took hold of the doorknob. My hand was cold.

*I still remember the feel of that doorknob. Smooth. Cold. I took one deep breath before opening that door.*

I touched the paper inside my shirt once more. Still there. The greeting, everything that had happened since, the main thing. Seven years' worth of words, even the order all decided.

*In that moment I believed that paper would protect me. That if I only spoke in order, only did as I'd prepared, this reunion would turn out just as I had pictured it. I would learn, the moment I opened that door, that this belief was wrong.*

The officer said quietly from behind me.

"…Take your time."

Those words were strangely kind. They didn't sound like something said as a matter of procedure. I nodded at them and pushed the door.
