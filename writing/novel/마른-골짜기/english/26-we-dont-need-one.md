# Chapter 26. We Don't Need One

> _Previously_ — Speon began digging a well at the eastern edge of the
> city. No one had asked him to. "Water that just sits rots. It has to
> flow to stay alive." That was all he said. Every day I went and watched,
> sharing my water with him. Eikon passed by once and looked without a
> word.

From the second night on, word began to spread.

The way rumor traveled in this city was different from outside. Outside,
a rumor grows as it spreads. A story about one person dying can go around
a village once and come back as three people dying. Nothing like that
happened here. However many people passed a sentence along, it never
lost a single syllable from the original. Someone's digging a well at the
eastern edge. That sentence went all the way around the city and came
back unchanged.

*I found that remarkable. A city that doesn't lie has rumors that travel
honestly too. They don't swell and they don't shrink. So in this city
there was never any reason to be frightened by a rumor. You were only
ever as frightened as the fact itself. And in this case, the fact wasn't
a frightening one. Someone's digging. That was all it was.*

On the third morning, someone came.

Not the station attendant. His clothes were different — gray, with a
small emblem on the collar. I'd seen that emblem before, on the
Curator's building.

The man stopped in front of the pit and looked down at Speon.

"…What are you doing here?"

"…Digging a well." Speon answered without pausing his shovel.

"…Do you have a permit for this?"

"…No."

The man nodded. His face wasn't angry. Just a face confirming a fact.

"…This city doesn't need a well," he said. "The preserved have no need
of water, and the living are supplied their fixed ration at the
distribution station."

"…I know that."

"…Then why are you digging?"

Speon paused his shovel for a moment. He brushed off dirt and looked at
the man.

"…Not needing it isn't the same as not digging."

The man nodded again at that. He didn't argue. He just wrote something in
what looked like a notebook.

"…Understood. This isn't dangerous work, so I won't stop it. I'm simply
informing you. It isn't needed."

Then he turned and left.

Watching him walk away, I felt a little foolish for having expected
something momentous. Only then did I notice my palms had gone sweaty. I'd
seen this kind of situation many times outside. Someone does something
without permission, people come to stop them, an argument breaks out,
sometimes it even comes to blows. I must have been waiting for a scene
like that. But nothing happened here. A man came, stated one fact, and
left.

*I've gone back over that exchange in my head more times than I can
count. I'd been expecting a fight. I'd expected him to say get a permit,
or stop this. He demanded nothing at all. He simply stated a fact and
left. It isn't needed. That single line sounded more absolute than any
order could have.*

---

That afternoon, someone else came.

An older woman. She was passing by, then stopped and peered into the
pit. There was a little curiosity in her face — a rare expression in this
city.

"…What's this?" she asked.

"…A well," Speon answered.

"…What's a well?"

I was startled by the question. It was the first time I'd met someone who
didn't know what a well was. Speon wasn't startled at all.

"…A way of getting water from the ground."

"…Ah." The woman nodded. "I thought water came from the distribution
station."

"…Station water comes from somewhere too. It's stored there. This is
water that just comes straight up from the ground."

The woman thought for a moment.

"…So you don't need to store it?"

"…No. Because it keeps coming."

The woman turned that over in her expression. Then she said,

"…But I've never once been short of water. I always get exactly what I
need, when I need it."

"…I'm sure you have," Speon said.

"…Then why is this needed?"

Speon picked his shovel back up.

"…Maybe you'll never need it."

The woman turned away, looking satisfied with that answer. It hadn't
been an argument. It was a conversation in which both of them had
agreed the other was right. I found that stranger still.

*People in this city don't lie. So the woman's "I don't need it" had
been true too. She really had never once gone without. And Speon hadn't
lied either. "Maybe you'll never need it" was something he really
thought, when he said it. Both of them were honest. And yet the two of
them were looking at entirely different things.*

---

That evening, Eikon came again.

This time she wasn't passing through. She walked straight up to the pit.
She saw me and, instead of a greeting, asked this.

"…Why are you helping?"

"…It's fun."

"…What's fun about it? Why make something no one needs?"

I didn't know what to say to that, so I looked at Speon. Speon kept
digging and just listened to the two of us talk.

"…It might turn out to be needed," I said.

"…I don't lack anything," Eikon said. Her voice wasn't angry. It was the
voice of someone genuinely curious. "It's not just me. Everyone here is
the same way. So why is this needed?"

I hadn't prepared an answer to that question. I couldn't say she was
wrong. It was true — no one in this city was going without water, no one
was struggling for it.

"…I don't know," I said honestly.

Eikon nodded at that, as if satisfied. It wasn't that she was mocking me.
It was more like a confirmation that her own thinking had been right.

"…Then why dig, if you don't know?"

"…Because Speon is digging."

"…Is that a reason?"

I couldn't answer that one either. Eikon looked down into the pit for a
moment. Then she turned and walked away. She hadn't been angry, and she
hadn't been mocking. It was simply the walk of someone who hadn't gotten
an answer to her question and was going back.

*I turned that question over all that night. Is that a reason. Truly, I
had no reason beyond the fact that Speon was digging. And yet it hadn't
felt like an insufficient one. That was strange. It couldn't be
explained by logic, but my mind was already made up.*

---

On the fourth day, the station attendant himself came.

When I went to refill my flask, he spoke first, before I could.

"…That man digging the well — do you know him?"

"…Yes."

"…We've had a few inquiries. Whether the ration needs adjusting."

"…What did you tell them?"

"…That it doesn't. The current ration is sufficient."

I wanted to say something to that, but nothing came out. It was true that
the ration was sufficient. No one died of thirst. No one got angry
because the line came up short. The system was running perfectly.

*Why does a perfectly running system need new water? I ran into that
question every time, and every time I stalled. This city had no words to
answer it with. The ration wasn't lacking. Whatever the well was needed
for, it wasn't a shortage. I still hadn't managed to put that into
words.*

As he filled my flask, the attendant added,

"…But he keeps digging, I hear."

"…Yes."

"…No reason to stop him. It's not dangerous."

"…No."

"…It's just a little remarkable, that's all. Doing it while knowing it
isn't needed."

Hearing that, I realized this was the first time someone in this city had
felt Speon to be 'remarkable.' Not anger, not interest — some feeling
somewhere in between.

---

Late that afternoon, three children came running up.

There were children in this city too. Living children, not yet
preserved. They were a little different from the adults. They crouched
in front of the pit, eyes bright, and asked.

"…Is real water actually going to come out of this?"

"…It will," Speon said.

"…When?"

"…Don't know."

"…If you don't know, why dig?"

I nearly laughed at that. The children were asking the same question the
adults asked, only in a different tone. The adults asked it and turned
away. The children asked it and didn't turn away.

"…Because it's fun." Speon said. "Want to try?"

One child reached for the shovel, but another grabbed his arm.

"…No. We have to be back at our set time."

"…There's still time left."

"…Still no."

Listening to that exchange, something in me turned strange. These
children were growing older. Not yet preserved, and maybe someday to be.
By the time this well was finished, some of them might already be doing
something else entirely. Or they might already have gone toward the
Preserve.

*I remembered those children's faces for a long time afterward. Curious
faces, and yet faces that turned back exactly on schedule. This city was
precise even with its children. It didn't stop their curiosity. It only
allowed exactly as much as was set.*

The children waved and ran off — right on time, exactly. Watching them
go, I thought about how, at that age, I'd played in the water without a
thought for the time.

---

On the fifth day, I asked Speon.

"…People keep saying it isn't needed. Are you all right with that?"

"…I'm fine."

"…Doesn't it make you angry?"

Speon drove the shovel into the dirt pile and rested a moment.

"…No reason to be angry. They're right."

"…Right? But it's needed. Water is."

"…Not right now. Really. The ration is enough, and the preserved don't
drink. What they're saying isn't a lie."

That answer frustrated me.

"…Then why do you keep digging?"

Speon looked at me. There wasn't a trace of irritation or impatience in
his eyes.

"…Not needing it now and never needing it aren't the same thing."

I turned that over for a long time. That it wasn't needed now was a fact
everyone in this city agreed on. That it would never be needed — no one
had ever confirmed that. This city believed it would go on forever exactly
as it was now. And that was the city's pride, and its premise. That
nothing changes.

"…This city doesn't look like it's going to change," I said.

"…It looks that way," Speon said. "But looking like it doesn't change
isn't the same as not changing. It just means the reason to change
hasn't come yet."

I turned that over for a long time too.

*I heard it, at first, as an argument meant to persuade. I know better
now. It wasn't persuasion. Speon wasn't trying to convince anyone. He
was simply doing what he believed was right, whether people said it was
needed or not. Persuasion demands a response. Speon demanded no response
at all.*

---

Through those six days, I measured the pit's depth every day. There was
no proper ruler, so I used my own forearm. The first day, waist-high; the
second, chest-high; the third, half my own height; the fourth, a little
past half. By the fifth day, Speon had to stand fully inside the pit
before even his head was visible.

*I wrote down every one of those numbers. I didn't know why, at the time.
Thinking of it now, it's because this city had exactly one thing that was
changing. The people didn't change. The ration didn't change. The visit
procedures didn't change. Only the pit grew a little deeper every day. I
was watching something change, for the first time in a long while. That's
why I wanted to count it.*

That evening, I walked along turning over what the station attendant had
said. Remarkable. Doing it while knowing it isn't needed. The words kept
circling in my head. Only then did I realize how rare a feeling
remarkable was in this city. Surprise was rare, fear was rare, joy was
rare. The people of this city lived among things they already fully
knew. Being remarkable is a feeling that needs something unknown to exist
at all.

*I pictured the attendant's face again. He'd said the ration was
sufficient, and in the same breath called it remarkable that Speon kept
digging. Both feelings lived inside one person at once. I think that was
the most honest reaction this city had. Not needed, and still
remarkable. Neither one was a lie.*

When I reached the pit, Speon was still digging, late into the evening. I
stood beside him that night, for the first time, and told him everything
I'd seen so far.

That evening, for the first time, I got angry at Speon. Or not exactly
angry — my words just came out sounding angry.

"…It's strange," I said. "No one gets angry, no one stops him, they just
say it isn't needed. I find that more frightening than anything."

"…Why is that frightening?"

"…If people got angry, you could fight them. There's nothing to fight
here. Because everything they're saying is true."

Speon set down his shovel and looked at me for a long while.

"…Are you talking about this city right now, or about the well?"

I couldn't answer that right away. It was both. Or maybe it had never
been two separate things to begin with.

"…I don't know," I said.

"…Neither do I," Speon said. "But once you do know, that's when the
reason to dig becomes real. Right now it's just your body doing the
digging."

I fell asleep without understanding what he meant.

---

On the sixth morning, a piece of paper was stuck into the ground in front
of the pit.

It was an official document from the Preserve. I read it first — I read
faster than Speon did.

> Notice regarding excavation work at this location. The current water
> supply system is operating normally across all sectors, and no
> assessment has found that additional water sources are needed.
> However, as the work itself has been confirmed to pose no hazard, no
> separate action will be taken to stop it. — Office of the Curator

I read the document aloud to Speon. He listened without stopping his
digging.

"…What do you think?"

"…About what?"

"…Doesn't it make you angry?"

"…Why would this make me angry. It's all true."

"…It says they're not even going to stop you."

"…Right. They won't. In this city, people stop something when they have
a reason, and don't when they don't. Right now there's no reason to
stop me."

I set the paper carefully on top of the dirt pile, weighing it down with
a stone so it wouldn't blow away.

*I still have that document. It's a document I can't resent anyone over.
There's not one lie in it, and not one threat. It simply states the
facts, exactly as they are. That's how this city fights. It fights by
not fighting.*

There was a signature at the bottom of the document. Office of the
Curator. Seeing that name, I thought of the Curator himself. The man who
had explained the release procedure to me. Kind, and worn thin. I found
myself wondering whether he'd written this document himself, or whether
someone had written it for him.

*I didn't hold onto that question for long, at the time. I learned later.
He was a man who wrote documents like this in his own hand. He didn't
leave it to others. Whatever he decided, he put his own name to it. I
would come to understand why, much later, the first time I saw his face
turn sad.*

I read the paper over once more. Every sentence was exact. No
exaggeration, no understatement. I couldn't explain, at the time, why
that exactness felt so chilling. I can now. An exact statement has no
gaps in it. And with no gap, there's nowhere to dig in.

---

That afternoon, I sat on the edge of the pit for the first time, swinging
my legs, and thought.

No one in this city hated Speon. No one tried to drive him out. Everyone,
politely, precisely, only ever said it wasn't needed. And every word of
that was true.

I sensed then, dimly, that this was a stronger defense than any
argument. Getting angry means acknowledging your opponent — admitting
they're worth fighting. This city didn't grant Speon even that much
acknowledgment. It simply treated him as something that had nothing to
do with it.

*Speon accepted that treatment. He never felt wronged by it. I felt, for
the first time, caught between those two postures. The city's quiet
indifference, and Speon's quiet persistence. Neither one showed anger.
And yet they were exact opposites.*

As the sun began to lean toward evening, Speon climbed out of the pit and
brushed off his hands.

"…Want to try digging a little yourself tomorrow?" he asked.

I couldn't answer right away. I wanted to. But it struck me as strange
how quickly I'd come to want it. I'd come here to bring my family back. I
hadn't come here to dig a well.

"…I'm not here to work," I said.

"…I know," Speon said. "What does that have to do with trying a shovel
once?"

I couldn't answer that either. I'd thought there was some connection.
When I tried to put it into words, there wasn't one.

"…I'll think about it."

Speon smiled. It was the first time I'd seen him do that. Deep lines
gathered at the corners of his eyes — the lines of a man who had laughed
a great deal, over a long life.

"…No need to think. Your body will decide on its own."

I heard that and refilled the flask and handed it to him. He drank and
gave it back. We sat a while without speaking.

On the way back to the dormitory, I went back over everything that had
happened in those six days, one by one. The official who'd asked for a
permit, the old woman who hadn't known what a well was, the station
attendant, Eikon, the children, and one sheet of paper. No one had gotten
angry. No one had stopped Speon. And yet, over those six days, the whole
city had been saying the same thing to him, over and over. That it wasn't
needed.

*That night I thought I finally understood why those words frightened
me. It isn't needed wasn't a wrong thing to say. That's why it couldn't
be argued with. Watching a man keep digging, surrounded on every side by
words that couldn't be argued with — that was the first time I wanted to
go on watching someone for a long, long while. And I still didn't know
where that feeling had come from.*

Lying in my room, I looked at my palms. Clean hands, without a single
blister. Looking at them, I counted, for the first time, how long these
hands had gone doing nothing at all. From the day I arrived in the city
to today — twenty-nine days. In all that time I had walked, stood,
stood in lines, filled out forms. The only things these hands had ever
gripped were paperwork and a flask.

*I fell asleep looking at that hand. The next morning, the moment I
opened my eyes, the first thing that came to mind wasn't my mother, and
it wasn't my brother. It was the pit. I didn't think that was strange, at
the time. Looking back on it now, I should have.*
