# Chapter 5. Perpetuity

> _Previously_ — Anemos learned the city had no well. The preserved have no need of water. He became the only person receiving a ration. He was told he would first have to meet the Curator before applying for a visit. The Curator, they said, was busy these days.

The reception officer handed me a sheet of paper.

"…Read this before your visit. Everyone reads it."

It had a title. "Concerning Perpetuity."

I stood right there and read it.

---

The first paragraph read like this.

> Perpetuity is the system operated by the Preserve. It preserves the consciousness and memory of registered subjects without damage. The preserved converse, remember, and judge. They do not disappear, and they do not suffer.

I read that sentence over several times. They do not disappear, and they do not suffer. Those two phrases sounded to me like the best words in the world.

*I could find no reason to doubt that sentence then. I can find none now either. That sentence was not a lie. In this city I never once found any evidence that this sentence was false. That is the difficulty of this book.*

Holding that paper, I thought of the man in the next room. The one whose brother had "become" preserved three years before. Passing him on my way out of the reception office, I brought up this paper.

"…Have you read this?" I asked.

"…When I first came. Everyone reads it."

"…Do you believe it? What's written here?"

The man thought a moment, then said,

"…There's nothing to believe or not believe. Go talk to my brother and you'll see. It's real."

"…What's real?"

"…The remembering. He remembers everything about when I was little. Even things I've forgotten."

That answer set my mind at ease. The unease that this city might be deceiving me about something eased a little.

*I would confirm later that this man's words were accurate. The preserved really do remember. I never once overturn that fact in this book. There was never anything to overturn.*

---

I moved on to the second paragraph.

> Perpetuity does not evaluate the preserved. It does not ask their qualifications. It does not weigh what their life before preservation was like, or what weight that life carried. Every registered person is preserved in the same manner, with the same diligence.

Reading that paragraph, I felt strangely at ease.

*Until then I had unconsciously assumed this city would screen for something. In our settlement, even to receive a single thing, your qualifications were weighed. Had you been a good person, had you been of use to the village, did you owe any debt. Even of the dead, people said such things. He was a good man. She was a person too soon lost.*

*This city didn't do that. At first that was simply reassuring news to me. Later I would learn that this was the most frightening part of this city. I won't write that part yet, here.*

I asked the reception officer,

"…So you'll preserve a bad person too?"

"…Yes. The registration standard is condition. Not the person."

"…What does that mean?"

"…Whether damage has begun in the living body — that's the standard. What kind of person they were isn't looked at."

That explanation gave me a strange sense of reassurance.

*I had long been used to things I couldn't have because I lacked the qualifications for them. Outside, a great many things were a matter of qualification. This city had no such standard at all. I liked that. I still admit that the feeling itself was good.*

---

The third paragraph.

> Perpetuity does not judge. It does not ask after sin. It does not weigh. It only preserves.

I read this sentence over a second time.

*I would learn later that this sentence stands in exact contrast to the systems of three earlier cities. I won't tell those cities' stories in this record. I'll only note that each of those cities had something that measured. There was a Scales. There was a Roll. Here, there was nothing.*

*I didn't know, then, how strange that nothingness was. I thought everyone would welcome hearing that there was no evaluation. So did I. I still think, even now, that this sentence itself is a kind one. Kinder than any system I had heard of before.*

There was a fourth paragraph too.

> The preserved have no need of new experience. This is not a defect but a stability. The purpose of this system is that the state at the moment of preservation remains exactly as it was.

I had no particular thought on first reading this paragraph. The word "stability" sounded to me like nothing but a good word.

*I would come to reread this paragraph many times later. That stability and stillness could be the same word — I couldn't think that, then. This is the sentence that hides most quietly in this entire book. I now think it is also the most frightening sentence.*

Seeing my face, the reception officer asked,

"…Is something bothering you?"

"…No. It's just… good, what it says."

"…That's right. It's a good thing to say."

The way that person answered, I felt something in the tone. Not so much conviction as repetition. It sounded like an answer given many times before.

*I didn't notice that repetition then. Later I would come to make the same repetition myself. It's a good thing to say. Every time someone asked me how a visit had gone. In the exact same tone the man in the next room had used when he told me it was good.*

---

Seeing that I had finished reading the paper, the reception officer asked,

"…Anything you're curious about?"

"…What is Perpetuity made of? Is it a machine?"

"…A bit different from calling it a machine, but you can think of it that way."

"…Who made it?"

The person's face turned thoughtful for a moment.

"…I don't really know that either. I only know it was made a very long time ago."

I didn't find that answer strange. Our settlement, too, had a well that no one knew exactly when had been dug.

*I didn't know then that this not-knowing had spread through this entire city. No one knew how Perpetuity had begun. Not even the Curator. Whether they knew and simply weren't telling, or truly didn't know, was never made certain by the end of this book.*

---

I folded the paper and sat a while in the shade of a tree near the reception office. There I overheard two people talking. Both were people of this city.

"…I heard that house's daughter-in-law came by yesterday," one said.

"…Yes, I heard."

"…First time in three years, they say."

"…I see."

That was all. The conversation went no further. I found that strange. In our settlement, that conversation would have gone on much longer. What it meant to come after three years, what had happened in the meantime, why she had come only now.

*I recalled that short exchange again later. Those two people weren't curious. They attached no feeling at all to the fact of three years. Neither surprise nor regret. They only exchanged the bare fact.*

*I know it wasn't rudeness. To those two people, three years and three days truly made no great difference. That not judging could show up this way too — I saw that for the first time in that tree's shade.*

---

"…Can I ask one more thing?" I said.

"…Yes."

"…You said there's no judgment. Then is there no one who's refused preservation?"

"…No one. Anyone who wants it is accepted."

"…What about someone who's accepted without wanting it?"

At that question, the person hesitated a little for the first time.

"…That… it can only begin if the person themselves wants it. Registration requires consent."

"…What if they're in a state where they can't consent? Too sick to."

"…In that case a family member can apply on their behalf."

I nodded at that answer. I thought of how my mother and my brother had been preserved that way. Both had had such high fevers they were in no condition to decide anything for themselves.

*I thought, then, that I had fully understood this answer. Later I would learn that this word "consent" is the heaviest word in this city. I'll write that story in Chapter 18.*

I asked one more thing.

"…Can I be preserved too? Right now."

The reception officer looked at me. There was no sign of surprise. This question, too, didn't seem to be a first.

"…In principle, yes. I told you the registration standard is condition. Right now you're in good health, so you're not a candidate, but if you wish, you can apply anyway."

"…Even in good health?"

"…Even in good health, we don't block the application. We just don't encourage it, either."

"…Why don't you encourage it?"

The person looked at me a moment, then answered.

"…Because you still have something left to lose."

I didn't understand that answer right there.

*I would take those words out and turn them over many times later. You still have something left to lose. I tried, then, to count what I stood to lose. I counted everything that could be counted. That something uncountable remained — I didn't know that, then.*

---

At the very bottom of the paper was a fifth paragraph.

> Family and acquaintances may visit at any time. Without any separate reservation or waiting, you may visit whenever you wish.

Reading this sentence, I felt a catch in my throat right there. Any time. I had waited seven years for that word.

"…Is this real?" I asked. "Can I really go any time at all?"

"…Yes. So long as you follow the procedure."

"…Does the procedure take long?"

"…Meeting with the Curator is the first step. After that it goes quickly."

I nodded at that answer. I was glad to hear that only the first step had to be gotten past.

*I didn't know then. That this word "any time" carried, along with its kindness, another face. Being able to go any time meant that if you didn't go today, you could go tomorrow; if you didn't go this month, you could go next month. It was a phrase that made all urgency disappear. I would come to see, later, just how slowly that phrase lets a person drift away.*

I folded that paper and put it inside my shirt.

*I still have this paper. Writing this record now, I spread it open on my desk and read it again. Not one sentence has changed. I have changed.*

Walking outside, I turned those paragraphs over again. Does not judge. Does not ask qualifications. Does not ask after sin. Preserves with the same diligence.

*That night, for the first time, I thought this: if a system like this had existed in our village seven years ago — no, eleven years ago — could my father have been preserved too? My father's disappearance wasn't from sickness. I said in Chapter 1 that I wouldn't write that story at length in this record. I keep that promise even now.*

*I'll set down only this much. I can no longer remember my father's face well. I remember my mother's face, but my father's face has gone blurred. I find that difference frightening. The blurring itself. This city was telling me it could take that very blurring away. I was drawn to that promise. I am not ashamed of that pull, even now.*

Walking back to the lodging that night, I thought for the first time that this city might be a good place.

*That thought wasn't wrong. This city was a good place. I don't want to paint this city as a bad place, to the end of this book. But being a good place, and giving me what I wanted, turned out to be two different questions. I wouldn't understand that difference until much, much later.*

Walking to the lodging, I counted. How many sentences on that paper ended in "does not." Six. Does not evaluate. Does not weigh. Does not judge. Does not ask after sin. Does not measure. Does not need.

*I walked counting those six negative sentences. I wrote in Chapter 2 that counting was a habit. This time the counting did something else. It confirmed to me that all six were good things to say. That confirmation was, that night, a comfort to me.*

*Now I count those six negatives again. And I think this. A system that says six times what it doesn't do is a system that has decided, six times, not to do something. That the deciding itself was a choice — it took me a very long time to notice that.*

---

On the way back to the lodging, I passed the fountain again. The waterless fountain I had seen that afternoon.

I stopped in front of it for a moment. And I recalled the sentences I had read that afternoon. Does not judge. Does not weigh. Only preserves.

I opened the flask and drank a little of what remained. Before that fountain, only the sound of my own flask sounded. The cap opening, the water sloshing. In that narrow plaza, that sound rang unusually loud.

*I should have noticed something in that moment. That not weighing and having no flow at all might be the same thing. I didn't notice, then. I only walked on thinking those sentences were kind.*

*I still think those sentences are kind. Even now, having finished writing this whole book. Only now I know that being kind and keeping someone alive are not the same thing.*

I closed the cap and hung the flask back on my shoulder. Walking the rest of the way to the lodging, I thought that tomorrow I would meet the Curator. I said that name to myself a few times in my mouth. The Curator.

*I said that name then with no feeling at all attached to it. It wasn't until the next day, seeing that face, that feeling began to attach to it.*
