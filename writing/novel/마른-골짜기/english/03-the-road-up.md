# Chapter 3. The Road Up

> _Previously_ — Anemos walked the four-day road, retracing his seven years outside: seventeen funerals, the death of the old man who taught him to dig wells, Gaon who had taken him in as family. Leaving all of it behind him, he turned his steps toward the last hill.

From the top of the hill, they'd told him, you could see the city.

The old man had said it. The last old man he'd met. I believed those words and walked toward that hill the whole afternoon of the fourth day.

Around sundown I reached the top.

*I remember that moment exactly. I was out of breath, my legs ached, and the flask was nearly empty. And when I raised my head, the first thought that came was not about the city.*

*It was the thought that it was quiet.*

---

The city sat on the flat ground above the valley.

It was less a wall than a low fence, ringing the place. The roofs stood in neat rows, all of a similar color. Not scattered in every color the way our settlement's were.

I looked at the sight for a long while. At first I thought it was beautiful.

*Thinking back now, that impression wasn't wrong. It really was beautiful. The trouble was that I only learned, much later, where that beauty came from.*

Before the sun went fully down, I started down the hill. About half a day's distance remained to the city.

As I went down, I listened.

Our settlement made sound even at night. Dogs barking, the well bucket, someone walking, a child crying. I had grown up inside those sounds.

This city had none of them.

*I put it down to distance at the time. I thought I simply couldn't hear it yet, still being far off. That it would come once I got closer.*

*It didn't come, even once I got closer.*

---

I stood a while longer on the hill, until the sun had fully gone down.

Seen from a distance, the city wasn't large. Only a little bigger than our settlement. That surprised me. For seven years I had imagined that city as far larger. I had thought of it as a place that could hold everything, and so guessed its size must be just as great.

*I learned later that size didn't matter. What this city held wasn't a matter of volume.*

A few streets showed between the roofs. Straight streets. Not a single one curved. I found that strange too. Every street in our settlement curved. As houses were added, the streets grew to follow them.

*This city's streets had been laid out fixed from the start. There was no need for them to grow. Since no more people were being born, there was no need to build more houses either.*

*I found myself wondering, right then, whether there were any children in this city. I'll write the answer later.*

I camped that night at the foot of the hill.

I meant to light a fire, but I gave it up. The city was too close. I had learned outside that lighting a fire when a stranger is nearby draws the eye.

I shook the flask. Little water was left. I drank it sparingly.

*That night, for the first time, I wondered whether there might be a well inside the city too. I assumed there must be. A place where people lived couldn't be without a well.*

*I would learn the next day that this assumption was wrong.*

The night sky was clear. Many stars. I counted stars until I fell asleep. That night I counted up to thirty-two. I fell asleep before I reached thirty-three.

*I dreamed that night. I don't remember what the dream was. Only that when I woke, a feeling lingered as if I had heard the sound of water. I still don't know whether that was a dream or real. That night I was still outside the wall, so it could have been real.*

---

The next morning I stepped onto the road that led into the city.

The road was strangely clean. Our settlement's roads were always cluttered with wheel ruts, footprints, animal tracks. This road had none of that. It looked like a road no one walked at all.

*And yet people did walk it. I passed three groups that morning. It was a road that people walked, and yet left no trace. I found that strange too, at the time, and let it pass.*

The first I passed was a man pulling a handcart. I stopped him and asked.

"…Is this the road to the Preserve?"

"…It is."

"…How much further?"

"…Half a day."

The man said nothing more and went on. I learned later that his short answer hadn't been unkindness. That was simply all he had to say.

*I'll set down here that most of the people I met in this city were like that. Not unkind. Just saying only what was needed. At first it felt cold to me. Later it came to feel, instead, honest.*

The cart carried cloth. All of it white or pale gray. I found that strange too. In our settlement people were proud of dyed cloth. Red cloth, blue cloth. The more it caught the eye, the better it was treated.

*I would notice later that I had seen almost no eye-catching color anywhere in this city. Brightness wasn't forbidden. No one simply made it. There was no one who wanted to catch the eye.*

---

Before noon, I passed a man walking with two children.

The children walked quietly. In our settlement, children that age would already have run around and shouted a good ten times over that distance. These children simply walked along behind their father.

"…Your children are very well-behaved," I said, striking up conversation as I passed.

The man glanced at me and said,

"…Not well-behaved. Just walking."

I thought that answer strange. I didn't know why "well-behaved" and "just walking" would be different.

*I would come to understand the difference later. To be well-behaved means holding back the urge to run. This city's children weren't holding anything back. They simply had no urge to run in the first place.*

*I learned later that the man wasn't from this city either. He too was on his way to visit someone. That he knew the difference was because he, like me, had come from outside.*

---

The second I passed was an older woman.

She was walking alone. Almost no luggage. I spoke to her too.

"…Visiting someone?"

The woman stopped and looked at me.

"…How did you know?"

"…There's nowhere else to go, on this road."

The woman laughed. A slightly sad laugh.

"…That's right. Going to see my son."

"…How long has it been?"

"…Three months."

I was surprised by that answer. Three months seemed short, I thought. For my family it had been seven years.

"…You go often, then."

The woman said, walking again,

"…I went every week, at first. Now it's once every three months."

"…Why did it taper off?"

The woman didn't answer that right away. Only after a few more steps did she say,

"…Go and see. You'll know once you go."

*I was hearing that answer for the third time now. The peddler, the old man, and now this woman. The same words, all three times. Only then did I begin to feel a little uneasy. Why does no one tell me in advance?*

*Now I know. It wasn't that they couldn't. It was that it couldn't be carried in words. It was something you could only know by going through it, and everyone who had gone through it in this city already knew that.*

I walked alongside that woman for a while. She asked me things first — where I was from, who I was going to see, how old I was.

"…Sixteen."

"…Did you come alone?"

"…Yes."

The woman stopped walking for a moment.

"…None of your family helped you?"

"…They did. Gave me the flask, and so on. But none of them came with me."

"…Why?"

I couldn't answer that. I had never once thought, at the time, about why my uncle hadn't come along.

*I would learn later. My uncle had been to this city once. A very long time ago, to see his own parents. He never once told me that story. I set it down here for the first time in this book.*

The woman spoke again, walking on.

"…I came with my husband, at first. Then he stopped coming."

"…Why?"

"…He found it hard."

I asked no further. I still didn't know what "hard" meant. It felt right, then, not to ask.

---

Past noon, the city's fence began to come into view.

Seen up close, the fence was low. A height you could climb over if you wanted. I found that strange. It seemed less a fence meant to keep something out than one meant simply to mark a line.

*That thought wasn't wrong. This city had no need to keep people out. No one who tried to come in would bring anything from outside better than what this city already gave. The fence wasn't there to block intruders. It was only a thing that drew a line.*

Inside the fence, the roofs came into closer view. I looked among them for rising smoke. The kind that comes from cooking. I saw none at all.

*I blamed the distance again, then. Too far away to see, I thought. I'll write later about the fact that I still couldn't see it once I was close.*

Walking along the fence, I stopped and parted ways with the woman. She went toward a different gate. I learned later that I had never even asked her name.

"…Goodbye," the woman said. "I hope you see something good."

I thought that farewell strange. Hoping I would see something good sounded like a thing you say when you're not yet certain.

*I never meet that woman again in this book. But the farewell stayed with me a long time. I heard similar farewells from other people many times later. None of them wished me well with certainty. That was, in their own way, their honesty.*

I watched the woman's back until it disappeared toward the other gate. Only then did I walk my road again. Walking alone had already, in that short time, come to feel strange.

A small stream ran beside the road. Glad to see it, I went over and filled my flask.

*This was the last water I filled outside. I didn't know then that it was the last.*

While filling it, I looked down into the streambed. The water was clear. A small fish passed between the stones. I watched that fish for a long while. It was no different from a stream outside.

*I would come to find it strange, only later, that this fish lived just outside the city fence. Nothing at all lived in the water inside the fence. Though I didn't even know yet, then, whether there was a waterway inside.*

---

Following the fence, I came to a gate.

There was no gatekeeper. Instead, letters were written on a wooden board.

> Those entering, please register at the reception office.
> The Preserve welcomes all.

The lettering was straight. Not a single crooked stroke. I thought of the wooden board at our settlement hall. The lettering there was always crooked. The size changed depending on who had written it.

I read that notice twice.

*The Preserve welcomes all. I believed those words exactly as they stood. It wasn't a lie. This city truly did let anyone in. The trouble was that letting anyone in and letting anyone out were not the same statement. I would learn that much, much later.*

I crossed the gate.

I have called that moment back to mind many times since. There was no special feeling as I set foot across. No threshold, even. Outside and inside simply ran together.

*But the sound vanished completely. Outside the gate, the sound of the stream had been audible. The moment I stepped through the gate, that sound cut off.*

I turned and looked back. The stream was flowing just the same. Only it could not be heard from inside.

*That was the first time I felt real fear. What I'd felt on the hill had been vague. This was specific. From a place that made sound to a place that did not — I had just crossed a boundary.*

I stood at the threshold without moving for a while. I even tried stepping back half a pace. Stepping back, the sound came again. Stepping forward, it cut off again.

*I went back and forth in that spot a few times. I know it was a foolish thing to do. But I wanted to be sure, then, whether it was an illusion or not. It was not an illusion.*

There was no one on the road but me. The people I had passed earlier had scattered to other gates, or had already gone in. I stood alone before that gate.

*I would later come to overlay that moment with the moment I had sat behind the hill at nine years old. Both were moments of deciding whether to cross or not. Nine-year-old me did not cross. Sixteen-year-old me did. That difference is where this book begins.*

I took one deep breath and went inside.

---

Just inside the gate stood a small building. A wooden board read "Reception."

I stood before it. Before going in, I shook the flask once. It was half full.

*I never once imagined that carrying this flask would look strange. At the time it was just a flask. Later it would become the one thing in this city that set me apart from everyone else.*

Beside the building was a small flowerbed. Flowers had been planted there. White flowers, all similar in color. They hadn't wilted, and there were no insects. I touched one of the flowers. It was real. Not artificial.

*I saw that flower again many times later. Always in the same spot, always in the same state. A flower that neither bloomed nor faded. It took me a long while to understand that it was a picture explaining this entire city.*

I hung the flask back on my shoulder, pushed the door, and went inside.
