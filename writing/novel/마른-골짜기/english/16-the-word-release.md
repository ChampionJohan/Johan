# Chapter 16. The Word Release

> _Previously_ — Eikon told me about someone who had once wanted
> something more. That person had eventually applied, and after that
> there had been no word of them. She called it a **sickness**. I
> couldn't sleep that night.

The morning air was cold. In this city the air was usually about the
same temperature always, but that morning it felt unusually cold.
Maybe it was because I hadn't slept.

I tried, as I walked, to put last night's thoughts in order. They
wouldn't come into order. What Eikon had said kept surfacing out of
sequence. It's a sickness. Because no one wants that. Why would it be
sad.

*That morning, for the first time, I thought this. Whether what I now
wanted to know was really for my mother's sake, or for my own. I
couldn't make that distinction then. I walked on without making it.*

I thought, suddenly, of the water jar my mother had filled the night
before she left. I hadn't drunk from it for three days. If I drank it,
it would be gone. Even then, I'd been trying to hold on to something.
It was similar now. Only this time it wasn't a water jar — it was a
person.

As I walked, I heard the sound of gravel under my feet. It was one of
the few sounds this city had. I counted my steps as I listened to it,
and gave up somewhere past a hundred. Counting wasn't going to change
anything.

On the way to the Curator's office I asked a few people about that
word.

The first person I asked was the ration-station clerk. Filling my
flask with water, I asked, almost in passing.

"…Do you know what release is?"

The clerk's hand stopped.

"…I've heard of it."

"…What is it?"

"…I don't know exactly either. I heard it's the procedure for
reversing preservation."

"…Have you done it? Or anyone you know?"

"…No. No one."

It wasn't a lying face. They really didn't know.

---

The second person I asked was an old man I ran into at the plaza. He
was sitting on a bench, so I sat beside him and asked.

"…Have you heard of something called release?"

The old man looked at me for a long while, then said,

"…I've heard of it."

"…Do you know what it is?"

"…I know the procedure. But I've never once actually seen anyone
who's done it."

"…Why not?"

"…Because no one wants it."

I already knew that answer. It was exactly what Eikon had told me.
And yet, coming from someone else's mouth, it felt different. It
seemed less like one person's thought and more like a fact this whole
city held in common. If one person had said it, it would have been
that person's opinion. Two people saying the same thing felt less
like an opinion and more like this city's rule.

"…Have you ever thought about it yourself, sir? Release."

The old man laughed at that question. It wasn't a mocking laugh. Just
a puzzled one.

"…Why would I think about it."

*I stopped walking when I heard that answer. Why would I think about
it. That line seemed to summarize every answer in this city. No one
said it was a bad thing. They just said there was no reason to think
about it.*

The third person I asked was a little different. A middle-aged woman
I met at the garden entrance. She said she was on her way to visit her
child. When I asked her the same question, her expression stiffened
for a moment.

"…Why are you asking that?"

"…Just curious."

"…Is it because of family, by any chance?"

I was startled by that question. She seemed to have read something in
my face.

"…Yes. My mother and my brother are here."

The woman nodded, then said in a slightly lower voice,

"…I thought about it once myself. A long time ago."

"…And?"

"…I only thought about it. I never went any further."

"…Why not?"

"…Weren't you afraid? Just thinking about it?"

The woman stopped walking for a moment at that question.

"…It wasn't so much fear. I understood quickly that thinking about it
would do no good."

"…Why would it do no good?"

"…My child was three then. Still three now. What could you ask a
three-year-old?"

I felt struck from behind by that, somehow. I didn't know exactly
where it caught, but it felt like there was another wall inside this
woman's words that I still didn't know about.

She didn't answer that question any further. She just smiled and went
on her way. I didn't notice, that day, what that smile was hiding.

*I would come back to that smile later. It wasn't a smile hiding
something. It was simply that she had nothing more to say, and smiled
because of it. People in this city didn't get angry or fumble for
words when they had no answer. They just smiled.*

---

I didn't ask a fourth person. Instead I asked myself. As I walked, I
kept repeating the same question. Why was I holding on to this word
so hard? No answer came. With no answer coming, my feet kept walking
toward the Curator's office anyway.

*I counted those four as I walked. The ration-station clerk, the old
man, the woman, and myself. All four had said the same thing, each in
a different way. No one wants it. There's no reason to think about
it. There's no one to ask. I had heard all three of the others, and
still my feet didn't stop.*

The Curator's office was the building where I'd applied for my first
visit. Past the window and down the inner corridor was a small room,
where you could meet the Curator.

I'd already been in that room once. The first time I arrived in the
city, when the visiting procedures were explained to me. The Curator
I'd met then had been kind, and had looked tired. That impression
still remained.

The receptionist asked,

"…What brings you in today?"

"…I have something to ask the Curator."

"…Is it visit-related?"

"…No. Something else."

The clerk wrote my name on a form, the same way as any other visitor,
as if there were nothing unusual about it at all. I found that strange,
somehow. Looking back later, that clerk's hand, not yet knowing how
heavy the question I was about to ask was in this city, moved with
the calmest touch of anyone that day.

The clerk went to check something in the back for a moment. I sat and
waited in a chair in the waiting room. It looked like the same seat
I'd sat in the first day. I couldn't even tell whether it had been
months ago or weeks ago. In this city, time didn't count itself well.

*In that waiting room, I realized for the first time that I didn't
know exactly how many days I'd been here. I was the one who counted
things, but since coming here, that had stopped working. The days
were too alike to tell apart.*

Besides me, there were two other people in the waiting room. One was
an older man, sitting quietly with a folder resting on his knees. The
other was a young woman, tapping her knee with her fingertips again
and again.

Watching those fingertips, I wondered whether she, too, was anxious
about something. But looking again, it didn't seem like anxiety — it
seemed more like a habit. Her expression was calm. That in this city
a body's motions and a face's words could differ from each other — I
noticed that for the first time then.

Each time a name was called, one person went into the inner room.
Watching their faces when they came out, they all looked about the
same as when they'd gone in. No one looked greatly changed.

*I thought that was strange, at the time. Outside, if you went into a
government office and came back out, your expression always changed.
Whether the news was good or bad. Here, people didn't change. Probably
because whatever news they heard here fell within the range they'd
already expected. I was the only one about to walk into that room
carrying a question outside anyone's expected range.*

---

The clerk came back and said,

"…Come in."

The Curator was seated inside the room. The same seat, the same
posture as the first time I'd seen him. He smiled slightly at me.

"…It's been a while. Are the visits going well?"

"…Yes. They're going well."

The Curator studied my face for a moment. His eyes had the look of
someone trying to read something. I found that gaze uncomfortable and
also, somehow, reassuring. At least this person didn't look at me
carelessly.

On the desk sat several stacked folders, all with covers of about the
same color. My eyes went to one of them for a moment before I looked
away. It felt like peeking at someone else's papers.

"…What brings you in today?"

I started to bring out the question I'd prepared, and hesitated a
moment. I didn't know why putting that word into the air felt so
heavy.

"…I wanted to ask what release is."

---

The Curator's expression shifted, very briefly. It wasn't surprise,
and it wasn't discomfort. It looked like something older than either
of those. I couldn't read, at the time, what it was.

The room went quiet for a moment. I couldn't even hear leaves
rustling outside the window. In this city, even the wind was quiet.

"…Where did you hear that word?"

"…From someone I know. Without a real explanation of what it is."

"…From whom, if I may ask?"

"…A girl named Eikon. I met her here."

The Curator nodded slightly at that name. He seemed to know it.
Whether that was because this city was small, or for some other
reason, I didn't know.

"…Eikon is a good friend." The Curator said, with a small smile.
"I've known her a long time."

"…How long?"

"…Seven years. You end up seeing a lot, in this seat, over that
time."

Hearing that, I tried, for a moment, to imagine what this person must
have seen in this room over seven years. I couldn't quite manage it.
Seven years was old enough to have seen every visit, application, and
refusal this city had had since it began.

"…That friend of yours didn't tell you anything bad, Anemos. If
anything, she told you honestly. Everyone here talks that way."

"…I know. I know it isn't a lie. That's exactly why I was more
curious."

The Curator said nothing for a moment. He bought time by pretending to
straighten the papers on his desk. I felt that silence was a silence
preparing an answer.

The Curator stopped straightening the papers and looked at me
directly.

"…Anemos." He finally spoke. "This isn't a question to be asked
lightly."

"…I know. I still want to know."

"…Why do you want to know?"

I couldn't answer that question right away. Why I wanted to know, I
didn't clearly know myself. It felt premature to say it was for my
mother — I hadn't confirmed anything yet. I just felt I had to know.

"…I don't want to stay not knowing."

The Curator looked at me for a while. That gaze felt long.

"…You're asking because of your mother and your brother."

"…Yes. Probably."

"…Is there a reason you said 'probably'?"

I couldn't answer that question right away either. I wasn't even sure
myself whether that was really it. Whether it was for my mother's
sake, or because I couldn't bear not doing something.

"…I don't know. I just felt like I had to know."

The Curator heard that answer and nodded. Something like resignation
was mixed into that nod.

"…All right. I'll explain it to you. But there's something I want to
say first."

"…What is it?"

"…Once you've heard this explanation, you can't go back to not having
heard it."

I didn't quite understand what that meant. But my body tensed before
I did.

"…That's all right. Please tell me."

The Curator still hesitated a moment longer. I saw that hesitation for
the first time then. In this city, people generally didn't hesitate.
If they knew the answer, they said it right away; if they didn't,
they said so right away. This man knew the answer and was hesitating
anyway.

"…This isn't a common thing for me to talk about." The Curator said.
"I've been here for years, and this question has rarely been put to
me this directly."

"…Doesn't everyone ask?"

"…Some do. But most, once they've heard it, don't come back."

"…Why not?"

"…That's something I'd rather you hear for yourself, and judge for
yourself."

"…Is it that frightening a story?"

The Curator didn't answer that question right away. He tapped the
edge of the desk lightly with his finger. It was the first time I'd
seen someone in this city make a gesture that looked that restless.

"…I'm not sure frightening is the right word. But it isn't an easy
story."

*I let that warning pass lightly, at the time. Why the Curator had
bothered to say it first, I would only come to understand properly
chapters later. After hearing what came next, I could never go back
to who I had been before.*

Outside the window, the shadows shifted a little. There was no sound
of the sun moving, but the light in the room changed, little by
little. I watched that change and waited for his answer.

The Curator straightened in his seat. He pulled one folder toward
himself but didn't open it. He just rested his hand on top of it.

"…I'll start from the beginning." The Curator said. "It's a simple
procedure. Simple, and not simple."

I wanted to write that contradiction down exactly as he said it. Not
understanding what it meant made me want to, more.

"…What do you mean?"

"…You'll understand once you hear it."

I set the flask on my knees and wrapped both hands around it. There
was no particular reason. I just wanted something to hold on to. The
flask's surface was cold. That cold was the only sensation in that
room I could actually feel in my body.

*I still remember that posture. Both hands wrapped around the flask. I
sat in that room in that posture, waiting for what would come next.
And what came was, in small ways, different from what I'd expected,
and different from what I'd feared.*

The Curator drew a breath. And began to speak.
