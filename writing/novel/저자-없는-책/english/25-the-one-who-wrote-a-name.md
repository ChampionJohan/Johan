# Chapter 25. The One Who Wrote a Name

> _Previously_ — There was no withdrawal procedure. No provision
> forbidding it, either. There was nothing at all. Only, opening a new
> deliberation was possible. It required the proposer's name. "What
> happens if I write a name?" I asked, and for the first time, the
> Secretary looked away.

Whenever I saw someone look away, I always used to think that person
knew something. After a few months in the Equity, I learned that was
the wrong guess. People looked away not because they knew something,
but more often because they didn't want to be bound by it once they'd
said they knew.

Did the Secretary know something, or did he just not want to be bound?
The next day I went to find him again. I asked the same thing.

"…What happens if I write a name?"

This time the Secretary didn't look away. Instead he said this.

"…That isn't something I can answer. You'd do better to look at past
cases."

"…Where do I look?"

"…It's all in the reading room."

*I thought at the time that answer was kindness. Looking back on it
now, it was kindness and it was passing the burden along, at the same
time. I learned in this city that the two aren't always different
things.*

So I went to the reading room.

---

The reading room was larger than the meeting hall. One whole wall was
shelving, though these days you read from a terminal, not paper. The
clerk at the desk had shown me how to search once, and I'd come back a
few times since. That day I had a purpose. To find whoever had written
a name in the signature box.

The method was simple. I typed "signature" into the search box, then
typed "entered" again. I expected almost nothing to come back, and
truly, nothing did. Nothing was itself a result. The screen read
"0 documents matching this condition."

I widened the condition. I entered only "signature box" and dropped
"blank." Then everything came up. Tens of thousands of documents. All
of them blank, of course. Finding the filled ones would take another
method.

*I spent the whole afternoon that day changing my search terms. Some
of the people reading this record will find this tedious, and I
understand how they feel. I found it tedious too. But enduring
tedium was almost the only thing I was good at, so I endured it.*

*I only learned later that the people in this city who actually find
something are rarely the clever ones. They're the ones who can bear
tedium. The reading room clerk was like that. So was Tyche. So was
the person I would meet later. This is a fact that runs through this
whole record, so I set it down here, once.*

In the middle of it Tyche passed by and stopped. She saw what I was
doing and sat down beside me.

"…What are you looking for?"

"…Someone who wrote a name in a signature box."

"…Is there such a person?"

"…I don't know yet."

Tyche looked at the screen for a moment and said,

"…It wouldn't be strange if there were. The regulations never barred
it. It's just low-probability. The chance that out of four hundred
people, someone would go and write their own name."

"…If it's low, does that mean never?"

"…No. If it's low, it just means it happens rarely."

That was true. Tyche was always right. I didn't know yet, then, why
that bothered me so much.

About three hours in, I found a different way. Not the signature box
itself, but the footnote that gets attached automatically underneath
it. The reading room clerk told me that in this city's documents, once
a signature box is filled, a small mark gets added next to the
document number.

"…There's a mark like that?"

"…There is, yes. It's used so rarely nobody knows about it."

"…It's used that rarely?"

"…I've worked here eight years, and I've seen exactly one document
with that mark on it."

I searched by that mark. One result came up.

---

A single name was written under the document number. The date was
eleven years ago. The deliberation district was the Second
Deliberation District, and the matter was a rezoning of a tract on the
southern outskirts of the city. On its face it looked like an
unremarkable matter.

I read the document from the beginning. As always, I read to the end.
The original matter ran forty-eight pages. Half was land registry.
Half was a record of opinions gathered.

The course of the matter wasn't different from any other deliberation.
Intake, review, confirming those involved, gathering opinions,
convening. Close to four hundred people had been involved. But at the
conclusion, the flow broke once. There had been an objection to the
result the Consensus had produced, and whoever filed that objection had
proposed a new deliberation. And in that proposal's signature box, there
was a name.

The name was short. One surname, one given name. I read it several
times. It wasn't a name I recognized. I checked whether anyone by that
name still lived in this city now. There wasn't.

The reason for the objection was written into the document too. The
original matter was to rezone the entire southern tract for warehouse
use. Several old houses stood on that tract, and once it was rezoned
for warehouses, those houses were slated for demolition. The demolition
itself wasn't the problem. The problem was that the matter contained no
procedure at all for where the people living in those houses would
resettle.

This wasn't something anyone had left out out of malice. That matter
dealt only with land use; relocating residents was a separate matter.
Each side had handled exactly what fell to it, correctly. But between
the two matters, nobody stood. The person who filed the objection had
pointed at exactly that gap. "If this matter passes, these houses come
down, and where those people go is in no matter at all."

The Consensus took in that objection. That it was taken in mattered. It
wasn't ignored. It was taken in and reviewed, and on review, judged
valid. But the procedure for accepting an objection and the procedure
for opening a new deliberation were different things. An objection
could be reviewed and end there. Only a new deliberation actually
changed anything. And the box for opening a new deliberation required a
name.

*Absent doesn't mean dead. I kept confusing the two, back then. The
person could have moved. Could have changed their name. Could simply
have dropped off this city's roster. Absent can always mean several
things, and I kept leaning toward the heaviest one.*

The new deliberation really was opened. The record said so. The
procedure again passed through nearly four hundred people, and a
conclusion came out. Part of the original matter was reversed. Only
part. Half of the southern tract stayed as it was; the other half was
confirmed for the new use.

That person's name was on the new deliberation's document too. In the
proposer's field.

What came after was the problem.

I looked for other documents carrying that name afterward. There were
some. Quite a few. That person had been called in on various matters
after that — as a witness, a confirmer, someone involved. The first
few months it was two or three times a month. The half year after
that, five or six times a month. Every document had a field confirming
that person had appeared, and every one of those fields was filled in.

And then, at some point between the spring and summer eleven years
ago, the documents carrying that name grew sparser, and then stopped
altogether.

---

I checked the date on the last document twice. Eleven years ago, early
summer. It was an ordinary confirmation request. There was a signature
showing that person had come. The next convening date was written in
too. Two weeks later.

I found that next convening's document. It was open. That person's
name wasn't on the list of attendees. It wasn't marked as an absence,
either. The field simply wasn't on the list at all.

I widened the search. By name, by document number, by the district
that person had likely lived in. Nothing came up. I checked the
document's revision history too, looking for some trace of deletion.
The Equity's documents keep their full revision history. There was no
trace of deletion. No mark of a correction, no mark of an erasure.

*I thought hard, more than once, about rewriting this part. There was
a temptation to write that I'd found something hidden. It would have
made this story more interesting. But that isn't true. I found
nothing. There was nothing to find.*

*The record wasn't cut off. It simply had no more. I didn't understand
that difference well then. I understand it now. Cut off means someone
severed it. No more means it simply went that far and no further.*

I went to that southern tract too. It was an hour's walk. The half now
confirmed for the new use had warehouse buildings standing on it. The
half left as it was still had a few old houses standing, just as
before. It looked like people lived there. Laundry hung out to dry.

Between the fact that the houses remained and the fact that person's
record didn't, I couldn't find any connection at all. The houses had
remained. That meant that person had achieved something. And yet the
one who had achieved it had vanished. Setting those two facts side by
side didn't settle my mind — it unsettled it. That there was no one
behind something that had gone right felt, somehow, stranger than
there being no one behind something that had gone wrong.

*I can't name this feeling exactly. It wasn't relief. Relief should
feel like something had ended, and I felt nothing like an ending. If
anything, it felt like something had begun. I didn't know then what.*

I asked the reading room clerk. Do you know this name, I said.

The clerk looked at the screen and shook her head.

"…First time I've seen it."

"…Would you know what became of this person?"

"…I wasn't here then."

"…Who would know?"

The clerk thought for a moment and said,

"…Well. I've heard there's still one person these days who writes a
name. Not from this city — someone who comes and goes."

I didn't let that pass. I hadn't planned to, that day, but I asked
without letting it go.

"…What's this person's name?"

The clerk told me a name.

"…Does this person still come here these days?"

"…I see them once or twice a month. I hear they do some kind of work
finding people, around here."

"…Finding people?"

"…I don't know the details. Moving from city to city, and if someone
asks them to find somebody, they find them. I've only heard it as a
rumor myself."

I had no real thought about that answer, hearing it. I supposed that
someone whose work was finding people might reasonably write their own
name. No one would ask someone to find a person for them without
knowing who was asking.

*That was a naive thought. That night I didn't even know it was naive.
I had simply set one fact next to another — that the record of the
person who'd written a name eleven years ago had stopped, and that
there was, now, one person who wrote a name. There might have been no
connection at all between the two. I knew that even then.*

*And still I set the two side by side. It's hard to explain why. Maybe
after three hours alone in the reading room changing search terms, you
start wanting to connect at least one thing to another. I know that
isn't an honest way to think. I'm setting down here, anyway, that I
did it.*

---

On my way home I passed the water channel. The western ditch was still
making its sound. I stood beside it for a moment and thought about
that person from eleven years ago.

A name had been written. A new deliberation had opened. Part of it had
been reversed. And then, called in again and again, until one summer
day the record simply stopped.

*I told my aunt about this that night. She listened a long while and
said, "…So nobody knows what became of that person?" I said that was
right. My aunt said, "…Then it's unknown, Hora. Don't decide something
bad happened."*

*My aunt was right. I had already decided, by then. That something bad
had happened. There was no basis for it. Knowing full well that
absence itself can't be evidence, I still read it that way.*

*Writing this book, I look back on this passage more than once. This
was probably the first time I went against Tyche's logic. That day
Tyche had told me about probability, and I thought I'd found a hole
that probability couldn't explain. Looking at it now, that too was a
mistake. It wasn't a hole. It was simply an ending.*

*Whether it was my nature, or my mother, or having stared too long at
that empty signature box, I still don't know for certain. But I know
one thing. Tyche spoke of probability that day, and I couldn't refute
it. And still I kept looking for that document. Being unable to refute
something didn't make me stop.*

That name — the one the clerk had told me, the person who still wrote
a name these days — I copied onto paper. I put it back in the drawer
where my mother's papers were kept.

Before I closed the drawer, I copied that other name, the one from
eleven years ago, onto the same paper. Setting the two names side by
side, there was nothing alike about them at all. Different surnames,
different numbers of syllables. And still I looked at those two lines
for a long while.

*Looking back now, I think I had already decided something, that day.
I still didn't know what had become of the person from eleven years
ago. But I couldn't leave that not-knowing as simply not-knowing. I
needed to find one more name and set it up alongside, to feel settled.
What would Tyche have called this? Probably "a meaningless
connection." If Tyche had said that, I wouldn't have been able to
refute her. I had no basis to.*

*And still I put that paper in the drawer. That there are things you
do without a basis — I learned that for the first time, then.*

I slept well that night. I don't know why. It wasn't because I'd found
an answer. Not one answer had come out of it. It was only that what I
would do the next day was decided, and that, I think, was what settled
me. In this city there are nights when just knowing what tomorrow holds
is enough to set your mind at ease.

The next day, I went looking for that person.
