# Chapter 34. The Water Reaches

> _Previously_ — The Secretary came to find me outside working hours
> for the first time. He said he had handled the convening for that
> deliberation eleven years ago too. "…I convened that one too." There
> was no apology. That evening, I heard that a sound different from
> usual was coming from the western water channel.

My aunt stopped in the middle of setting out dinner.

"…Did you hear about the western ditch? They say the water's making
a strange, loud sound today."

"…Strange how?"

"…I don't know, I only heard it secondhand. Are you going to go
look?"

I set down my bowl and put on my coat. My aunt didn't stop me. She
knew too — in this city, if you're curious about something, going to
look is the fastest way to find out.

Outside it was already dim. Walking through a few alleys, my heart
beat strangely fast. I didn't quite know why. Whenever I felt
something was changing, I always hurried toward it. I don't know
myself when that habit began.

---

The sound of water reached me from the western alley on. It was
clearly louder than usual. Too widespread a sound to be coming from a
single ditch.

When I reached the end of the alley, I saw why.

The water channel had widened. It used to be a narrow ditch you could
barely set one foot across; now it was twice that width. The current
had picked up too. The water, once the color of earth, looked a
little clearer. It seemed as though more water had come pushing in
from somewhere beyond the city's boundary.

*Watching this that night, I truly felt that this water had come
"inside" this city. When I'd first seen it, it had been flowing
somewhere at the western edge of the city, out at the boundary. Not
anymore. The channel had already worked its way into the very alleys
we walked through every day.*

People had come out to stand beside it. Few faces looked startled.
Most looked curious. Two children had gone in barefoot and were
splashing at the water's edge, and a few adults stood watching them,
laughing.

---

I ran to the front desk. One person was on the night shift.

"…Excuse me — the western water channel. It's come inside the city.
Where do I report this?"

The clerk tapped at the screen. He searched a long while and shook his
head.

"…There's no matter number for this."

"…What do you mean, no matter number?"

"…To file a report there has to be a related matter, but this water
channel itself has never come before a matter. I don't know what to
file this under."

"…So you just can't do it?"

"…I can't open a new matter on my own judgment. That's outside my
authority."

I'd expected that answer, and still, hearing it out loud, it left me
stifled.

*What Tyche had said before came back to me. "If it's never been
submitted, it doesn't exist. Not hidden — just not submitted." I'd
thought I understood that, at the time. But I'd only understood it in
my head. Only now, watching the water actually rise around my own
feet, did I feel in my body how frightening that sentence really
was.*

---

The next day, on my way to the district hall, I ran into the
Secretary. It was the first time since that night a few days before.
I brought up the water channel.

"…Secretary, do you know about the western water channel? It's come
inside the city."

"…I know."

"…Then why isn't anything being done?"

"…There's no matter for it."

"…Can't you make a matter, Secretary?"

"…What I can make is a convening, not a matter. A matter only exists
once the department with jurisdiction takes it in."

"…Then which department has jurisdiction?"

The Secretary thought a moment.

"…This water channel's jurisdiction has never been settled. Matters
involving the city boundary belong to the outskirts management
department; matters involving water supply belong to the water supply
department. Which of the two this falls under, no one's decided yet."

"…So no one can do anything?"

"…Someone first has to propose which department it belongs to. Only
then can a matter be opened."

"…Can't you do that, Secretary?"

"…I could propose it. But I haven't yet judged this to be a problem."

"…Water's come into the city and that isn't a problem?"

"…Something bad has to happen for it to be a problem. So far, nothing
bad has happened."

That answer was exactly what Tyche had said. That two different
people had said the same thing confirmed for me, again, that this was
how the whole city worked.

---

That afternoon I went to the reading room and searched for whether
anything had ever come into this city this way before, without a
matter behind it. I spent half a day changing my search terms, over
and over. I found almost nothing. A few very old records turned up,
all trivial things. A tree that had grown up on its own by the
roadside. A garden plot that had grown up though no one had planted
it. Since neither had ever come before a matter, there wasn't even a
record of when the tree or the garden had first appeared. It was only
written that they'd simply been there, from some point on.

*Looking at these records, I thought this city might have been built
this way from the start. Things that had gone through deliberation,
and things that had simply grown up with no one noticing. This city
called only the first kind "the city," and treated the second kind as
though it weren't part of the city at all. And yet the second kind
was plainly there too. The tree, the garden plot, and now this water
channel.*

I asked the reading room clerk.

"…Have things like this ever come before a matter, later on?"

"…Sometimes. If someone feels it's a bother. But that tree's still
standing there without a matter, even now. So's the garden plot."

"…So if no one's bothered, it just stays that way forever?"

"…Seems like it. I'd never thought that was strange before, but
hearing you say it, it does seem a little strange."

"…So this water channel will just keep on being like this, until
someone's bothered by it?"

"…Probably. But everyone likes it, don't they? Is anyone likely to be
bothered?"

I couldn't answer that question. If truly no one was bothered, this
water channel would stay outside any matter forever. I learned, that
day, for the first time, that even a good thing could stay outside a
matter. The clerk said this and turned her eyes back to the screen.
The conversation ended there.

---

The next morning, the channel had widened further. The city still
hadn't dealt with it. Not so much hadn't dealt with it — there simply
was no procedure to deal with it at all.

On my way to the district hall I met Tyche. She knew about the water
channel too.

"…This really isn't going to become a matter?"

"…Someone has to bring it up for it to become one. It doesn't rise on
its own."

"…Then who's supposed to bring it up?"

"…I don't know that either. Normally, something like this needs a
settled jurisdiction before it can be raised, but this channel doesn't
have one settled either."

"…So it just stays like this forever?"

Tyche thought a moment.

"…No. Once someone feels it's a problem and raises it as a matter,
it'll get handled. But right now, no one seems to feel it's a
problem."

"…Water's come right into the middle of the city and that isn't a
problem?"

"…People like it. They drink it, children play in it. If it isn't
something bad, no one brings it before a matter. This city's only
built to respond to bad things."

That was true. And that truth was what chilled me.

---

All that week the channel kept widening, little by little. The city
still did nothing. People drew the water and used it. Did laundry in
it. Children splashed in it. One household brought a jar and filled
it there.

Near the market, someone set out a bench beside the channel. In the
evenings people gathered there, sitting with their feet in the water,
talking. Watching that scene, I felt again how quickly the people of
this city adjusted to something new. The astonishment didn't last more
than a few days. By the end of a week, everyone was acting as though
this water had always been there.

Someone built a small water wheel and set it up in the current. It
was a child's toy, but grown-ups would turn it once, passing by, and
laugh. Watching that water wheel, I felt something strange. On water
that had come before no matter, with no one's permission, someone had
built something with their own hands and set it up. That was a rare
sight in this city.

A child once showed me the water wheel, proud of it. "Look, I made
this myself." I told her she'd made it well. She turned the wheel a
few more times, delighted. That child had no curiosity at all about
where this water had come from, or whether it was in any matter. All
that mattered to her was that the water was there, and the wheel
turned.

*Watching that child, I remembered what Tyche always said. That
nothing gets treated as a problem unless it's something bad. For that
child, this was exactly the right attitude. It wasn't something bad.
I felt no urge at all to grab her and ask, "Doesn't this feel
strange?" That wasn't hers to carry.*

I'd thought this might be dangerous, but nothing happened. The water
was clear, and no one who drank it fell ill. It wasn't something bad.
It truly wasn't something bad.

*Writing this passage, I wonder whether I myself had been hoping
there was something bad in this water channel. If there had been
something bad, it would have been easier, in a way — there would have
been a reason to bring it before a matter. But there was nothing bad.
It was simply good water, come into the city with no procedure at
all. That was what unsettled me most.*

My aunt drew that water and used it too. One evening I saw her
carrying a water jar on her head and asked,

"…Aunt, did you check whether this is safe to drink?"

"…Check what. Everyone drinks it."

"…But we don't even know where it came from."

My aunt set the jar down and said,

"…Hora, water's always been something you drink without knowing where
it came from. Whether it comes from the sky or from a mountain.
There's nothing strange about that."

I couldn't answer that. It wasn't wrong.

That night, the woman from next door came over and talked with my
aunt about the water channel. Both of them talked about it as though
it were old, familiar weather. "The water's come in early this year."
"Right, and it's stronger than last year's." Listening to that tone, I
felt this water channel had already entered this city's everyday
language. It wasn't in any matter, but it was in their speech.

*I think this is the quietest passage in this chapter. No great event,
no great line. Just two people talking about the water channel the way
they'd talk about the weather. But that quiet was what frightened me
most. I'd thought frightening things came loudly. This one simply
walked, quietly, into an evening's small talk.*

---

That evening, I walked along the water channel. I wanted to see, with
my own eyes, how deep it had come into the city.

The channel ran from the western alley all the way to near the
market. Its width wasn't even. Narrow in some places, wide enough in
others for people to sit with their feet in it.

Someone I'd never seen before was sitting by the water. He had taken
off a pair of worn shoes beside the water and sat with his feet in
it, quietly looking at the water.

I walked past him. No one else looked at him. My own eyes went to him
only for a moment, and I didn't stop walking either. There was always
someone sitting by the water in this city; it wasn't anything unusual.

*Looking back on that evening, I can't remember any one thing I saw by
the water as anything special. The children, the people doing
laundry, the people carrying water jars. And that man. All of it was
just the scenery of the waterside. I didn't fix my eyes on any of it,
that day. That itself struck me as strange, only much later.*

---

Walking home, I ran into Tyche again. She was carrying a water jar.

"…You use this water too, Tyche?"

"…Yes. Everyone else does; there's no reason I shouldn't."

"…But doesn't it seem strange? Everyone using water that isn't even
in any matter?"

Tyche shifted her grip on the jar and said,

"…It's strange. But strange and bad are different things. I only
worry about the bad kind."

"…I worry about the strange kind too."

"…That's the part where you and I differ."

Tyche said that and smiled. It wasn't an unkind smile. It was just a
smile confirming that we were different.

*Watching this water channel, I felt it again. The Consensus had
hidden nothing about this water. There was nothing to hide. It was
simply that no one had ever brought it before a matter. And yet that
fact — that no one had brought it up — hadn't stopped the water from
rising into the middle of the city. If a system only stops something
once something bad has happened, then anything that isn't bad can go
on coming in, without any check at all, for as long as it likes.*

*If this city was always asking me one question, it was probably
this: is something no one did the same as nothing happening at all?
I had only ever thought of this question in terms of people — the
person from eleven years ago, the four hundred people, the signature
box. But watching the water channel, I understood for the first time
that this same question applied just as much to something that wasn't
a person. Water that no one had blocked kept coming in, for no other
reason than that no one had blocked it. It was good water. But the
way it came in, itself, wouldn't let me rest easy.*

I left my window open that night. The sound of water drifted faintly
down the alley. I fell asleep listening to it. The next day, I decided
to take a step I'd been putting off for a long time — going to meet,
in person, the one I had only ever known by number.
