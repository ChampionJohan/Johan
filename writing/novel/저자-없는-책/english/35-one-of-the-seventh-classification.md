# Chapter 35. One of the Seventh Classification

> _Previously_ — The water channel had come inside the city, but with
> no matter behind it, no one dealt with it. People simply began using
> the water. That night, I decided to take a step I'd been putting off
> for a long time — going to meet, in person, the one I had only ever
> known by number.

The deliberation I'd first been assigned, long ago, concerned people
grouped under the Seventh Classification. The summary had been
accurate, the procedure had followed the regulations, and it was only
after reading the full text to the end that I'd understood what was
actually happening. Of the people reassigned by that deliberation, I
had never, in all this time, actually met a single one. The roster
held only numbers. 7-118, 7-119, 7-120. No name appeared anywhere.

*I still don't think being called only by a number is a bad system, in
this city. There really was discrimination that it reduced. It's only
that, somewhere along the way, it kept nagging at me that I had never
once seen the person behind that number.*

The day after I saw the water channel, I found the first number on
that roster and went to the reassignment district. It was about an
hour's walk.

The whole way there, I practiced what I would say. I tried starting
with, "Hello, I'm a district-assembly assistant member," but it
sounded too official, and I gave that up. "Do you happen to remember
me" was strange too — there was no reason a person I'd never met
would remember me. In the end I arrived at the entrance of the
district without having settled on a single sentence.

*Writing this chapter, I admit again how long I'd put off this walk.
Earlier I'd said I wanted to meet that warehouse manager I only knew
by number. And still I put it off for months after that. I told
myself, at the time, that it was because I was busy. Looking at it
now, that was an excuse. I think I was simply afraid.*

---

The reassignment district was newly built communal housing at the
city's eastern edge. The buildings were clean, well kept. I hesitated
a moment out front. I'd made up my mind to come, but standing at the
door I still didn't know what to say.

I gave my number at the front desk and stated my purpose. I said I was
a district-assembly assistant member, and that I had something to
confirm relating to a past deliberation. That much explanation was
enough. No one in this city pressed for more.

"…You're 7-118?"

The desk clerk sent word inside. A little while later, a person came
out.

---

They looked much older than me — around my aunt's age, maybe. Their
clothes were plain, their hands rough. There was no particular wariness
in their face at seeing me.

"…You're from the district assembly?"

"…Yes. I'm Hora."

"…Hora. What brings you here?"

Their voice was calm. Not knowing how to begin, I simply told the
truth.

"…The thirteenth deliberation I was ever assigned was the Seventh
Classification matter. I read the full text to the end, that time, and
since then I've never actually met anyone who really knows what
happened to them because of that deliberation."

"…So you came to find me?"

"…Yes. It might sound strange, but I just wanted to meet you."

They looked at me a moment, and smiled slightly.

"…It's not strange. If anything, you're the first person who's ever
come to me over something like this."

---

We sat on a bench outside the building. They spoke first.

"…I know that deliberation. It was the result that came down to me."

"…How was it, at the time?"

"…Angry, at first. But looking back, it wasn't all bad. I got a place
to live here, and work too."

"…But the deliberation document only had the reassignment in it. It
didn't say how you were actually living, after."

"…That would be right. A document only writes down as far as the
decision. What comes after is just living, so."

That was new to me. Up until then I'd thought reading a deliberation
document to the end meant knowing everything. But life went on after
the document. That was a part no document could hold.

"…Where did you live before?"

"…South side of the city. I did small farm work there. Here, instead
of farm work, I manage this housing complex. It wasn't work my hands
knew, but I'm used to it now."

"…Did you like the farm work better?"

"…It's less about liking it or not — it was work I'd done for over
twenty years. My body didn't take to a different kind of work right
away, when I started."

"…That doesn't show up in the document either."

"…No. The document just says 'reassignment complete.' It doesn't hold
things like how long it took my hands to get used to it."

Hearing this, I thought of that warehouse manager Tyche and I had read
about, side by side. The one moved to office work — they were
probably going through something like a similar span of time right
now, I thought. I decided, in that moment, that I needed to go meet
that person too, someday.

---

"…You're 7-118. But what's your real name?"

Asking this, I worried for a moment whether I was being rude. But they
didn't seem to mind at all.

"…I have one, of course."

"…Could I ask what it is?"

They looked at me a long while, then said,

"…I'll tell you. But why do you want to know?"

"…Just — I wanted to call you something besides a number."

They told me their name.

*I won't write that name here. There are several people in this
record whose names I keep from the page, but this person's is the one
I hold back the longest. I'll write the reason in chapter 48. For
now, I'll only set down the moment of hearing it.*

Hearing that name, I felt strangely at ease. That there really was a
name behind a person I'd only known by number felt like a confirmation
I shouldn't have needed to make, and yet, needless as it was, it
landed heavily in me.

"…That's a pretty name."

"…Thank you. But no one calls me by it here."

"…Why not?"

"…The number's easier. It's on all the paperwork, and everyone calls
me by it. It hurt at first, but now I just take it as how things are."

---

"…It hurt, but you got used to it?"

"…Not so much got used to it — I just don't have the strength to be
hurt by it every time. Living comes first."

That sounded like something Tyche often said. Doing today's work. And
yet this person's "today's work" carried a different weight than
Tyche's had.

"…Are you all right now, then?"

"…I'm all right. The first few months after the reassignment were
hard, but I'm settled here now. There's work, there are neighbors."

"…Do you think everything about that deliberation turned out well?"

They thought a moment at this question.

"…Not everything. A few things I still don't understand. Why I had
to come here specifically, why nowhere else would do. But I have to
save my strength for that too — questioning it."

"…Temel said something about saving strength too."

"…Who?"

"…Ah, never mind. Someone I know said something similar."

Just then a child ran up and pressed close beside them. Looked around
seven or eight.

"…My neighbor's child. We got close since I came here."

The child looked me over and asked,

"…What's your number?"

I didn't understand for a moment.

"…Your number. Everyone here has one."

"…Ah — I don't live here, so I don't have a number."

The child looked at me, astonished.

"…There are people without numbers?"

The person beside her stroked her hair and laughed.

"…There are more people without numbers outside than with them, you
know."

The child ran off again, looking unconvinced.

*I turned this short exchange over for a long time. To this child, a
number wasn't strange — it was simply how things were. I was the
strange one, for having none. Watching a child who'd grown up without
a number outside this city grow up, inside it, taking a number for
granted, I felt something I couldn't quite name. No one here was a
bad person, and still that seat didn't sit easily with me.*

---

"…Why are you this curious, Hora? About someone like me?"

I couldn't answer this right away. I thought a moment and said,

"…I've only ever seen people inside documents. Only by number. But
that isn't really that person, is it. I wanted to know that."

"…What changes, knowing it?"

"…I don't know. I just think — once I know, I'll read that document
differently, the next time."

They nodded.

"…That sounds like reason enough to me."

*I held onto that answer for a long time. Reason enough. I'd been
doubting, myself, up until then, whether what I was doing had reason
enough behind it. And of all people, the person who had actually been
inside that document was the one who told me my reason was enough.
That, strangely, left me feeling lighter.*

---

"…But I still don't really understand what the Seventh Classification
actually is," I asked. "It's just a category, isn't it. But what
standard is inside it."

"…I don't know exactly either. I was just told, one day, that I'd
been placed in that classification."

"…Don't they tell you the reason?"

"…They do. It's all in the document. It's divided by a few standards —
residential district, occupation, household makeup. I read that
document too. But even reading it, I couldn't quite make out why
those particular standards had put me here, of all places."

"…Doesn't that seem strange to you?"

"…It does. But it isn't just me who's strange about it — hundreds of
people similar to me came in here together. So it wasn't like I was
singled out. That's a small comfort, at least."

That echoed something Tyche had said, talking about the four hundred
people. That no one was singled out. I only truly felt, for the first
time, how that could be a comfort, hearing it in this person's voice.
Reading it in a document, it had just been a number. Now, the person
behind that number was taking that same fact as their own comfort.

*In that moment I thought of Tyche again. She would have explained
this situation in exactly those terms. And this person, in much the
same way Tyche did, was steadying themselves. Watching this, I
confirmed again that Tyche's logic really was, in this city, a way
that let people hold on. That's the reason I can't bring myself to
hate that logic.*

---

The sun was going down. As I stood to leave, I asked one last thing.

"…Could I come see you again?"

"…Of course. Any time."

"…Next time, could I ask you about that warehouse manager too? I
wondered if you might know them."

"…Well, all sorts of people from all sorts of districts end up living
mixed together here. I can ask around for you."

"…Thank you."

"…But Hora — if you go looking for people one at a time like this,
when will you meet them all? There must be quite a few of us."

That made me laugh.

"…I might not meet everyone. But meeting one at a time seemed better
than not meeting anyone at all."

"…That's true too."

"…But next time, what should I call you? By number, or by name?"

They smiled a little.

"…Whichever's easier for you. Either's fine with me."

I said goodbye and turned to go. The whole way home, I repeated that
name inside myself, over and over. I didn't write it on paper. This
time, I decided not to put it in the drawer. I wanted to keep this
name somewhere other than that drawer. I didn't know exactly where,
even then.

*Writing this record now, I still don't set that name down here. I'll
wait until chapter 48. Until that day, this name stays with me alone.
I think that's the most honest thing I can do, writing this chapter.*

I got home and told my aunt what had happened that day. She listened
quietly and said,

"…Good that you got the name, then."

"…Yeah. But strangely, just that seems to have changed something."

"…What changed?"

I couldn't answer that right away. I only had the feeling that
something had changed. Putting that feeling into words would take more
time.

My aunt asked again.

"…Aren't you going to tell me the name either?"

"…No. Not yet."

"…Why?"

"…I don't know. I just want to keep it that way."

My aunt didn't press further. Instead she said this.

"…You know, Hora, I've noticed lately — you keep holding things back.
The papers, and now this name too."

"…Is that strange?"

"…Not strange, but I do wonder what it's all for."

I couldn't answer that either. Even I didn't know exactly what it was
all for. But the act of holding something back, itself, felt like the
only way I knew to treat something as precious.

*Writing this chapter, I bring that evening back again. For a while
after receiving that name, I grew strangely careful. Even reading a
document, whenever I saw a number, I became aware there might be a
name behind it. What had changed wasn't the document. The document
stayed the same. What had changed was me.*

That night before falling asleep, I shaped that name silently with my
lips, without any sound. Somehow, saying it out loud felt like it
would use it up. Why I felt that way, I still can't explain exactly,
even now. I'll only set down here that the night passed quietly, that
way.
