# Chapter 45. Tyche's Reprieve

> _Previously_ — Temel told the two of them what their names meant.
> Hora — the hour, the appointed time. Tyche — chance, luck. Tyche
> laughed on hearing it, and after laughing, could say nothing at all.
> The next morning, the two of them worked as usual.

For several days after that, Tyche was largely no different than
usual.

She sorted materials, sat in on meetings, ate lunch with me. The
subject of names never came up again, not once. I thought that was
Tyche's own way of doing things. Letting some things simply pass.

*During those few days I wanted to ask her, several times, why she
hadn't been able to say anything, that day. I didn't ask. Writing this
book, I've thought many times that holding that question back was the
right thing to do. I still think so now.*

Temel left this city the next morning. I couldn't go to see him off. I
had a backlog of notices to process. I heard later from the person at
the reception desk that he'd shouldered his bag and gone quietly, as
usual. Hearing that news, I felt a pang, but soon acknowledged that this
was just like him. He was a person who moved on without leaving
anything behind. This time, though, he'd left behind two names.

One thing, though, had changed. Before this, whenever Tyche organized
deliberation materials, she liked to cite probability and statistics.
That this agenda had turned out this way because of how many people
were involved, and so the probability was high, that kind of thing.
During those few days, Tyche never once used that word. I only noticed
on the third day.

"…Tyche, you haven't been talking about probability lately."

"…Have I not? I hadn't noticed."

"…You really haven't. I counted."

Tyche laughed a little.

"…You really do count things like that, Hora."

Then she changed the subject. I didn't press her further.

*I've thought about this small change many times since. Whether not
using the word probability meant something, or whether there had
simply happened to be no material that week calling for it — I never
did find out, in the end. I could have asked Tyche again, and I didn't.
I felt it was right to leave some things unconfirmed.*

---

On the sixth day, Tyche came to find me first.

It was near the end of the workday. Only the two of us were left in
the meeting room. Tyche stood for a moment with the papers she'd been
organizing still in her hand, then spoke.

"…Hora."

"…Yeah."

"…I've been thinking, ever since then."

I stopped what I was doing. Tyche's face looked like she'd come
prepared with something. She hadn't brought materials the way she
usually did. She'd come empty-handed.

---

"…I've always said that wanting there to be a meaning, and believing
there is one, are different things."

"…Yeah."

"…That still hasn't changed. It's the same now."

Hearing that, I tensed a little. I wondered if Tyche had come to lay
out some conclusion.

"…But the truth is, that day, hearing what my name meant, I did laugh.
I still don't really know why it was funny to me."

"…Why do you think it was funny?"

Tyche thought for a moment.

"…Probably because it fit too well. I'm someone who's said, my whole
life, that everything is chance — and my own name literally meant
chance. That struck me as absurd."

---

"…But what that means, I still don't know, even now."

I nearly asked what she meant by that, then held back. Tyche went on.

"…The reason I couldn't say anything after I laughed was that I didn't
know what to say. I felt like I should say something, but I didn't know
what. That had never happened to me before."

"…Do you know now?"

"…No. I still don't know. That's why I came. To tell you I don't
know."

*I expected, in that moment, that Tyche would say something different.
Maybe I even hoped for it, a little. That she thought there might be
meaning after all. That the probability of a name fitting this well
was too low. That she might fill, differently this time, the hole
she'd admitted to before, further back.*

---

Tyche walked to the window. The one that faced the water channel. The
sun was setting, and the light outside the window was red.

"…I've worked it out like this. The person who chose my name was my
parents. It's unlikely my parents knew the Greek word τύχη when they
chose it. My family weren't people who'd studied that kind of thing.
Probably they liked the sound of it, or it was a name they'd heard
somewhere."

"…So the meaning attached itself by chance."

"…Yeah. The meaning attached itself by chance. Actually, I even ran
the numbers on it."

"…You ran the numbers?"

"…Yeah. Roughly how many names are used in this city, how many of
those carry a Greek meaning, and of those, what the odds are that the
meaning would happen to match the person's character. I calculated it,
roughly."

"…And?"

"…Low. Low, but not zero. About that."

*I thought, in that moment, how like Tyche this was. Even in a moment
like this, she was someone who didn't let go of calculation. But what
came after that calculation surprised me.*

"…But…"

Tyche stopped. I waited.

---

"…That I grew up to be someone who believes in probability and
chance the way I do now — I think all of that can be explained too.
My grandmother's words, the environment I grew up in, the work I
love. All of it has a reason. It's not because of my name."

"…But?"

Tyche turned to look at me.

"…But that my name happened to mean that, and that I happen to be
living out that meaning — calling even that a coincidence has started
to feel a little hard for me now."

---

Tyche kept looking out at the water channel for a while. I didn't
press her. The clock in the meeting room sounded unusually loud. I
thought, over those few days, that Tyche hadn't been sleeping well at
night. The skin under her eyes was darker than usual.

"…Actually, I meant to come say this yesterday too, and didn't."

"…Why not?"

"…I couldn't get it sorted out, what to say. It's still not fully
sorted out today either, but I came anyway."

I held my breath and waited for what would come next. I felt something
large was coming.

Tyche looked straight at me and said.

"…I suppose my name meaning that is a coincidence too. Isn't it?"

I opened my mouth to answer that question.

Tyche didn't wait for my answer.

She turned around just as she was, gathered her papers, shouldered her
bag, and walked toward the door.

"…See you tomorrow."

Her voice was no different than usual.

The door closed.

---

I stayed alone in that room for a long while. Outside the window it
had gone fully dark by now. The sound of the water channel came in
over the windowsill.

I looked at the chair Tyche had been sitting in. The papers she'd been
holding still lay on the table. I gathered them up and put them in the
file box. Something Tyche would ordinarily have done herself.

*Putting those papers away, I felt strangely urgent. I wanted to chase
after Tyche and finish giving her my answer. I felt that if I opened
the door and went out, I could probably still catch up to her. I
didn't. I already knew, in that moment, that if Tyche had wanted an
answer, she would have waited.*

I stood there for a long while. What I'd meant to say stayed right
there in my mouth. I never told anyone that, in the end. I don't intend
to write it down in this record either.

Turning off the light and leaving the meeting room, I ran into the
caretaker in the hallway. He looked at me and asked.

"…Why are you leaving so late today?"

"…Oh, a conversation ran a little long."

"…What about?"

"…Just, about names."

The caretaker nodded, not looking particularly curious, and walked on.
I was strangely grateful for that indifference, that day. That the
world was turning as unremarkably as ever — that short exchange
confirmed it to me again.

*I've rewritten this scene many times. Each time I wanted to revise it
so that I answered her with something before Tyche walked out the
door. But that wasn't the truth. Tyche really did leave without waiting
for an answer. I decided to leave that fact exactly as it was.*

*Tyche hadn't asked that question wanting to be persuaded. Nor had she
asked to be refuted. Even now I don't know exactly who that question
was aimed at. It could have been me. It could have been Tyche herself.
It could have been no one at all.*

*At the very front of this record, I meant to write that this book was
the story of two people arriving at different conclusions over the
same document. Writing this chapter, I feel again how exact that was.
Tyche and I heard the same two names on the same evening, from the same
person's mouth. I saw something in them. Tyche saw a question. Neither
of us was wrong.*

---

The next day Tyche really did come in as usual.

"…Good morning."

"…Yeah, good morning."

Neither of us said anything about the day before. I didn't bring it up
either. We sorted materials as usual that day, and ate lunch as usual.

At the restaurant, Tyche said, out of nowhere.

"…Hora, remember what you told me before. That maybe nothing changes,
but even so, it won't be empty."

"…Yeah, I remember."

"…That kept coming back to me, strangely, this whole time. I think
yesterday's question was something like that too. My answer doesn't
change. I still believe in probability. But I think it's better that I
asked that question than that I never did."

"…Why?"

"…Because once I asked it, it stopped being nothing at all. It's not
empty anymore."

I couldn't say anything back, hearing that. Tyche was handing my own
words back to me, in her own way.

"…So you've filled something too, then."

"…Filled what?"

"…That question. Like a signature box."

Tyche gave a short laugh at that.

"…It's not that big a deal. But, well, maybe it's something like
that."

We finished eating after that, with nothing more said. The restaurant
was crowded as usual, and no one paid us any attention.

*I don't convert Tyche in this book. I have no intention of doing so.
Tyche lives on, even after asking that question, as someone who still
believes in probability and procedure. Only, I think that question
probably never entirely vanished inside her. If it had vanished, she
wouldn't have asked it to begin with.*

---

When I told my aunt this story, she was quiet for a long while.

"…So what did you answer?"

"…I couldn't. Tyche didn't wait."

"…Good."

"…Why?"

My aunt set down her rice bowl.

"…Some questions aren't meant to get an answer. You just say them to
someone."

"…Then why say it at all?"

"…So you can move on to what comes next. Not saying it and moving on,
and saying it without hearing an answer and moving on — those are
completely different things."

"…How are they different?"

My aunt thought for a moment.

"…If you don't say it, it's as if the question never existed. If you
say it, even without hearing an answer, the question stays. Somewhere."

I turned that over. I thought about where Tyche's question was, right
now. In the air of that meeting room. Inside Tyche herself. Or inside
me. I couldn't settle on any single one of those. Probably it was all
three.

I thought about it for a long time. I still can't be certain whether
Tyche's question was really that kind of question. But I do know she
would have waited if she'd wanted an answer. Tyche was a person who
waited. Always. Only that day, she didn't.

"…Aunt, you've never even met Tyche. How do you know her so well?"

"…I don't know Tyche. I know that there's a kind of person like that.
A person who asks a question and then runs. I was like that too, when
I was young."

"…You were?"

"…Yeah. I once asked your mother a question, just one time, and left
without hearing the answer."

I'd never heard this before.

"…What was the question?"

My aunt laughed a little.

"…I'm not going to tell you that. That one's mine."

I didn't press any further. I realized, for the first time that day,
that my aunt had a drawer of her own too. And that maybe everyone in
this city was carrying, somewhere, one question they'd never been able
to speak.

That evening I opened the drawer and looked again at my mother's twelve
pages, and the short notes I'd kept there all this time. I didn't
write Tyche's question down on paper. It wasn't the kind of thing that
could be written down. Instead of writing it, I decided just to
remember it, for a long time.

*I gave this chapter the title "Reprieve." A postponing of judgment.
That night, Tyche postponed judgment on herself. She didn't say it
wasn't chance. Nor did she reconfirm that it was. She just left one
question behind and went. I don't know when that reprieve will end.
Maybe it never will. I think that, too, is Tyche's own to keep.*

---

Several more weeks passed after that. Tyche was still someone who
spoke of probability. Still precise, still kind. Only, from that night
on, Tyche gained a habit of pausing mid-conversation, sometimes. Very
briefly. A habit she'd never had before.

I never asked what that pause was. It might have been nothing at all.
It might just have been that Tyche had grown a little more careful,
since that day.

*I wrote, somewhere in this book, that I wasn't going to try to win
against Tyche. I keep that promise here too. Tyche hasn't changed. If
anything has changed, it's only that Tyche now carries, with her, one
question she asked herself. I think that's enough. Maybe that's the
whole of what one person can do, in this city.*
