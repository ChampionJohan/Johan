# Chapter 7. Open to Inspection

> _Previously_ — Hora meeting the Secretary. Kind and exact, but always
> speaking without a subject. "It was put in order." "It was confirmed."
> Once, just once, an "I" slipped out, and was corrected on the spot. She
> took the manner of speech for nothing more than a habit of the post and
> let it go.

One day I got curious. Could you really look at anything, all of it.

People in this city often said "the Consensus hides nothing." I'd learned it at school, heard it in the meeting room, heard it from Tyche too. I believed it, but believing it and checking it were two different things. I was someone who checked.

So that afternoon, I decided to pick a deliberation I had no part in and go read the full text. Any case would do. I just went looking for a district entirely apart from the Fourth, an issue entirely unfamiliar to me.

---

I'd had the thought for a few days before that, but that day was the first time I actually acted on it. What I picked was a case out of the Second Deliberation District. It concerned a road-widening project, something I had no connection to whatsoever. There was a reason field on the application, and I wrote "practice at inspection." There was no box beneath it asking for further purpose.

The counter handed the material over with no particular verification procedure.

"…Can anyone look at this?"

"…Yes. You just fill out the application. We only check your identity."

"…You don't ask why?"

"…Why would we need to. It's public material."

That person finished the identity check and handed the paperwork straight over. The procedure was so simple that I wondered if something had been left out, and asked again.

"…Is that all? There's nothing else needed?"

"…No. Just an identity check. Anyone who lives here can see all of it."

"…People from outside too?"

"…Yes. Though they need a temporary registration card. That doesn't take long to issue either."

"…What about someone without the card, just looking briefly?"

"…That's not allowed. The identity check is required. But once you have the card, you're shown everything, exactly the same as a citizen. There's no difference in treatment."

I nodded at that once more. That there was a procedure, but no discrimination. I liked that distinction.

Hearing that, I felt, all over again, just how wide open this city keeps its own records. Even someone who wasn't a citizen could see everything, so long as they went through the proper procedure.

I liked what they'd said. Why would we need a reason, they'd asked back. If there's nothing to hide, a reason isn't needed either.

It occurred to me, suddenly, that I'd heard other cities pry into your reason for wanting to see a record. They said whoever wanted to see it was questioned first about why, and if the reason wasn't acceptable, they weren't shown it. I couldn't make sense of that approach. Who decided whether a reason was acceptable or not, I wondered. Here, that question didn't even exist.

The counter clerk added one more thing as they handed the paperwork over.

"…Come back if you need anything more. I can pull the related material for you too."

"…Related material?"

"…Yes, past cases connected to this one. I can print you out a list, if you'd like."

I found that offer striking. Kindness that told me something I hadn't even asked for. I requested the list right there, and that afternoon I walked out with far more material than I'd originally planned on.

The list had twelve past cases on it. I'd meant to read only three of them, but reading one led to a mention of another case, and chasing that down led me to another, until I'd ended up reading all twelve. I lost track of how much time had passed. Only when the window outside went dark did I hear the announcement that the reading room would soon close.

The librarian looked at me and said, smiling,

"…That's quite a long session for practice at inspection."

"…Yes, it was interesting."

"…It's been a while since I've seen someone like you. Most people find exactly what they need and go."

Hearing that, I felt, once again, that I was a somewhat unusual person in this room. It wasn't an unpleasant feeling.

---

The material was vast. The original request, opinion papers from the districts involved, the calculation's grounds, records of objections filed, all the way through to the final confirmation — it was all there. I turned through it page by page.

The objection records were especially striking. Who had filed the objection, what the objection was, and how the Consensus had recalculated in response were all set down. Some objections had been accepted, some had been rejected, and even the rejected ones had grounds attached explaining why.

*I was struck, reading through these grounds for rejection. Rejection hadn't been handled carelessly. Instead of closing the matter with one line — "insufficient grounds" — it explained, with numbers and cases, exactly why the grounds were insufficient. I thought, at the time, that this city was truly conscientious.*

The names of the people who'd filed objections were written as numbers too, and I noticed the same number recurring across several cases. I realized this was the trace of one person filing an objection more than once. The first and second had been rejected, and on the third, partly accepted. Reading through those three attempts in order, I tried to picture the face of someone who hadn't given up. There was no face. Only a number. And yet I could still sense that this person had been tenacious. That a record can show you an attitude even when it won't show you a face — that was the first time I understood that.

I read back over the passage where the third objection was partly accepted. It set down, too, exactly what had changed from the first two times. One newly submitted piece of material had been decisive — material that hadn't existed for the first two objections. Seeing that, I confirmed once more that this city truly did change its conclusion when the material changed. This wasn't a stubborn system. It was flexible in the face of new grounds.

---

That day I gathered the material and settled at an empty desk near the meeting room to read. No one asked me why I was sitting there. That evening I told Tyche about the experience.

"…I looked into a case that was completely unfamiliar to me today. It was really all there."

"…Of course. It's all there."

"…But the strange thing is, nobody says anything to me about it. About why I'm looking at it."

Tyche laughed.

"…Why would they ask? It's there to be looked at."

"…But other cities don't show any of this, apparently."

"…So I've heard. I've heard that too. But I don't really understand why they wouldn't show it. Isn't it because there's something to hide?"

I nodded at that. It sounded right. If there's nothing to hide, of course it would be shown.

---

I came to enjoy this habit of inspection often, after that. Not for any particular purpose — it was simply interesting. Picking an entirely unfamiliar case from an entirely unfamiliar district and reading it start to finish. I wondered why other people didn't do this.

I once asked the reading room librarian about it.

"…Does anyone besides me come look at unfamiliar cases like this?"

The librarian searched their memory for a moment and said,

"…Sometimes. But most people come looking only for what's connected to themselves. Someone who just reads something unrelated for its own sake — well, they're rare."

"…Has there been anyone else like me?"

"…There has. People like that were usually researchers. History, or statistics."

Hearing that, it struck me as a little odd that I was doing this without being a researcher at all. But being odd wasn't an unpleasant feeling.

The librarian gave me a further look around the reading room. The stacks weren't large, and a few electronic terminals sat around. A usage notice was posted on one wall, and it read, among other things: "All material in this reading room may be inspected by any citizen of the Equity. No separate approval procedure is required." I read that sentence twice. That no approval was required seemed to sum up this city better than any explanation could.

"…When was this reading room established?"

"…I don't know exactly, myself. It's old. I've heard it's been here since the city was founded."

I was satisfied with that answer. That it had been there from the beginning. It sounded to me like this hadn't been thrown together hastily later on, but that this city had decided, from the moment it was founded, never to hide anything.

Feeling good that day, before I left the reading room, I asked the librarian one more thing.

"…Is there anything you truly can't see here? Really?"

The librarian thought for a moment and answered.

"…Hmm, well, obviously nothing that hasn't been filed yet. Once it's filed, you can see all of it."

"…What about what hasn't been filed?"

"…That doesn't exist yet, so. How would you show something that isn't there."

I laughed at that answer. It was the obvious thing to say. Asking to be shown something that doesn't exist was a request that made no sense to begin with. I didn't find that answer strange in the slightest.

The librarian added, "…But once it's filed, you really can see everything. If there's ever something we can't show you, it's genuinely because it hasn't been filed yet. There's no other reason." I believed that. I had every reason to. Up to that day, I had never once had the experience of this city hiding something from me. So I had no reason not to believe it.

Leaving the reading room, I confirmed one thing to myself all over again. Every document I'd seen that day, anyone else could see exactly the same way. I hadn't been given some special privilege. I liked that equality.

---

I looked up the inspection regulations too. Surprisingly simple. Identity check, application, inspection. The only restrictions were on a small portion of personal information, and even that wasn't fully concealed — it was replaced with a number.

I liked that restriction. A method that protected a person while never hiding the record itself. A name got swapped for a number, but what had happened to that person stayed on the record, unchanged.

*Looking back now, this part strangely locks together with something else. Being turned into a number meant becoming anonymous — but it also meant that person could never again find their own name in this record. At the time I read this only as protection. I didn't know yet how to read it any other way.*

Since I'd gone looking through the regulation document, I looked over the old history of revisions to the inspection rules too. There had been several revisions, and most of them moved in the direction of making the procedure simpler. At first you'd had to write three pages of application; now it was one. At first approval had taken days; now it was instant. I liked that every one of these revisions moved in the direction of making inspection easier. This city was moving, over time, in the direction of ever more transparency.

I read that regulation document from beginning to end. The front held the inspection procedure, the back the restrictions. The restrictions were genuinely short. Exactly three lines. Personal information replaced with a number; certain items concerning minors carry a notice when inspected; and the last line read this: "Anything not specified in this regulation is not restricted from inspection."

I liked that last line best of all. That not restricting was the default. I guessed that in other cities it was probably the opposite — that only what was specified could be inspected, and everything else was forbidden. Here it was the reverse. Prohibition was the exception.

In the margin of that revision-history document, I found a note someone had left by hand, long ago. The ink had faded badly. It read: "Inspection is not a right — it is the baseline." Who had written it, and when, wasn't recorded. I liked that sentence enough to copy it into my notebook.

---

A few days later, I looked up a very old case. Something from about twenty years back. It was all there. Only the form from that era differed slightly from now — the content was every bit as thorough. Grounds, objections, confirmation, and at the very bottom, the signature box.

It was empty.

I didn't think that was strange then, either. That a twenty-year-old form had a signature box too, and that it was empty too, looked to me like nothing more than this city's consistency. If anything, it was reassuring. It meant this wasn't only true of recent documents — it had been this way, straight through, from long ago. Nothing had suddenly changed. This had simply always been what kind of city it was.

Returning that material as I left, I thought this had been a pretty good day. That in a city that hides nothing, you could open up anything at all. I thought that was freedom.

I told my aunt about it when I got home too. "…Auntie, I read a case today that had absolutely nothing to do with me. I even looked at one from twenty years ago." My aunt asked, looking curious, "…Why would you look at something like that?" "…No reason. It was interesting." My aunt laughed. "…That's such a you answer." I couldn't tell whether that was a compliment, but looking at her face, it seemed closer to one.

"…But Auntie, it really was all there. Nothing was hidden at all."

My aunt paused over the dishes and looked at me for a moment.

"…That's a good thing. But everything being there doesn't necessarily mean you can know everything."

"…What do you mean by that?"

"…No, never mind. I don't really know myself. Anyway, it's good that it's all there."

I didn't pay much attention to my aunt trailing off like that, at the time. She sometimes said things and then pulled them back in mid-sentence. I only thought of it as a habit of hers.

That night, before I fell asleep, I went back over, once more, the twelve cases I'd read that day, in order. None of them had felt forced. None of them had felt manufactured. The grounds had piled up the way grounds should, and the conclusion had followed from the grounds. I thought that was proof of a truly good city. A bad city, I thought, wouldn't have gone to the trouble of piling up its grounds this conscientiously. I fell asleep easily that night, thinking that thought.

The next morning, on my way to the meeting room, I passed the reading room again. The door wasn't open yet, but I slowed my steps a little and looked at it anyway. The fact that everything about this city was held inside, in that moment, felt like nothing more than reassuring.
