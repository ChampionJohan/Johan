# Chapter 3. The First Deliberation

> _Previously_ — Hora going back over, one by one, just how fair a city the
> Equity is. No lines, no favors, the loud never win. She likes this city.
> She genuinely likes it. On the morning of her second day, she sits down
> again with a thin stack of material.

I should describe the Fourth Deliberation District's meeting room in more detail, since the time I spent in that room is going to make up most of this record.

One long table, twenty-two chairs. At first I wondered why twenty-two, but no one knew. They only said it had always been that way. I checked whatever regulations I could find, and none of them said why the seat count was twenty-two. It was simply twenty-two.

Nineteen of those seats were filled. The other three, I heard, had stood empty for a long time. I learned only later that I'd nearly become one of those three — that the seat I sat in had, in fact, originally been one of them.

Nothing hung on the walls. There was one window, of frosted glass. Light came through, but you couldn't see outside. On the first day I thought that was strange. But after a few days, not being able to see out was actually a relief. There was nothing to let your mind wander off toward.

Of the nineteen people, I knew almost none of the faces. Their ages were all different. The oldest was older than my aunt, and the youngest only a couple of years older than me. I wondered what everyone had been doing before they came here, but no one introduced themselves. There was simply no occasion for it. I thought that was strange, but after a few days I saw it wasn't a bad thing either. If you didn't know what a person did, you could hear their words as just words, not as that person's words.

In front of each seat sat a small nameplate, but it held a number, not a name. My seat was number seventeen. I got confused for a while over whether that number was mine or the seat's, but I found out later it belonged to the seat. Meaning I was the person sitting in seat seventeen, not that I was seventeen myself. I didn't understand, then, why that distinction mattered.

There was no trace on the nameplate of who had sat in seat seventeen before me. The nameplate was as spotless as if it had been replaced with a fresh one the very day that person quit. Sitting down in that seat, for the briefest moment, I wondered whether some warmth from the person before me might still be left in it. There wasn't. The chair was just a chair. I still don't know, even now, whether that left me disappointed or relieved.

---

The first case up for deliberation was a materials reassignment.

The proposal was to divert part of a building-materials request from the eastern district out of the western district's supply. The west had a surplus, the east had a shortfall, and the Consensus had calculated the difference and sent it up to this room.

There was no chair's seat, as such. One of the people seated ran that day's session, but that person wasn't called a "chairperson." They were only called whoever was handling the session that day. A different person might handle the next one, I was told.

I asked the person next to me, that day, who decided who ran it. "…We take turns. There's an order to it." "…Who sets the order?" "…Seat number order. Today happened to be seat five's turn." I liked that answer. Even running the session wasn't anyone's choice. It was simply the order. Not because the person in seat five was especially capable, and not because the others trusted them. It was only that the order had come around to them.

Running the session didn't make that person's voice carry any more weight, either. The one running it still had to raise a hand and file an objection like anyone else, and never got to put a case in order first just because it was their turn to run it. I liked that this was the rule I found most agreeable in that room. A word from whoever held the microphone weighed exactly the same as a word from whoever didn't.

I looked up that rotation schedule later. The twenty-two seats turned in a fixed cycle. Which meant that seventeen, my seat, would someday come up for its turn running the session. I tried imagining how that day might feel. I couldn't quite picture it.

The rotation schedule still carried the three empty seats in it, unchanged. Even a seat no one sat in came up in its turn to run the session. When that happened, I was told, it was simply skipped. Something about that skipping stayed with me, somehow. The schedule remembered who was missing, but nowhere did it say why they were missing. The schedule knew who was absent but not why.

The person running that first session skimmed the material once and said,

"…The eastern request and the western surplus match what the Consensus calculated. Any objections?"

No one raised a hand.

"…If there are none, it's put in order."

And that was the end of it. I sat waiting for what came next, but the session had already moved on to the next case. I thought, at the time, that it had gone by so fast I must have missed something.

Later I opened the material again and read this first case from beginning to end. The request, the calculation's basis, a comparison table of holdings by district, three past cases of the same kind. It was all there. I didn't know, at the time, whether anyone besides me had read it to the end. The summary alone was already enough. I only read it to the end because I read slowly, not because I suspected anything. This distinction is one I end up making again, many times, later in this book.

---

The second case took a little longer.

This time there was an objection. Someone described as the western district's representative raised a hand and said,

"…The west also has construction planned for next spring, so if it's diverted now, there could be a shortfall come spring."

The person running the session wrote that down. Then said,

"…We'll check."

And they truly did check. An aide beside them tapped at a terminal and brought up next spring's planned construction on the screen. There really was planned construction in the western district. The Consensus recalculated it. It took a few minutes. A new result came back. This proposal would reduce the reassigned amount slightly, and draw a little more surplus from another district instead.

"…Will this do?"

The western representative looked at the new figures and nodded.

"…That's fine."

*I still call this scene to mind often, even now. No one got angry. There was no sense of anyone having beaten anyone. The western representative hadn't gotten everything they'd asked for, and their face was truly satisfied all the same. That satisfaction struck me as strange. In another city, I've since heard many times from people who came from outside, this moment would have had raised voices in it. Here, only numbers passed back and forth twice, in place of voices.*

I asked that western representative separately, later, why they'd been satisfied when they'd received less than they'd first asked for. They smiled briefly and said,

"…It's not that I got less. I got as much as I needed. What I first asked for, I'd padded generously."

"…Why pad it?"

"…In case it fell short. But once the Consensus recalculated, it turned out I didn't need to pad it after all."

Hearing that answer, I confirmed again that this person had neither lost nor won. The numbers had simply been squared. Squaring the numbers needed neither a winner nor a loser.

That person then went on gathering their materials and added one more thing. "…When I first started here, I couldn't break the habit of padding generously either. After a few years, I just started asking for what I actually needed. Because I knew the Consensus would square it either way." I wondered whether that could be explained with the word trust. It looked, to me, less like trust than like a state where being on guard had simply stopped being necessary. Whether not needing to be on guard is the same thing as believing — I didn't know then, and I still don't.

---

The way people spoke was strange.

No one said "In my opinion." No one said "I object." Everyone said, "This needs to be checked," "It will be put in order for you," "If that's what was passed on."

I couldn't tell whether this was modesty, or simply how these people talked. I asked the person next to me, quietly,

"…Does no one say what they actually think?"

"…This is what they think. Saying it needs checking is an opinion too. Saying it's fine is an opinion too."

"…But why not say it that way? …Just, like, I'm worried about this."

The person beside me thought for a moment, then said,

"…Say it that way, and it becomes my worry. Say it needs checking, and it becomes something everyone has to check."

I thought about that late into that night. That there was a way of speaking that kept a worry from becoming anyone's own — that was the first time I noticed such a thing existed.

For a while after that I practiced that way of speaking. At home, instead of saying "In my opinion" to my aunt, I tried saying "This seems like it needs checking." My aunt laughed. "…You've been going to that meeting room and now you talk like a government office." I was embarrassed and stopped, but the moment I stepped back into that room, the speech came out of me on its own. As natural as changing clothes.

*Looking back now, I think this was the beginning. Change how you speak, and how you think changes a little too, along with it. I may have been losing, little by little, the way of saying "what I think," even then. I didn't notice the losing, at the time. That becoming comfortable and losing something can arrive together — I only learned that much, much later.*

---

By the third case on, I'd already grown a little used to it.

A number was called, the summary was read aloud, objections were asked for, and if there were none it moved on, and if there were, it was checked and recalculated. Not one of the twenty-two seats ever said they had decided this. Everyone said it "was put in order."

The session ended after the sixth case. I checked the clock — it had only been a little over twenty minutes. I couldn't believe six matters had been handled in that short a time.

*Growing used to something is a frightening thing. On the first day, this pace felt so foreign that I kept checking the clock. But after sitting in that room more than two hundred times since, I stopped checking it. I've never measured exactly how long it takes for the foreign to stop feeling foreign. I think it would have frightened me, if I had.*

The fourth case was an adjustment to school assignments. The fifth was the order of road repairs. The sixth was a proposal to move one of the ration counters, because several complaints had come in that its current location was hard to reach, and the Consensus had calculated a new site. All six cases followed the exact same pattern. Summary, objection, check, put in order. Some finished in three minutes, some took nearly ten. The difference in time came down to whether there had been an objection or not — it didn't seem to have anything to do with how big the matter was. Something that touched children's lives directly, like a school assignment, and something as small as a counter's location, were handled at the same speed.

I thought that was strange, and then, thinking it over again, saw there was no reason it should be. To the Consensus, it was all the same calculation. The Consensus made no distinction between what people considered important and what they considered unimportant. Whether not making that distinction meant fairness, or meant something was being missed — I couldn't answer that, that day.

---

After the session ended, people filed out one by one. As I gathered my material, I asked the person who'd run the session,

"…What's decided today — who finally confirms it?"

That person looked at me and said, smiling,

"…It's already confirmed. Just now."

"…But the, um, signature, or something like that—"

"…Ah, the signature box. You don't have to fill that in. No one's ever said to fill it in."

I nodded at that answer. That day, I really did believe it was that simple. No one had said to fill it in, so it wasn't filled in. There was nothing more to it.

*Reading this exchange back now, I know that person wasn't deceiving me. They genuinely believed it that way too. That it wasn't filled in because no one had said to fill it in. They had probably never once wondered why no one had said so.*

"…Then why does that box even exist?" I asked again. The person thought for a moment and answered, "…Hm. It's probably just been there from the start. Maybe it just never got removed when the form was updated." I thought that answer sounded plausible too. Old forms tend to keep boxes no one needs anymore, after all. That was truly all I thought, at the time. That the box might still be there out of laziness, or because remaking the form was too much trouble. The thought that it might still be there because there was no one to fill it in — that never once crossed my mind, that day.

On the way home I passed the water channel. The water was still making its sound. This time I didn't stop. My head was full of the meeting room's twenty-two seats, and the frosted window, and six decisions no one had decided. The water channel was pushed far down my list of thoughts that day.

Leaving the meeting room, I looked once more at the frosted window. Outside was still invisible. That day, for the first time, I wondered whether that window had been frosted from the start, or had become that way later. There was no one suitable to ask, so I let it go.

My aunt had dinner ready and waiting when I got home. She asked how it had gone. I started to tell her what had happened that day, in order, and realized, once I actually tried to put it into words, that there wasn't much to say. "…We handled six cases. They all went fine." My aunt nodded and said, "…That's how it always is there. It goes fine."

I thought she was right. It really had gone fine. Not one thing about it had been bad. But that night, before I fell asleep, for no reason, I called back each of those six "it will be put in order" moments, one by one. I never did manage to remember who, exactly, had put any of them in order. The verb "put in order" had no subject. At the time, I only thought of that as a habit of speech.
