# Chapter 8. The One Who Reads to the End

> _Previously_ — Hora confirming with her own eyes that the Consensus
> truly hides nothing. An unrelated case, material from twenty years back —
> a single application form was enough to see it all. Her aunt let slip,
> almost in passing, "Everything being there doesn't necessarily mean you
> can know everything," but Hora didn't hold onto it.

I still haven't seen anyone in that meeting room, besides me, read the full text to the end.

I don't want to write this as if it were something to boast about. I've never once thought of it that way. Reading it all didn't make me any smarter, and it didn't lead me to any more correct a judgment. I simply couldn't sit comfortably if I hadn't read it all. That's all there was to it.

To be precise, it's less that I haven't seen anyone and more that I'm certain I never have. Every time a session ended, I stayed behind alone and read. Everyone else looked only at the summary and judged whether to object from that. That was enough. The summary was accurate.

---

I once asked the person next to me about it.

"…You don't read the full text?"

"…Why bother? It's all in the summary."

"…In case something's missing from the summary, maybe."

They looked at me like I was strange.

"…The summary is made by the Consensus too. If the Consensus could leave something out, it wouldn't be the summary you couldn't trust — it'd be the full text itself."

I couldn't argue with that. It was true. Making the summary was, in the end, the same system. If you trusted the system, you had to trust the summary too.

That person told me about when they'd first come to this room too. "…I used to read it all too, at first. About six months, I think. But it matched the summary every single time, so at some point I figured looking at just the summary was enough." I found that six months striking. I was already nearing two hundred days of reading, and this person had stopped after six months. I found myself wondering, in that moment, why I couldn't stop. No answer came.

*And yet I kept reading. Believing and habit lived on different levels. I believed in the Consensus, and at the same time, I couldn't stop reading to the end. At the time, I didn't think of these two things as a contradiction.*

That person went on to say a bit more about themselves. "…I read it too, at first. But after a few months it all started to look alike. The summary was never wrong, not once, so at some point you just stop reading." I understood what they meant. I checked too, every time I read. The summary really was never wrong, not once. But that checking itself couldn't stop in me. It wasn't that checking reassured me — checking was simply what I did.

"…How long do you think you'll keep reading everything like that, Hora?"

"…I don't know. I think I'll keep doing it."

"…Doesn't it wear you out?"

I answered that question honestly.

"…It does. But not reading makes me more anxious."

"…Anxious? About what?"

I couldn't answer that one right away either. I didn't know exactly what I was anxious about myself. It was just that if there was a document I'd skipped over without reading, thoughts of it kept coming back to me that night before I fell asleep. Only checking what I'd missed let me sleep easy.

"…I don't know. It's just how it is."

They nodded at that without any particular reply. I found that silence comfortable. It wasn't a silence that tried to talk me out of it.

A few days later, I happened to overhear that person talking about me to someone else. Not to badmouth me — just explaining me to another member. "…The new one's a little unusual, but not unusual in a bad way. The questions she comes up with after reading everything turn out to be useful, more often than not." Hearing that made my face heat up a little. At the same time it wasn't an unpleasant feeling. That was the first time I'd heard "unusual" and "useful" sitting in the same sentence.

Once, they asked me in passing, "…Doesn't it get boring, staying behind alone reading for two hours?" I thought about it and answered, "…Sometimes it does. But once I've read it all, that boredom fades fast." They laughed and shook their head. "…I can't stand that boredom long enough to read it all. You're really something." I still couldn't tell if that was a compliment, but it didn't sound bad.

After that, a few members started asking me things directly. "…Was there anything unusual in the full text of this provision?" "…What did the full text look like for that last case?" I answered conscientiously every time. Mostly I said there was nothing much. There really wasn't, most of the time. Sometimes I'd point out one small detail, though it never once changed a decision. But the fact that people had started asking me at all — that struck me as a little remarkable, in itself.

---

Tyche asked a similar question once too.

"…Why do you always read all the way through the full text, Hora?"

I couldn't answer right away. After a moment I said,

"…Just, it's a habit."

"…Why did the habit start?"

I hesitated, wondering whether to bring up my mother. In the end I didn't. It felt too personal.

"…I don't know. It just turned out that way."

Tyche nodded. They didn't press further. I was grateful for that consideration, but at the same time I felt my own answer had fallen short. It wasn't that I really didn't know. I just hadn't wanted to say it.

Tyche asked something else instead.

"…So what changes, once you've read it all?"

"…Hmm, nothing really changes. It's just that I end up in a state of knowing."

"…A state of knowing?"

"…Yes. A state where I've confirmed everything there is myself. Passing over something without confirming it makes me more uncomfortable."

Tyche looked thoughtful, hearing that.

"…I'm the opposite. It's a relief knowing the summary's right even without checking. But don't you not trust that knowing itself, Hora?"

That question caught me off guard. It wasn't that I didn't trust it. I did trust the Consensus. And yet I couldn't bear not checking. I couldn't find an answer, that day, for how to explain both of these things together.

Tyche looked a little sorry after saying that. "…Ah, that might have come across kind of harsh. I didn't mean it that way." "…No, it's fine. It's a question I'd never thought about myself." I really was fine. Tyche's question had been sharp, but not in a tone that pushed me into a corner. It had simply been curious. I liked that about them.

---

Once I'd read the full text in the meeting room, I'd also gotten into the habit of laying the summary and the full text side by side to compare them. The summary really was accurate. Never once wrong. But there were things in the full text that weren't in the summary. Mostly minor details. A past case similar to one in a given district, say, or the origin of a particular provision.

Every time I found details like this, I wrote them in my notebook. Not for any particular use. Writing them down was simply the habit.

I once showed that notebook to Tyche. They looked through it, one entry at a time, and said,

"…This is all correct. But why does it matter?"

"…I don't know if it matters. It was just there."

"…Being there doesn't mean it all matters."

I couldn't answer that either. It was true. Being there didn't mean it all mattered. But that day, too, I didn't throw the notebook away.

I'd actually started keeping notebooks before the meeting room, back when I was still in school. I had a habit of writing things in the margins of my exam answer sheets too. Teachers sometimes frowned on it, since it made the sheets messy. I always started to erase it, and ended up handing it in as it was, unable to. Erasing it felt stranger than leaving it.

The notebook had grown fairly thick over the course of a few months, that way. Sometimes, when I was bored, I'd flip back through it from the start. Most of it really was trivial. The origin of some district's name, why some provision's numbers had come out the way they had, which year had had an unusually high number of objections. None of it seemed connected to any of the rest. I thought that was only natural. This notebook hadn't been kept to dig up some suspicion — it was just things that had caught my eye while reading, copied down.

There was no particular order or classification anywhere in the notebook. It was just written in whatever order things caught my eye. Sometimes I actually liked that disorder. It wasn't an organized record — it was just the trace of the spots where I'd paused while reading.

I once showed the notebook to my aunt too. She flipped through a few pages and said, "…This is just like something your mother used to do." Surprised, I asked, "…Mom did this too?" "…Yes, she used to write things in the margins of her ledgers. Didn't seem to serve any particular purpose, but she liked it." Hearing that gave me a strange feeling. I couldn't tell whether this was a habit I'd inherited, or just a coincidental resemblance.

---

I mentioned something similar to the Secretary once too. I'd gone to return material at the counter, and since the Secretary happened to be there, I took out the notebook and showed them. They looked it over and their face turned briefly interested.

"…You record all of this?"

"…Yes, just a habit."

"…That's a good habit. I used to do something similar, myself."

"…Not anymore?"

The Secretary thought for a moment before answering.

"…At some point it just stopped. Once the work piled up."

I took that answer without much thought. It seemed plausible enough that a heavier workload could do that. I didn't notice, at the time, that "at some point" had come up again.

"…Did you used to carry a notebook around too?"

"…Something like that. A small notepad. I don't even know where it is anymore, now."

"…Wouldn't you want to look for it?"

The Secretary smiled briefly. "…Hmm. I wonder what the point would be, even if I found it." I sensed something like regret in that answer, but it never showed on their face at all, and I wondered if I'd only imagined it.

The Secretary looked back over my notebook and added a few more words.

"…Seeing this, you must like observing things, Hora."

"…Not so much observing — it's just that things catch my eye while I read."

"…That's what observing is. I used to be the same. I'd write down whatever caught my eye."

"…Why don't you anymore, then?"

The Secretary smiled briefly.

"…Convening and passing things on takes all day, so there's no room left for anything to catch my eye. And besides, whatever I might write down would become my own opinion, wouldn't it. My own opinion isn't allowed to go into the minutes."

I nodded at that explanation. This person had given up the habit of observing on their own. So that their own opinions wouldn't get mixed into the record. I thought that was a kind of professional restraint. How long, and how deeply, that restraint had changed this person — I couldn't gauge it, not then.

"…So what opinions are left in you now?"

I asked, on impulse. The Secretary looked at me for a moment, then laughed.

"…Hmm. That's a question I've never thought about."

That answer was light, like a joke, but I felt there was something behind that lightness. I didn't know exactly what. All that stayed with me was that the laugh had ended a little too quickly.

---

There was a time I wondered myself why I was so especially slow. Ever since school, everything took me longer than it took other people. Exams, reading, even eating.

I once asked my aunt about it.

"…Auntie, why am I so slow?"

My aunt thought for a moment and answered.

"…You're not slow. You just hate missing anything."

"…That's the same thing as being slow."

"…Slow and thorough are different things. It's just that the world usually only notices the slow part and doesn't give the thorough part its due."

That felt like comfort, hearing it, but it didn't entirely settle me either. The fact of being slow didn't change.

Once the meal was ready, my aunt told me an old story. When I was little, reading picture books, the other kids would skim just the pictures and move on, but I traced every single letter, she said. "…Your teacher worried you were slow. But I never worried." "…Why not?" "…The questions you asked after finishing were always different from everyone else's. They were questions that came out of being slow. They weren't bad."

Hearing that, I felt, all over again, grateful that there had been at least one person at home who never worried about my slowness. At school it had always been something to worry about; at home, it wasn't. I think that difference is part of what carried me this far.

"…But Auntie, in the meeting room, I'm the only one who's like this. Doesn't that look strange?"

My aunt laughed.

"…So what if it does. You're just doing it your way."

That comforted me a little. Your way. My aunt used that phrase so easily, but it always sounded difficult to me.

---

That evening, I stayed in the meeting room and read a new case from beginning to end. I read the summary first, then the full text, then the underlying material all the way through. It took three hours. The caretaker came and went twice.

Once I'd finished, I looked back over the last page one more time. The list of grounds, the progress of the handling, and down to the blank box at the very bottom. I wrote the gist of the day's case in my notebook in two lines and cleared my seat. That was all there was to that day. Nothing in particular weighed on me.

On the way home I passed by the water channel. The sound of the water was the same as ever. I didn't stop that day either. My legs ached from three hours of reading, and all I could think of was getting home quickly to lie down.

My aunt was waiting by the door. "…Late again today." "…Yeah, there was a thick one." My aunt served dinner without asking me anything. I was grateful for that. If I'd had to explain what I'd read every single day, I think I would have given up this habit much sooner than I did.
