# Chapter 9. Four Hundred People

> _Previously_ — Hora is the only person in the meeting room who reads the
> full text to the end. Asked by Tyche why, she only said, "Just a habit."
> Asked what changes once she's read it all, she said, "Nothing changes.
> I just end up in a state of knowing." Tyche wasn't entirely convinced by
> that answer, but didn't press further either.

I started paying close attention to the handling-progress page for a very small reason.

That day, as usual, I'd finished reading the full text and was skimming the handling progress attached at the very back. A table listing, in chronological order, which department had done what and when. I always flipped through this table fast. It was nothing but numbers and department names lined up, no name in sight, so there wasn't much pleasure in reading it.

That day the table was unusually long. Over two pages. Bored, I counted the lines.

Four hundred and twelve lines.

I thought at first I must have miscounted, and counted again. Same result. Four hundred and twelve lines. I pulled out the handling-progress tables from cases I'd read before and looked them over again. None of them fell below a hundred lines. Even a short case ran to about a hundred and thirty; a long one ran past five hundred. That was the first day I truly felt, in my bones, just how many hands had touched the forty pages of material I'd been reading, session after session. Until then I'd taken that part as nothing more than a table and passed over it.

---

I asked Tyche about that number the next day.

"…Is it right that four hundred and twelve people were involved in this case?"

Tyche flipped through the screen and nodded.

"…That's right. The average is around four hundred. Bigger cases run higher."

"…Four hundred people? Really?"

"…Yes. Why, does that surprise you?"

I was surprised. Having watched the sessions end in that twenty-two-seat meeting room, I'd been vaguely assuming those twenty-two people were the ones deciding. Four hundred didn't fit that picture at all.

"…But there are only twenty-two people in the meeting room."

"…The decision doesn't happen in the meeting room. The meeting room is only the last step. Before that there are people who compile the material, people who review it, people who take in objections, people who pass it on, people who confirm it. All separate people."

Tyche pulled out one more document to show me. The numbers were broken down by department. Sixty people for the material-gathering team, eighty for the grounds-review team, a hundred for opinion collection by district, and then everyone else who handled passing it on, recording, and confirming.

"…All of this is for one case?"

"…Yes. Bigger cases get split into even more."

---

I told my aunt about it that evening.

"…Auntie, apparently four hundred people are involved in a single case."

My aunt stopped what she was doing and looked at me.

"…Four hundred? What do you mean?"

"…There's a separate person who gathers material, a separate person who reviews it, a separate person who passes it on. All separate. So added up, it's over four hundred."

My aunt thought for a moment and said,

"…Then if something goes wrong, it's not one person's fault."

Hearing that, I realized it was exactly what I'd wanted to say. I took it as a good thing, that day. My aunt seemed to mean it as a good thing too.

"…Right. Since it's not one person deciding the whole thing, even if that person had bad intentions, the other four hundred would just check it again anyway."

My aunt nodded, ladling out the soup.

"…Hearing it like that, it's not a bad system. If anything's off anywhere, it'd get filtered out somewhere else."

*I remember this evening precisely. That day, the number four hundred put me at ease. I still don't think that ease was entirely wrong, even now. But at the time, I didn't think, even for a moment, to look at the other side of this number. I don't see that side until years later. It doesn't appear in this chapter. I've decided to write this record honestly, so I won't write what I didn't see then as if I saw it now.*

---

A few days later, curious about why this number had come to be, I went looking for the regulation behind it. The reading-room librarian found me an old piece of material — discussion records from forty-some years back, from the early days when the Consensus was first introduced.

Back then, far fewer people had been involved, it said. Around ten to a case. But there had been a problem. A few times, when someone acquainted with one of those ten filed an objection, the speed of handling or the outcome shifted, subtly.

*Checking into it, it wasn't anything you'd call manipulation. No bribes, no threats. Just listening a little longer to someone you knew, and passing over someone you didn't a little faster. A very small difference. But when that small difference piled up, it split the outcome.*

So the city chose to increase the number of people involved. It broke up what any one person could see into small pieces, so that no one could hold an entire case in their own hands. Whoever gathered the material didn't review it; whoever reviewed it didn't pass it on; whoever passed it on didn't confirm it. Each person touched exactly one small fragment.

The last line of the document read: *"The expansion of the number of participants is a measure to structurally dilute the influence of any given individual."*

I read that sentence twice. Dilute. I liked that word. Even if someone wanted to put a hand on something, the portion they could reach was so small that reaching it left them with no power at all. I thought that was a truly clever design.

The librarian found me one more piece from that old period. A deliberation over one district's ration amount, preserved as the last case on record from the ten-member-committee era. It later came out that one of the ten members lived on the same lane as the person who'd filed the objection. There hadn't been any improper favor. The investigation record said so plainly. But when that member had reviewed the objection, they'd said, in passing, "Ah, I know that household's situation" — and it had stayed on the record in the minutes exactly as said. The real problem was that this one remark hadn't tipped the outcome to either side. Because no one could prove it hadn't.

*Reading this record, I didn't feel, even for a moment, that this member had been a bad person. They'd simply recognized a neighbor. And yet this city judged that even that much recognizing was dangerous. I didn't think that judgment was excessively strict. If anything, I thought it had to be exactly that strict to live up to the name the Equity.*

---

A few days later, I told the Secretary this story too, when I stopped by the counter.

"…I read material from forty-some years ago today. Back then there were only ten members, apparently."

The Secretary paused their paperwork and looked at me.

"…That's right. Things were very different then."

"…Why did it grow all the way to four hundred?"

"…Because knowing someone keeps creeping in. When you know someone, you can't help but favor them a little. Even trying hard not to favor them, it still tips a little, in proportion to what you know. That happens even without any bad intent."

The Secretary glanced toward the window while saying that. The window you couldn't see through, and yet there were people who looked that way out of habit anyway.

"…So now no one knows each other, at all?"

"…No one does. Whoever gathers material doesn't know who reviews it. Whoever reviews it doesn't know who passes it on. Not knowing each other is what keeps things from tipping."

I found that a little sad to hear, and at the same time reassuring. That sadness and reassurance could sit together in one sentence didn't strike me as strange, at the time.

"…Do you know either, who these four hundred are?"

"…I don't. I only convene. The convening list is pulled by the Consensus and handed over. It isn't chosen by me."

I nodded, hearing that answer. Here too, once again, the Secretary said that nothing was chosen by them. That day I let the sentence pass. My mind had been taken up by the number four hundred; there was no room left to weigh that sentence.

"…Don't you think four hundred is a lot, though?"

The Secretary answered, starting to sort the paperwork again.

"…It is a lot. But knowing what happened when it was fewer — I think more is better."

I agreed with that. That day, I truly thought so.

---

I showed that document to Tyche the next day. Tyche already knew about it.

"…I learned about this too. At school."

"…This really seems like a great method."

"…It is. And that's why I've never once heard, in this city, of anyone being asked for a favor. Asking for one wouldn't do any good."

"…No good at all?"

"…None. Nothing is held in any one person's hands — so who would you even ask? There's no one to ask, from the start."

Hearing that, I thought of the "I'll talk to my uncle about it" I'd heard when I was fourteen. Not understanding it, back then, had been because in this city, there truly was nothing that could be settled by talking to an uncle. This number, four hundred, was exactly why. I found that connection striking.

"…But with as many as four hundred, isn't it actually harder to manage?"

Tyche shrugged.

"…It is harder. But that's exactly what the Consensus does. Gathering everything four hundred people did into one. A person couldn't do it. Because it's the Consensus, it can."

I nodded at that answer. If a person had to do it, this number would have been impossible from the start. To weave together the work of four hundred people, someone would have to know all four hundred, and no such person could exist. So this was, necessarily, work only a system could do, not a person. That day, for the first time, I clearly understood this was one of the reasons the Consensus was needed.

---

Out of curiosity that day, I picked one line at random from the handling-progress table. "Passed on 217, 2:20 p.m." I wanted to try finding out who this 217 was, and asked Tyche.

"…This 217 — can you find out who this person is?"

Tyche tapped at the screen a few times and shook their head.

"…That number is the counter that handled it. Not a person's number. Whoever was sitting at that counter that day becomes 217."

"…So yesterday's 217 and today's 217 could be different people?"

"…Yes, absolutely. Counters rotate in shifts."

That let a little of the air out of me. I'd been secretly hoping I might be able to follow one person all the way through this table. But there was no "one person" in the table to follow in the first place. The number was attached to a seat, not a person.

"…So you can't find anyone at all from this table?"

"…There's no need to find anyone, that's why. What matters isn't who did this work — it's that the work got done."

Hearing that answer, I felt hollow for a moment, and then found myself agreeing again. Thinking it through, Tyche was right. There wasn't really any reason I needed to know who 217 was. What I'd wanted to confirm was whether this had been done properly, and that was already fully set down in the table. Finding a person and confirming that work had been done were different matters. That day was the first time I drew that distinction clearly.

---

Leaving the meeting room that evening, I opened the handling-progress table once more. Four hundred and twelve lines. Each line held a number, a time, and one short word — "passed on," "reviewed," "confirmed," "convened."

I traced those lines with a finger and counted them all the way through. It took a while to count them all. While I counted, I felt strangely at ease. That four hundred and twelve people had each added one very small portion to make this single sheet of paper. It seemed like the higher that number climbed, the smaller the room got for any one person to harbor a bad intention.

On the way home I passed by the water channel. The sound of the water was still there. That day, for once, I stopped and stood watching it for a moment. The water, too, must have come flowing from somewhere, through many branches, to reach this point — and each of those branches felt, somehow, like one person's share out of the four hundred, and I laughed a little at that. I knew it was a comparison that made no real sense, but that's just what occurred to me, that day.

My aunt was waiting at the door.

"…What did you count this time?"

"…People. Four hundred and twelve."

My aunt laughed. "…Such a you kind of day." I only laughed back, without a reply.

Over dinner, I explained to my aunt everything I'd learned that day, from the start. That there used to be only ten people; that one acquaintance could tip the outcome; that this was why they'd grown it to four hundred. My aunt listened all the way through, spoon still in hand.

"…So, when there are fewer people, feeling gets mixed in, and when there are more, there's no room left for feeling to get in."

"…Yeah, exactly that."

"…But then, if there's someone really good among those four hundred, doesn't their share get diluted just the same?"

I couldn't answer that question right away. I'd never thought about it that way.

"…That's true, but isn't that unavoidable? To cut down a bad person's share, a good person's share has to shrink along with it."

My aunt looked at me for a moment and nodded.

"…I suppose so. I don't know which way is better, myself. But if it means fewer people get wronged, that's enough, I'd say."

I nodded at that answer. That day, I thought so too — that was enough.

*I slept very soundly that night. I slept trusting the number four hundred as a safeguard. When that trust begins to shake, reading on in this record will tell you. But in this chapter, I still doubted nothing. That my aunt's one remark — that a good person's share gets diluted too — was, in truth, the most precise thing said about this city that whole evening, I only come to understand much, much later.*
