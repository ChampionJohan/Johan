# Chapter 16. The Objection

> _Previously_ — Hora compared the summary against the full text, line by line. The summary was accurate. There was no lie in it. It was only that within the rule of five sentences, there was no room for four hundred ten people. There was no one to be angry at, no one to confront. So Hora decided to file an objection.

The objection form had three fields.

*Deliberation matter under objection*, *grounds for objection*, *requested action*. I wrote the document name in the first field. My hand stopped at the second.

I didn't know what grounds to write. "The summary is short" didn't seem like it would work as grounds — the summary had followed the rule. "No one decided" didn't seem like it would work either — that was true of every deliberation in this city.

*I sat in front of that field for about thirty minutes. Not because I had nothing to write, but because I had too much. If I wrote it all, I thought it would run past three fields.*

A sample objection form was posted on the wall at the reception desk. The sample was short and clear. *An error exists in the selection criteria for the target site of this deliberation, and a re-review is requested.* Reading that sample a few times, I realized what I wanted to say didn't fold up as neatly as this. What I'd felt as wrong wasn't contained in the single word "error."

I redrafted my note three times. The first draft ran too long and spilled past the field. The second draft had too much feeling in it. I wrote the sentence "I think this is wrong" and crossed it out. In this city's documents, the phrase "I think" didn't belong. I already knew, from what I'd learned, that a feeling with no grounds behind it wasn't something a deliberation dealt with.

In the third draft I cut all the feeling and kept only the facts. That turned out to be the shortest version, and also the one with the most force.

In the end, I wrote this:

*Among the proposed relocation sites, items 3, 7, and 12 remain in a state pending detailed confirmation and have been redesignated as unregistered regions. This repeats the exact same structure as the original problem. A re-review is requested for the four hundred ten people affected.*

---

I submitted it at the reception desk, and the clerk looked it over and tore off a receipt for me.

"…How does an objection get processed?"

"…It's referred to the deliberation district and reviewed again."

"…Who reviews it?"

"…The whole district. Same method as the original deliberation."

"…The same people?"

"…Yes, usually. Unless the assignment changes."

Hearing that, I found it a little strange. If the same people reviewed it again the same way, why would the result change? But I didn't say that thought out loud. There was a procedure, and I wanted to follow it and see.

*Thinking back now, I should have said that thought to the clerk then. Saying it might not have changed anything, but at least I would have known, sooner, what I was expecting.*

"…How long does processing take?"

"…This one's a big matter, so plan on it taking a while. About two weeks."

I took the receipt and left. A single sheet of paper. Stamped with a receipt number and the date.

On my way out I looked at the bulletin board. A chart titled "Objection Filing Status" was posted. It listed, side by side, the number of objections filed over the past year and, of those, the number of cases where the original decision had been changed. A hundred twelve filed, three changed.

*I stopped for a moment looking at that number. Three. Three out of a hundred twelve. I wondered what those three cases had been about, but I had no time that day and simply moved on. I would look into those three cases later, but that's much further ahead.*

*Oddly, I wasn't discouraged looking at that chart that day. A hundred twelve to three still meant that three, at least, had been changed. I believed mine could be one of those three. There was no basis for that belief, but at the time I didn't yet know it was a belief without basis.*

---

For those two weeks I handled other deliberations as usual. Other than having been referred, there was no news at all about the Seventh Classification case.

Every day during those two weeks, I passed the reception-desk bulletin board and looked at the objection-status chart again. The numbers stayed the same. A hundred twelve, three. I stopped in front of that chart for a few seconds every time. It was the kind of behavior that would look strange to someone watching, but no one thought it strange of me. No one here thought it strange to stand in front of a chart.

Once, I ran into the person from the next seat in front of that chart. The one who'd told me, on my first day, that just looking at the summary was enough.

"…I heard you filed an objection."

"…I did."

"…It won't be easy. I filed one for a small matter once, myself. Came out the same."

"…Same reason — no procedural defect?"

"…Yes, the exact same phrasing."

Hearing that, I felt puzzled — whether "the exact same phrasing" meant a standardized reply, or whether it really had been that there was no procedural defect every single time. *Thinking about it now, both were probably true. The procedure really was followed correctly every time, and that's exactly why the same phrasing kept coming out.*

I asked Tyche how it was going.

"…How far has the objection gotten?"

"…It's under review at the district. I don't know much more than that either."

"…Can the person who filed the objection give a statement?"

"…You can. In writing."

"…Not in person?"

"…The original deliberation is mostly written too. Objections are the same."

I wrote up more supporting material and submitted it in writing. I copied the table for items 3, 7, and 12, marked the question mark and the blank cell, and noted that there was no market at the destination site where the support funds could actually be spent. *I thought this was fairly concrete grounds for the problem. At the time I believed I'd found something new. I thought I'd seen something no one else had.*

*I found out later, though. That table had already been reviewed once, during the deliberation itself. The conclusion of "temporary designation" had come out of that very review. I hadn't found anything new — I had simply read, belatedly, something that already existed.*

Even after submitting the written statement, I went to the archive every afternoon to check on the objection's progress. The status screen only showed stages. *Received → Referred → Under Review → Result Notified.* I watched that screen stay stuck at "Under Review" every day. It stayed that way through the tenth day.

On the tenth day, on a hunch, I called the reviewing department. Phones weren't a common way of doing things in this city either, but the receipt had an inquiry number printed on it.

"…Could I ask about the progress on the objection for the Seventh Classification matter."

"…It's under review now."

"…How much longer?"

"…If it stays on schedule, it should come out sometime this week."

"…Could I ask which part is being focused on?"

"…We're looking at exactly what you wrote in your grounds. Items 3, 7, and 12."

That answer wasn't given carelessly. It really meant they were looking at exactly what I had written. *Hanging up, I understood that this person hadn't given me a short answer to brush me off. The situation had simply been simple enough to warrant a short answer. There were only the three items I'd written to review.*

---

Two weeks later, the notice of the objection's outcome arrived.

A two-page document. I read this one to the end too.

The first page was a summary of the objection's substance. What I had written had been summarized accurately. The unconfirmed status of items 3, 7, and 12, the request to re-review the four hundred ten people affected. Nothing was missing.

The second page was the review's result.

*Upon review, the pending confirmation status of items 3, 7, and 12 is determined not to be a procedural defect, and it is confirmed that inter-departmental consultation is ongoing. The reclassification of the region as unregistered is a temporary measure for administrative convenience prior to implementation and is expected to be finalized by the implementation date. The original decision is therefore maintained.*

I read that sentence several times.

*It was true that it wasn't a procedural defect. There really was no defect. It probably wasn't a lie that the consultation was ongoing, either. Only when that "ongoing" would end wasn't written here either.*

There was a signature box at the bottom of the second page too. I looked at it reflexively. Empty. *By now I didn't startle seeing that box empty. If anything, it would have startled me more if it had been filled.*

---

I decided to go find the department in charge directly and ask.

The person who had handled the objection review was a woman who looked to be middle-aged. Documents were stacked on her desk.

"…I received the review result. When does the consultation end?"

"…That's not something I handle. It has to go over to the relevant department to know."

"…Then which department is that?"

"…The department that manages the relocation sites. It should be in the document."

"…Could I ask them and find out?"

"…They'll probably have to pass it further up too. That's usually how it goes."

Hearing this, I had a guess. It wasn't that there was nowhere to ask. There was always somewhere else to ask. Only the end was nowhere in sight.

*I remember this moment. I already knew that the structure — passing things up, layer by layer — hadn't been designed badly. If anything, it was a structure built so that no single person could decide unilaterally. But that good structure was making it impossible to find the one single person who knew what I wanted to know.*

"…I filed this objection because I thought there was a problem here."

"…Yes, I understand that. But what we confirmed was the procedure. The procedure was all followed."

"…Isn't the procedure being followed and this being okay two different things?"

She looked at me and was silent for a moment. Her face wasn't angry.

"…You're right. They're different things. But I'm the person who looks at the procedure."

*Hearing that answer, I knew this person, too, hadn't lied, just as Tyche hadn't. Her job really was to look at the procedure. Whether there was anyone who looked beyond the procedure — I started looking for that from that moment on.*

I asked her, on the spot, for the list of everyone involved. It was public material, so it wasn't hard to get.

"…What are you going to do with this?"

"…Go visit them, one at a time."

She looked at me for a moment. Her face wasn't trying to stop me.

"…There are four hundred thirty-two of them."

"…I know."

"…You're going to visit all of them?"

"…Just a few, to start."

She didn't ask any further and printed out the list for me. Several sheets stapled together. No names — only department, position, and stage of involvement. Passing on it, stage 1. Putting it in order, stage 2. Confirming, stage 3. Convening, stage 4. It was laid out that way.

*Taking hold of this list, I felt a strange sensation. A list of four hundred thirty-two people with not one name on it. In exactly the same way that the people of the Seventh Classification had no names, the names of the people on this list were also missing. Not the measured and the measured-against — both sides, without names. In this city, that was simply how things were done.*

---

On my way home I took out the receipt and the notice side by side. Receipt number, processing period, review result. All of it connected up properly.

*What I want to say in this chapter is one thing. The objection procedure had no flaw. I submitted a written statement, I received a review, I received a result. Every stage was recorded, and every record was open. And yet at the end of that flawless procedure, four hundred ten people's places were still empty.*

*That night I had a thought for the first time. If the procedure wasn't wrong, was what I was trying to change the wrong direction? I held onto that thought for a long time, but I couldn't reach an answer.*

*I also thought this. If the objection had been accepted and the original decision reversed, I would have felt satisfaction in this procedure. I would have easily concluded that the procedure had disappointed me. But the procedure hadn't disappointed me. It had simply worked, from beginning to end, honestly — and this was the result of that honest operation. That this was a far more difficult problem to handle was something I realized for the first time that day.*

I told my aunt the result, and she was quiet for a moment before asking,

"…So what are you going to do now?"

"…I don't know. But I'm going to meet the people directly."

"…Who?"

"…Everyone involved in this matter. There's a list."

My aunt looked at me. Her face was worried, but she didn't try to stop me.

"…How many is it?"

"…More than four hundred."

My aunt heard that number and said nothing. In front of that number, I wasn't confident myself that I could really meet all four hundred. I just wanted to meet even a few.

My aunt cleared the soup bowls and said,

"…Hora. It might not be those people's fault."

"…I know. I'm going to meet them anyway."

"…What are you going to ask when you meet them?"

I couldn't answer right away. After a long moment I said,

"…I don't know. I just feel like someone must know."

"…Know what?"

"…Why this turned out this way."

My aunt didn't ask any further. That night I spread out the list again and drew a line under the first name — not a name, but the first department and position — and underlined it. Passing it on, stage 1, initial receiving officer.

*Drawing that line, I didn't feel like I was starting something great. I had simply decided what to do next. Deciding what to do next was, at the time, the entirety of what I could do.*

*I fell asleep with the list beside my pillow. The next morning I was going to go find the first person. I had no idea at all, then, what that person would say to me. If I had guessed, I might have been a little less surprised. I had no preparation at all when I went and knocked on that door.*
