# Chapter 21. There Is No Subject

> _Previously_ — The Secretary answered everything Hora asked, without a single stumble. Not confirmed yet, calculation is finished, convening is done. Not one thing snagged, and yet Hora came home feeling, strangely, like nothing had resolved at all. She found the name for that strangeness only the next morning.

The name for the strangeness was simple. There was no subject.

The thought came to me the moment I opened my eyes that morning. Going back over what the Secretary had said the day before, I realized not a single sentence had ever had "I" in it. Not confirmed. Calculation finished. Implementation date set. Regulation amended. Meeting convened. All of it went that way.

I'd already noticed this once before. At the time I'd let it go as simply a manner of speech. Tyche, too, had said, "That's just how the Secretary's job is." I'd accepted that and moved on.

*But it wasn't a manner of speech. That morning, I saw for the first time, head-on, that this was too perfect to be called a mere habit. We'd talked for over two hours, and not one sentence began with "I did." If a person keeps a manner of speech without a single slip for two straight hours, that isn't a habit anymore. It's something else.*

Eating breakfast that morning, I ran back through the way everyone I'd met had spoken. The initial receiving officer had said, "I only passed it on." The surveying officer had said, "I only measure." All twelve of them had used "I." Even when saying what they'd done was small, that small thing always had an "I" attached in front. Only the Secretary was different. Even though what he'd done was far bigger, he never placed himself in front of it.

I said as much to my aunt too.

"…Aunt, all twelve of them said, 'I did it.' Only the Secretary doesn't."

"…Isn't that because those people think they did something small? They say the important people talk humbly."

"…But it feels different from humility. If you're being humble, you still say 'what, me' but you still put in the 'I.' The Secretary doesn't put anyone there at all."

My aunt tilted her head at that but didn't press any further.

---

That afternoon I went back to the reception desk. The Secretary was in the same seat as the day before.

"…Hora, back again."

"…Yes, there's something else I wanted to ask, from yesterday."

"…Ask me anything."

I went straight to the point.

"…Secretary, did you know that not once, in everything you told me yesterday, did you say 'I did it'?"

The Secretary looked at me a moment. It wasn't a surprised face. If anything, a thoughtful one.

"…Is that so."

"…Yes. Not confirmed. Calculation finished. Convening done. It was all like that. Not once did you say, I confirmed it, I convened it."

"…I suppose I did."

"…You're doing it again right now. It's not 'I did that,' it's 'I suppose I did.' If it were you saying it normally, I'd think you'd have said something more like, 'I must have said it that way.'"

The Secretary gave a small smile at this.

"…It seems you know my own way of speaking better than I do, Hora."

I didn't feel that smile as unkind. And yet I didn't stop there, and kept asking.

"…Why do you talk that way?"

---

The Secretary set his papers aside and looked at me straight on.

"…What do you mean by that?"

"…You're missing from everything you've done, Secretary. Convening happened, confirmation didn't happen, results came out. You talk as though it all just happened on its own. But you're the one who actually did it."

"…I did do it, yes."

"…Then why don't you say it that way?"

The Secretary went quiet for a moment at that question. I felt that this silence was different from his usual silences. In front of other questions, he'd pause briefly while looking something up or reaching for a memory. This silence wasn't that. It wasn't a silence of searching for something. It felt more like a silence of looking at something.

*I remember those few seconds exactly, even now. The window let in no view of outside, and the room was quiet. During that silence I half-wondered whether I was pushing too hard, and felt a little regret. But before I could put that regret into words, the Secretary spoke first.*

"…How do I put in something that isn't there."

---

I heard that and, for a moment, didn't understand what it meant.

"…Sorry?"

"…The subject. How do I put in something that isn't there."

I stayed still for a moment. This didn't sound like an excuse. It didn't sound like an apology either. His tone was exactly the same as always — the same tone from yesterday explaining the site-confirmation procedure, the same tone from this morning's greeting. No different.

"…What do you mean by that?"

"…If you ask me what I did, I can answer. Convened, passed it on, kept the record. Those are things that exist, so I can answer for them. But putting 'I' at the front of a sentence is a different matter. That's putting in something that isn't there."

"…But you're there. You exist, Secretary."

"…I exist, yes. But the 'I' that would go in that place, in that sentence — that doesn't exist. I'm not the one who made that sentence happen. I'm not the one who made that matter turn out the way it did, either. I only stood on the path the thing traveled along."

*Hearing this answer, I felt something strange. This wasn't an excuse. It wasn't an apology. He wasn't trying to dodge his own wrongdoing right now. He really was reporting a fact. That there was no subject to put into his own sentence — he was saying it the way you'd report that an item wasn't in the storeroom.*

---

It was a long while before I spoke again.

"…So do you think you did nothing at all, Secretary?"

"…No. What I did exists. Convening, passing things on, keeping the record. That I did. It's only that I can't say I "made" the result those things add up to."

"…What's the difference?"

"…If I hadn't convened, the meeting wouldn't have opened. That much is true. But that I convened it doesn't mean the decision that came out of that meeting became my decision. I'm the one who opens the door. What gets decided inside the door isn't my share."

"…But you keep opening it. Every single time."

"…Yes. That's my job."

I tried to find something to grab hold of in that answer, and couldn't. His logic was every bit as gapless as Tyche's. Only Tyche's logic hadn't been cold, and neither was the Secretary's. Both were simply exact.

"…Secretary, then what would you do if someone said to you, 'isn't this your responsibility'?"

"…I've rarely heard anything like that. But if I did, I'd tell them the one piece that's mine. What I convened, what I passed on. That's as much as I have to give. I can't give what isn't there."

"…But then no one takes responsibility for anything."

"…I think it would depend on how you use the word responsibility. For the piece I did, I take responsibility. If the convening was wrong, that's my fault. But if you ask who takes responsibility for the whole outcome, I don't know that. I've never seen the person who's supposed to be sitting in that seat."

I copied this down on paper as he spoke, feeling that he'd just said something very important to me, almost in passing. That he'd never seen the person supposed to be sitting in that seat. He hadn't said that seat doesn't exist at all. He'd only said he didn't know whether it existed or not — that he, himself, had simply never seen it.

---

There was one more thing I wanted to confirm.

"…Did the person who held this position before you speak this same way, too?"

"…The one before me? I never met them. They resigned before I arrived."

"…Do you know why they resigned?"

"…No. It wasn't written in the handover documents."

I paused for a moment, then asked,

"…Was that person's signature box empty too, by any chance?"

The Secretary took an old handover binder from his drawer and leafed through it. It smelled of old paper.

"…Yes. It's empty."

"…So would that person have spoken without a subject too, like you?"

"…That's something I can't know. But this way of speaking doesn't seem to be mine alone. When I was first learning this job, the person who taught me spoke this way too, as I recall."

"…Was that person a Secretary too?"

"…Yes. Secretary of a different deliberation district."

Hearing that answer, I began to think this wasn't one person's personality — it was something attached to the seat itself. I remembered something Tyche had said before: that a person who wants to stand out doesn't last long in this job. If people who don't stand out are the ones who stay in this seat for a long time, and if the speech of those who stay a long time hardens into this shape, then this wasn't any one person's problem.

---

I wanted to check one more thing right there. I went to the archive and pulled out five years' worth of old minutes. All of them written by the Secretary. I sat at a desk and went through them page by page, counting how many times a sentence began with "I."

I went through nearly a hundred pages. Not one single time. Convened, distributed, confirmed, received. It was all like that. Only in one corner of one very old minutes record was there a trace of a correction by hand. Someone had written "I distributed" and struck a line through it, rewriting it as "was distributed." The ink color was different. It had clearly been corrected later.

*I stared at that trace for a long time. There was no way to know who had corrected it, or when. The Secretary himself might have looked back and corrected it later, or someone else might have corrected it while reviewing. Either way, that struck-out "I" felt, to me, like the one remaining trace of something that still lingered somewhere in this city. Long, long ago, in this very seat too, someone had once written "I."*

I kept that minutes page in my mind like a photograph and left the archive.

---

"…Secretary, isn't this hard for you?"

I hadn't known this question was coming, myself. It was only after it left my mouth that I realized it was the question I'd really wanted to ask.

The Secretary thought a little longer for this question.

"…I'm not sure what you mean by hard. I do the work I'm meant to do. I know that work produces something. But to say I made it — I'm only a very small piece of that whole thing."

"…Even a small piece is still a piece."

"…That's true. So I do acknowledge my piece. What I convened, what I passed on, what I recorded. That piece is mine. But attaching my name to the whole picture — that would be manufacturing something that isn't there and pinning it on myself."

I heard that and couldn't press any further. Not because I had nothing left to ask, but because I no longer felt certain he was hiding something from me. He wasn't hiding anything. He really believed, truly, that there was nothing to put in front of his own sentence.

---

That evening I told my aunt about it. She stopped, mid-spoon, and looked at me for a long while.

"…He said, how do I put in something that isn't there?"

"…Yeah. It wasn't an apology, wasn't an excuse. He just said it, plainly."

"…Why does that stay with you so much?"

I couldn't answer right away. After a long moment I said,

"…It would have been better if he were lying. But he really believed it. That he has no subject."

My aunt didn't say anything more and slid the rice bowl over to me. I turned that sentence over late into the night. How do I put in something that isn't there. I thought again about the moment he'd once slipped and put "I" in by mistake, then corrected himself. At the time I'd thought it was just a habit slipping out. Now I saw it differently. He really was living in a place with no subject, and the occasional "I" that slipped out wasn't a mistake — it was a very small trace still remaining in that place.

The next day I told Tyche this too. She heard me and blinked, wide-eyed, for a moment.

"…He said, how do I put in something that isn't there?"

"…Yes. Those were his exact words."

Tyche went quiet for a moment, then said,

"…That's so like him."

"…What do you mean?"

"…The Secretary really can't lie. I know that. But hearing this, I think maybe not lying and not putting himself into a sentence might be the same thing."

I heard that and thought for a long while. It felt like Tyche had said something important, but exactly what it was, I couldn't quite pin down that day.

*That night I copied this sentence onto paper. How do I put in something that isn't there. I decided that night to put this sentence at the center of this record. If this book ever holds anyone guilty of anything, it isn't on account of this sentence. This sentence wasn't a crime. It was simply a fact.*

*Writing this book, I've rewritten this chapter more times than I can count. Several times I felt the urge to paint the Secretary badly. But every time, I found myself returning to that sentence. How do I put in something that isn't there. There was no falsehood inside that sentence. I couldn't force a crime that wasn't there into him.*

*I still didn't know, standing before that fact, what I could do. I might have been able to push him and force him to say, "I decided this." But words wrung out that way could never be true. Making him say something that wasn't there was as much a falsehood as making him deny something that was there. That night, for the first time, I accepted that this problem wasn't one I could solve by pushing him.*
