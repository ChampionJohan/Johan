# Chapter 17. I Only Passed It On

> _Previously_ — The objection was formally received, formally reviewed, and dismissed as "no procedural defect." Hora received the list of those involved — four hundred thirty-two people. She decided to go find the first name on it: the initial receiving officer.

At the top of the list it read, "initial receiving officer, Ninth Administrative Office."

I didn't know where that office was, so I asked at an information desk three times. The Ninth Administrative Office was at the far south end of the city, in a district I'd never once been to.

The clerk at the desk marked out the route on a map and asked,

"…What takes you all the way out there? Most people never go that far."

"…I'm going to meet someone."

"…Someone you know?"

"…No. This will be the first time."

The clerk didn't press further and handed me the map. In sixteen years living in this city, I'd received this kind of consideration more times than I could count. *This city's people rarely ask why. Not asking is a kindness — that's what I'd learned, and I'd learned it well. But that day, I found the kindness a little disappointing. If anyone had asked me, "Why are you going, is something wrong," I probably would have poured out everything right there.*

The walk took more than an hour. As I walked, I rehearsed in my head, several times over, what I would ask. In the end I couldn't use a single word of what I'd practiced.

*Writing this chapter, I want to set down precisely how nervous I was that morning. I felt as though I were bracing for a confrontation. I went in ready to fight someone. As it turned out, almost none of that preparation was needed.*

*After this chapter I go on to meet eleven more people, and I won't write each of those meetings out in this much detail. But I want to set down this first meeting in full. Every subsequent thing that happens has its shape already, entire, inside this one.*

---

The Ninth Administrative Office was a small building. It had three service windows and six waiting chairs. No one was waiting.

A notice board on the wall listed the office's business. *Receiving unregistered-residence lists, formal review, forwarding to the higher department.* Only those three things. Reading them, I understood that these three things were the whole of what the people here did all day long. Nothing more, nothing less.

I showed the list to the person seated at the reception window and asked,

"…Which of you is the initial receiving officer?"

She looked at the list and pointed at the seat beside her own.

"…That's me. I received it."

I lost my words for a moment. I hadn't expected to find him this easily. Of four hundred thirty-two people, the very first one, sitting in the exact seat the list said — and it really was her. I'd expected finding this person would take some remarkable effort. It hadn't. Even without a name, knowing only the department and the seat, finding a person in this city was not hard.

"…Are you the one who received the Seventh Classification matter?"

"…Yes, that's me. Why?"

There was no wariness in her voice. Only a plainly curious one.

---

I sat down in a chair and explained the situation — that I'd filed an objection, that it had been dismissed, and that I was going to meet the people involved directly.

She listened to me to the end. She didn't interrupt in the middle. While I spoke, she stopped tidying the papers on her desk and looked at me. Her manner of listening was earnest.

*I still think, even now, that her earnestness was striking. She could have treated me as a nuisance visitor. A sixteen-year-old had come to talk about an objection. But there wasn't the slightest trace of that in her. This city's people were mostly like that. Listening to someone to the end wasn't a special thing here.*

"…So this is about items 3, 7, and 12?"

"…You know about it?"

"…The summary of your objection came to us too. It gets circulated to every related department."

*Hearing that answer, I was surprised for a moment. It meant my objection had reached all four hundred thirty-two people. This, too, hadn't been hidden. I was actually puzzled that she'd agree to see me at all, already knowing about my objection.*

"…What do you think about this matter, then?"

She thought for a moment before answering.

"…I can tell you what I processed. But I haven't really thought much about the matter itself."

"…Why not?"

"…What I did was receive it. When the unregistered-residence list comes up, I receive it and forward it to the higher department."

---

I asked her more specifically what she had done.

"…All I did on this matter was one thing. I took the unregistered-residence list forwarded from the regional management office, checked the format was correct, and sent it up."

"…You didn't make the list?"

"…No, the list comes from the regional management office already made. I just check that it matches the set format."

"…And the relocation sites?"

"…Not my responsibility. Other departments handle that after I forward it."

"…Did you know items 3, 7, and 12 remained temporarily designated?"

"…I saw it in the summary of your objection. I didn't know before that."

"…You didn't know before?"

"…No. What I do stops at receiving. I don't see what happens after that."

Something caught at me in that answer. I say "caught," but I felt no sense at all that she was lying. She really seemed not to have known.

"…Then who makes the list in the first place?"

"…The regional management office. They survey the land, confirm registration status, and make the list."

"…Do they know how this list gets used?"

"…I couldn't say. I don't see that side of it either."

"…So is there actually anyone who knows the whole picture in this matter?"

She thought for a moment and said,

"…The district committee members would, wouldn't they. They're the ones who gather it all and look at it."

I heard that and laughed inwardly. I was that very committee member. And I hadn't known either. Not until I'd read all three hundred pages instead of the forty-page summary.

*In this moment I felt something turn over inside me. She believed there was someone out there who knew the whole picture. At first, so had I. But now I know. There's no one appointed to see the whole picture. You only see it if you're the one willing to look.*

I asked her, too, where the regional management office was located. I meant to go there next.

"…There's a lot of people there too. I don't know who's in charge, myself."

"…Then how do I find out?"

"…Go and ask, and they'll tell you. Everyone knows exactly what they did."

That turned out to be true. And over the following weeks, I would keep confirming just how accurate that statement was. Everyone knew exactly what they had done. Only no one knew what the whole added up to mean.

---

"…But with a matter this big, don't you feel like something's off? It's eleven hundred forty people."

She looked at me and thought for a moment.

"…To be honest, I see intakes like this every week. This one was one among them. It was a big one, sure, but I only look at the quantity. If the format's right, I forward it."

"…Doesn't a bigger quantity make you more careful?"

"…It does concern me. But my job isn't to make judgments. I only passed it on."

"…Who makes the judgment, then?"

"…I don't know exactly either. It gets reviewed at the higher department, and that goes up to the district, and the district decides it through the Consensus, doesn't it."

"…Isn't there someone who judges it somewhere in the middle?"

"…It's less a judgment than each person confirming at their own stage. I confirm the format, the next person confirms the quantity, the next confirms the basis. That's how it builds up into a decision. It's not a structure where one person judges."

Listening to that explanation, I realized I was hearing the very same thing this city had taught me from the start. The Consensus doesn't weigh — it gathers. I'd learned to call that a humble system. Hearing her say it, that humility began to show a different face.

*I remember this sentence exactly, even now. I only passed it on. She didn't say it like an excuse. It wasn't a defensive tone either. It was simply the tone of someone stating a fact. I couldn't find any shame in that tone anywhere. There was nothing to be ashamed of.*

*I've used this sentence as the title while writing this book. It's this chapter's title, and it might as well be this Part's title too. Everything about this city is in this one sentence — I've come to think that many times since that day. Saying "I only passed it on" isn't a lie. It really is only passing it on. The problem is that four hundred thirty-two people, each, only passed it on.*

---

There was one more thing I wanted to ask.

"…So what do you do if something like this feels wrong to you?"

"…I point people to the objection window. I've actually guided a few people there myself."

"…Have you yourself ever filed an objection?"

She looked at me a moment and laughed. It wasn't a mocking laugh.

"…Me? What would I know to object over? I'm just someone checking the format."

"…But you must have felt something was strange, at least."

"…I have. But feeling something is strange and being sure it's wrong are different things. I don't know the whole picture of this matter. All I see is the one sheet of list that comes into the intake box."

*I thought about this for a long time. She was right. She really did see only one sheet. Almost no one in this city had read all three hundred pages the way I had. Most people saw only the one page in front of them, and if that one page didn't look strange, they moved it along to the next.*

---

Finally I asked,

"…Is there anything you regret, by any chance?"

She thought for a long while before answering.

"…Regret… I'm not sure. Since I don't know what I did wrong, I don't know what I should regret either. But hearing what you've told me, Hora, I can't say my mind is at ease."

"…What do you mean, not at ease?"

"…That there was a person behind the paper I passed along — it felt more real to me today than it usually does. Usually it was just a number."

"…So what do you want to do now?"

She thought for a moment.

"…I don't think there's much I can do. I still don't know the whole picture. But next time a list like this comes through, I think I'll look at it a little more closely."

"…What difference does that make?"

"…Honestly, I don't know. It might not make any difference at all. But like I said, after today I don't think it'll just look like numbers to me anymore."

I understood this answer wasn't promising any great change. And yet it didn't feel entirely empty either. *Writing this chapter, I still don't know how to weigh this answer. Is it more honest to say nothing will change, or to say something will? She said, a little of each, both at once.*

I thanked her and left. She stood and saw me out until I was gone. It wasn't a special courtesy. Just this city's ordinary manners.

---

*Leaving the Ninth Administrative Office, I felt something hollow. I'd expected to hear something decisive from her — a hidden fact, someone's wrongdoing. Instead all I'd heard was one person's honest account of her ordinary day's work.*

*I think I have to set down this hollowness honestly. I was a little disappointed that day. Maybe I'd wanted to find a bad person, someone I could hate. If there'd been someone to hate, this would have all been much simpler. But I couldn't find anyone to hate. Instead, four hundred thirty-one more people I couldn't hate remained.*

*And I didn't know yet, that day, that this same honest account would repeat itself four hundred thirty-one more times. If I had known, I would have been a little less shocked. Or — maybe I'd have been more shocked. Once can be coincidence. Four hundred times is not.*

On my way home I marked the first line on the list. *Confirmed.* Beside it I wrote in small letters: *Not ashamed. No lies.*

I told my aunt about the person I'd met today. She stopped in the middle of setting out dinner.

"…What did this person do wrong?"

"…No, she says she didn't do anything wrong. Listening to her, I don't think she did either."

"…Then why did you go see her?"

"…I thought she might know something."

"…Did you find out anything?"

I thought for a moment and answered.

"…I found out this person doesn't know the whole picture."

My aunt looked at me.

"…So will the next one know?"

"…I don't know. But there are four hundred thirty-two on the list, so if I go one by one, maybe someone who knows will turn up."

My aunt didn't answer that and finished laying out dinner. We ate that night mostly in silence.

*That night I spread out the list again. I traced the second line, the third line, the fourth line with my finger. Department names and positions ran on. I still believed then that if I met all these people, sooner or later I'd find someone who said, "I decided it."*

*How long that belief lasted, I write in the next chapter. To say it now, ahead of time: not long.*
