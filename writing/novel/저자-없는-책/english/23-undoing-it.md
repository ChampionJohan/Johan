# Chapter 23. Undoing It

> _Previously_ — The Seventh Classification matter was confirmed. It wasn't that Hora had been late. The Secretary had said, "There's no one in this procedure that it waits for." Hora could find no mention anywhere in the confirmation notice of a procedure for undoing it. Whether that meant it was forbidden, or simply that no such thing existed, she still couldn't tell.

The next day I went to the archive.

What I was looking for was simple: a procedure for undoing a confirmed deliberation. Cancellation, withdrawal, annulment — I didn't care what it was called. I already knew that every procedure in this city had a name. The objection-filing procedure had a name, the confirmation procedure had a name. Surely a procedure for undoing something had a name too, somewhere.

---

The archivist pulled out the index for the regulation book. A thick bundle of paper. Procedure names were listed in alphabetical order.

I went through from the beginning. Decision, review, dismissal, rejection. There. I went to "withdrawal." Not there. I went to "cancellation." Not there. I went to "annulment." Not there.

I went through the entire index again, start to finish. Reception, distribution, convening, deliberation, confirmation, objection, re-review, dismissal, notice, implementation, closure. All of it was there. Only the procedure for undoing something already confirmed was missing.

Just in case, I looked up similar words too. Correction, amendment, supplement. These three existed. Hopeful, I read the correction procedure first — and reading it, this turned out to be a procedure for fixing typos in a confirmed document, or simple numerical errors. That was different from undoing the decision itself. At the very bottom of the correction clause I found this sentence: *This procedure does not alter the substantive content of the decision.* Reading that sentence, I let out a small laugh. This city had even written down, ahead of time, the words "this is not undoing."

*I felt a rush of excitement in that moment, the excitement of having discovered something. I want to set that excitement down honestly. In that moment I believed this was hidden evidence. That someone had deliberately left this procedure out. That belief collapsed completely by that afternoon.*

*I traced the index with my finger and counted it over several times more. There were eighty-one procedure names in total. Eighty of the eighty-one were things I'd already heard of, in this city, by now. Only the one — the undoing procedure — had never existed from the very start. I couldn't find any difference at all between those eighty and this one missing thing. The empty space wasn't marked or highlighted in any special way. It was simply, quietly, absent from where it should have been.*

---

I asked the archivist.

"…Is there no procedure to undo a confirmed deliberation?"

The archivist checked the index again and shook her head.

"…Not in the index, no. But not being in the index doesn't mean it's not in the regulation. The index is built around the things that get used often."

"…So do I have to go through the full text of the regulation?"

"…The original text is right here. Would you like to look at the whole confirmation chapter?"

I took that chapter and read it start to finish. The requirements for confirmation, the effect of confirmation, the post-confirmation implementation procedure. Twenty-two clauses in detail. I read all twenty-two. Not a single one addressed undoing.

*At first I thought this was some strange coincidence. It wasn't that exactly one of twenty-two clauses was missing. The concept of undoing itself did not exist anywhere within this chapter. It was as though this chapter had been written by someone who'd never imagined that such a thing could happen at all.*

---

I combed through the entire regulation again. This time not just the confirmation chapter — the general provisions, objections, retrial, closure, every chapter. I hoped to find, somewhere, a sentence like "a confirmed matter may, in special circumstances, be reviewed again."

In the last clause of the general provisions, I found this sentence.

*Matters not specified in this regulation shall not be processed without a separate procedure.*

I read that sentence several times. What isn't specified isn't processed. The undoing procedure wasn't specified, so it wasn't processed either.

"…Is this saying undoing is forbidden?"

The archivist looked the clause over with me and tilted her head.

"…It doesn't say forbidden. It just says it isn't specified."

"…Is that different?"

"…It's different. Forbidden means don't do it. Not specified means... that the procedure itself simply doesn't exist."

At first I didn't quite understand that difference.

I asked the archivist one more thing.

"…Has anyone ever asked about this before?"

She thought for a moment before answering.

"…Let me think. I've worked here six years, and I don't think there's been anything like this in that time. If I check the index request records I could know for sure, but that would take some time to dig up."

"…Could I ask you to?"

The archivist happily brought out an old request-record binder. I spent the whole afternoon going through it. I looked for anyone who had made an index request using words like "withdrawal," "cancellation," "undoing." I went through several years' worth and found no such request. Most requests were practical matters — "inquiry on confirmed implementation date," "list of relocation destination sites," things like that.

*Sitting in front of that regulation book that afternoon, I began to truly feel it might be possible that I was really the first person to ever ask a question like this. Being first sounds like it ought to be something remarkable, but in that moment I felt nothing of the sort. It was frightening, if anything. That I was now asking a question no one had ever asked — it felt like walking, alone, down some wrong road.*

---

That afternoon I went back to find the Secretary.

"…There's no procedure in the regulation for undoing a confirmed deliberation. Is this forbidden?"

"…It isn't forbidden."

"…But there's no procedure."

"…Right, there isn't."

"…If it's not forbidden, why isn't there one?"

The Secretary thought for a moment before answering.

"…To forbid something, someone first has to propose a reason to forbid it. That's how every regulation in this city gets made. Someone proposes that it's needed, it gets reviewed, and through that review it becomes regulation. The withdrawal procedure isn't missing because someone forbade it. It's missing because no one has ever proposed it, up to now."

"…Not even once?"

"…Not that I know of."

"…So does that mean no one in this city's whole history has ever thought a deliberation went wrong?"

"…That's probably not true. There have been a hundred twelve objections filed. Plenty of people thought something was wrong. It's just that none of them ever carried that thought all the way to the proposal — 'there should be a way to undo something even after confirmation.' Most stopped at the objection stage, or where that didn't work, simply accepted it."

"…Why do they accept it?"

"…Because the procedure had no flaw. In front of a flawless procedure, proposing a new procedure is a much harder thing to do than finding a flaw."

I stood a long while in front of that answer.

*I want to set down this moment as precisely as I can, in this record. The withdrawal clause's absence wasn't a conspiracy. No one had hidden it. It was only that no one had ever said it was needed, so a procedure like that had never been born. Not a forbidden door — a door never built.*

---

"…Then if I propose it now?"

"…You can propose it. But that's a procedure for making a new regulation, so it takes a long time. It would be hard for it to apply directly to this matter. The principle is that a regulation applies from the time it's made onward, to matters that come after."

"…So this matter can never be undone?"

"…There is, at present, no procedure to undo this matter."

I hung a small hope on that phrase, "at present." It sounded to me as though it meant — none at present, but perhaps someday.

"…Then is there another path I could use for this matter?"

The Secretary answered this question a little differently.

"…There's no path to undo it. But there is a path to open something new."

"…What's that?"

"…I'll tell you that another time. Today's purpose was confirming the regulation."

I found that strange. Why not tell me now, I wondered. But I didn't press further and went home for the day.

---

Leaving the reception desk, I ran into Tyche. I told her what I'd found out that day, exactly as it was.

"…There's no withdrawal procedure. Not forbidden, either — it just doesn't exist."

Tyche looked a little surprised to hear it.

"…I didn't know that either. Really, none?"

"…Right. I searched the whole regulation and found nothing."

"…That's strange. I always assumed it must exist somewhere."

I could tell Tyche really hadn't known. There wasn't the faintest trace of concealment on her face. Tyche knew this city's structure better than anyone, and yet this empty space was a fresh discovery to her too.

"…Why do you think it doesn't exist, Tyche?"

Tyche thought for a moment and answered,

"…Probably because it was never needed. I've rarely felt like a deliberation went wrong, in this city. The process has no flaws. But a flawless process doesn't always mean a good outcome — like this matter. No one ever built the procedure to fill that gap."

"…Did no one build it because they didn't know it was needed, or because they couldn't build it even knowing it was needed?"

"…I don't know. But either way, the result is the same, isn't it. That it doesn't exist, right now."

I couldn't argue with that. She was right.

I suddenly thought of the objection-status bulletin board. A hundred twelve filed, three changed. I asked Tyche about those three.

"…Those three that got changed through objection before — were those changed after confirmation too?"

Tyche thought for a moment and said she'd look it up for me. I went back to the archive that afternoon and looked up the three cases myself.

All three had happened before confirmation. Cases where, right after the district's decision came out — before the notice had even gone out — an objection had been accepted. I overlaid the dates of these three cases against the stage table in the confirmation regulation. Before confirmation, there had clearly been a door through which objections could pass. Through that door, three times, something had changed. But that door closed the moment confirmation happened. Beyond that, there was no door at all.

*I want to set down this distinction precisely. There was a door called objection. It opened three times. But that door only opened before confirmation. A door that opens after confirmation had never been built, from the start. This is an entirely different matter from a door being locked. In front of a door that doesn't exist, no amount of searching for a key does any good.*

---

On my way home I kept walking with the regulation copy in my hand. I'd read it all the way through, and the clause simply wasn't there. That day, for the first time, I understood exactly that "doesn't exist" could split into two different things. Something absent because it's forbidden, and something absent because no one has ever made it. The first was a wall. A wall you're not supposed to cross. The second wasn't a wall at all. It was simply ground no one had ever walked yet.

*And yet the two came out to exactly the same thing. Wall or unwalked ground, I was standing in front of it, equally stuck, either way. There was only one difference. A wall, I couldn't get past. Unwalked ground — I might be the one to walk it first.*

*That night I wrote this distinction out on paper several times over. Forbidden — someone decided it must not be. Absent — no one ever decided it could or couldn't. I set these two words side by side and stared at them for a long while. It occurred to me, that night, for the first time, that the scarier thing in this city might not be the first one, but the second. In front of a forbidden door, at least you can resent someone. In front of a door that doesn't exist, you have no one to resent — you just have to stand there.*

*I want to set down, too, as I put this regulation book back in the drawer, that this isn't this city's malice. I wasn't digging through these clauses that day to hate anyone. I was just looking for a way through. And I confirmed that the way wasn't there. Confirming that it wasn't there was, in its own way, a result too.*

I told my aunt about it, and she thought for a long while before saying,

"…So there was never a 'don't,' and never a 'do,' either."

"…Right. No one's ever brought it up, apparently."

"…Strange. A city this big, this old, and no one's ever once thought of it?"

"…That's what the Secretary says, anyway."

My aunt looked at me for a moment and said,

"…Hora, then maybe you'll be the first."

I couldn't say anything to that.

"…What's good about being first?"

"…I don't know. I don't even know if it's good or bad."

My aunt laughed at that. It was a slightly wistful laugh.

"…Your mother was the same. She never knew whether going all the way through with something was good or bad, and she went all the way through with it anyway."

I looked up at that. It had been a long time since my aunt had brought up my mother.

"…What did she go all the way through with?"

"…Oh, all kinds of things. I wasn't trying to bring it up right now — just seeing your face reminded me."

My aunt said no more and got up. That night I thought again of the last line in the twelve pages my mother left behind. *I only hope you'll be someone who reads to the end.* Reading to the end, and going all the way through — that night, they felt like the same words to me.

I kept thinking, until I fell asleep, about what it meant to be first. I was curious, too, about the "new path" the Secretary had mentioned. I decided to go find him again the next day.
