# Chapter 19. Tyche Explains

> _Previously_ — Hora met twelve people. All twelve were honest, and none of them felt ashamed. Each had done exactly their own share; no one had decided "let's do it this way." Hora went to find Tyche, hoping she could explain this structure.

Tyche was sitting by the window in the deliberation support office. I pulled up the chair beside her.

"…I met twelve people."

"…I know. The departments have been a little stirred up."

"…Stirred up? No one got angry, though."

"…It's not that they're stirred up out of anger. It's that they didn't realize the matter they were each involved in was this big, and they found out through you. That's the stir."

I heard that and stopped for a moment. That my going around asking questions had set something off in these people — I only realized it then, for the first time.

Tyche set aside the document she'd been looking at while I sat down. Her whole manner suggested nothing else was urgent right now.

"…Did I do something wrong?"

"…Not wrong. It's just an uncommon thing to do, in this city. Someone going around and looking for what's behind their own job."

"…Then why has no one ever done this uncommon thing?"

Tyche shrugged lightly.

"…Because everyone's busy. And because the summary is accurate. When there's an accurate summary, there's no reason to read three hundred pages."

Listening to her, I understood this wasn't meant as a criticism. Tyche really believed it. That any reasonable person would trust the summary and move on.

Tyche glanced toward the window for a moment, then looked back at me.

"…I wonder about it myself, sometimes. Why I don't do it."

"…Do what?"

"…Not reading things to the end. I was born and raised in this city, I know the summary is accurate — and even so, I could still read the whole thing sometimes. But I never have. Not once."

"…Why not?"

"…I don't know. I just never felt the need to, I guess. How did you start doing it, Hora?"

I didn't answer that question right away. I didn't want to bring up my mother here. I only said,

"…It's just a habit."

Tyche didn't press further on that answer. Not pressing was this city's manners, and that day I was grateful for those manners too.

---

"…Tyche, can you explain why this turned out this way?"

Tyche looked at me straight on. Her face wasn't angry, wasn't bothered. If anything, it looked as though she'd been waiting.

"…I can explain it. But it won't be the answer you want."

"…I still want to hear it."

Tyche pulled out a sheet of paper and began to draw. One small circle at the top, several circles below that, more circles below that. At the very bottom, the circles multiplied into dozens. A triangle shape.

"…This triangle."

Tyche tapped the top circle with the tip of her pen.

"…This is the decision. This many people are involved to reach one decision. But the top circle actually isn't a circle. It's an empty seat. No one is sitting there."

Looking at that drawing, I realized this was the same shape I'd been seeing all along in the signature box.

"…This is this city's deliberation structure. There's a decision at the top, and it passes through several stages to get there. Each stage has its person, and each person handles only their own stage."

"…I know that already. I heard it from all twelve."

"…But you don't know why it was designed this way."

I nodded.

"…Where did you learn this triangle?"

"…During training. Everyone who becomes a deliberation assistant learns this. It's the very first thing taught, on the first day — this exact drawing."

"…Doesn't anyone find it strange when they see it? That the top is empty?"

Tyche thought a moment and shook her head.

"…When I first saw it, I actually felt reassured. If someone were sitting at the top, it would all come down to that person's state of mind. If it's empty, it's not resting on anyone at all."

Hearing that, I realized Tyche and I had felt completely different things looking at the exact same drawing. I looked at that empty seat and grew uneasy. Tyche looked at that same empty seat and felt reassured.

---

"…What would happen if one person decided everything, start to finish?" Tyche asked.

"…It would become that person's responsibility."

"…Right. But what if that person's judgment is wrong?"

"…Then it becomes that person's fault."

"…So that person would feel burdened and try to avoid making judgments. Or, the opposite — they might try to force their own judgment through no matter what. Neither is good."

I couldn't argue with that. It was true. The idea that things go wrong when one person holds everything was common sense I'd grown up hearing in this city since I was small. I'd heard it many times in school too.

"…So this city made it so that no one person decides. The decision is broken into small pieces and spread across many people. Each person only needs to do their own share, exactly. No one has to carry the weight of judgment."

"…But breaking it up that way means no one can see the whole."

"…Right. That's the cost of this method."

---

"…But that cost is too high. Four hundred ten people are going to a place that doesn't exist — can that be dismissed as a cost?"

Tyche set down the paper and looked at me.

"…Hora, what do you think it would've been like if it hadn't been done this way?"

I couldn't answer.

"…If one person had judged it, that person might have made a mistake, or been biased. It might have split into who gets measured and who does the measuring. Do you know why this city ended up this way in the first place? Because one person's judgment dividing people up turned out wrong far too often."

"…So they made it so no one judges at all?"

"…Yes. And that really did work. Has there ever been a decision in this city made because someone hated someone else, Hora? Have you ever seen that?"

I could only say I hadn't. There really hadn't been.

---

"…So what about this matter, then? The outcome was bad."

"…A bad outcome and an unfair process are different things. The process was fair. Everyone was reviewed by the same standard, everyone went through the same procedure. The outcome coming out badly isn't the fault of this procedure — the situation itself was simply bad."

"…What do you mean, the situation was bad?"

"…Unregistered residences weren't supposed to exist in the first place. They accumulated, and this time they had to be processed all at once, and among them some of the destination sites still aren't confirmed. This isn't a problem with the procedure — it's a problem with reality."

*Listening to this answer, I felt that Tyche really was speaking with complete honesty. She wasn't dressing up her words to persuade me. She was just saying what she saw.*

"…Then why did that problem with reality arise? Why did three whole destination sites go unconfirmed?"

"…Because the department in charge hasn't been staffed yet."

"…Why hasn't the department been staffed?"

"…That's a personnel placement issue. As this matter suddenly grew in scale, the manpower needed grew too, and manpower placement is only possible with next quarter's budget."

"…Then couldn't they have just waited for next quarter? Why was the implementation date set before that?"

Tyche paused on this question for a moment.

"…That... I don't know exactly either. Scheduling is set by a different department."

"…Another different department?"

"…Yes."

I listened to that answer and counted silently, in my head. Site confirmation, personnel department's business. Personnel placement, budget department's business. Implementation schedule, yet another department's business. Every time I asked about one problem, a different department came up.

*This was the first moment I realized it wasn't that Tyche's explanation was wrong — it was that it was too accurate, and that accuracy was what frustrated me. Tyche could name the exact department for every single question. And yet no matter how far I traced those departments, no one ever appeared at the end who said, "I decided this." The more complete Tyche's explanation became, the more helpless I felt.*

---

"…But Tyche, some of those twelve felt something was strange. The sixth person did. And someone on the committee raised an objection too."

"…Yes, I know that. It's in the minutes."

"…But that strangeness didn't get reflected anywhere."

"…It was reflected. It was raised as an objection, and it was processed through a vote. That's what reflection is."

"…If you lose the vote, that's the same as not being reflected."

"…No. It was reflected. The result simply defeated the strangeness. That, too, is procedure."

I couldn't find words for a long while after that. Tyche's logic had no gaps. Wherever I poked at it, she already had an answer waiting there.

"…So if I file a new objection now, would it come out the same?"

"…Probably. As long as the situation doesn't change. If items 3, 7, and 12 get confirmed, it might turn out different. But since they're not confirmed yet, filing another objection now would probably come out the same."

"…Then what can I do?"

Tyche didn't answer this question right away. She looked at me for a moment and said,

"…I don't really know that either. I can explain this structure, but what can be done outside of it — that's not my territory."

*I realized only later that this answer was the first time Tyche had ever said "I don't know." Tyche knew everything there was to know about the structure. It was only outside the structure that she truly didn't know. Or — maybe "outside the structure" was a concept that didn't even exist for Tyche at all.*

---

"…So are you saying this structure is perfect?"

"…I'm not saying it's perfect. No structure is perfect. I'm just saying this structure wrongs people less than other structures would."

"…Wrongs them less? What about those four hundred ten?"

"…Those four hundred ten are having a hard time because the outcome was bad — not because anyone in the process hated them or discriminated against them. It's a fact that they weren't registered, and that fact applied to everyone equally. It was bad luck. Not being wronged."

Something caught at me in that.

"…Why is bad luck different from being wronged?"

Tyche looked at me for a moment, then said, very gently,

"…Being wronged is what you call it when someone does something unjust to you. But this time, no one did anything unjust to those people. They simply happened to be in that position. The way you ended up sitting on this committee, Hora — that was the same thing. A vacancy, a number. That was luck too."

---

I heard that comparison and couldn't say anything. It was true. I too had ended up in this seat through a vacancy and a number. Those four hundred ten were in their position through registration and a number too. The structure was identical.

"…But I ended up in a good seat, and those people ended up in a bad one. Same luck, different result."

"…Yes, the results are different. But that's simply the nature of luck. Some end up on the good side, some on the bad. It looks unfair, but in the sense that no one intended it, it's fair."

"…Is it fair just because there was no intent?"

"…At least no one did this because they hated someone. I think that matters. If there's no hatred, then at least there's no unfairness that came from hatred."

I heard that and thought for a long while. Tyche wasn't entirely wrong. This city really had no hatred in it. I'd known that fact from the start, and I'd always thought it was one of the good things about it.

"…But even without hatred, bad things can still happen. That's what you're saying."

"…Right. That's exactly what happened here. Bad results can come about even without hatred. I don't deny that."

*In this answer, I felt for the first time, with certainty, that Tyche wasn't trying to persuade me. Tyche wasn't defending this structure. She was simply someone who saw this structure accurately. Defending it and describing it accurately were different things, and I had kept confusing the two.*

"…So nobody gets wronged."

Tyche said that last.

"…That isn't a bad thing."

*I wanted to argue with that. I really did. But that night, and for several days after, I couldn't find the words to argue back. Tyche hadn't said it to win against me. She really believed it, and there weren't many holes in that belief.*

*Leaving Tyche's room that day, I had a thought for the first time — that maybe I was the one who was wrong. That this structure might really be the best there is. That thought was what troubled me most.*

Tyche added one more thing as I was about to get up.

"…Hora, I don't think what you're doing is meaningless. Meeting twelve people was probably a good thing. At the very least, those people are thinking more now about what they did."

"…But the outcome doesn't change."

"…The outcome not changing doesn't mean it's meaningless."

I looked up at that.

"…What do you mean by that?"

"…I don't know. It just feels that way to me."

*This last sentence stayed with me oddly, for a long time. Up until then, Tyche had been someone explaining everything through logic. But in this last sentence she said, "I don't know, it just feels that way." That there was a place logic couldn't reach, even for Tyche — I saw that for the first time then.*

I left the deliberation support office and walked the corridor. The window let in no view of outside, but that day I found myself unusually curious about what lay beyond it.

That night I told my aunt about Tyche. She listened for a long while and said,

"…It sounds like what she said might be right, or it might not be."

"…Same here."

"…But the fact that you couldn't argue back — that's the stranger part, to me."

I couldn't answer that. That I hadn't been able to argue back was what stayed with me longest that night.
