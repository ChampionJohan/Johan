# Chapter 18. Twelve More

> _Previously_ — Hora met the initial receiving officer. "I only passed it on." An honest statement. No shame in it either. After that, Hora decided to try meeting a few more of the four hundred thirty-two on the list.

Over the two weeks that followed, I met twelve more people. I kept up my other deliberation work too, crossing names off the list whenever I found a spare moment.

*I could have written each of these twelve out in detail, one at a time. But then this book would start to read like a roster. Instead I'm gathering here what I heard in common from these twelve. Each was a different person, a different office, a different voice. And yet what they said was strikingly similar.*

*I went to see one or two a day. Some days I went to the wrong department entirely and wasted half a day. One person happened to be on leave and I had to go back. Finding people in this city was never a hassle, but it certainly took time.*

---

The second person was the surveying officer at the regional management office. Her job was to physically measure unregistered residences. She held a tape measure and a small ledger in her hands.

"…All I do is measure. Whether a place is registered, how wide it is. I measure that and send the list to the listing department."

"…When you're measuring, you must see people living there."

"…I see them. But what I measure is the land, not the people."

"…Isn't it strange to measure only the land when you can see people living there?"

"…At first it felt strange. But that's my job. How many houses, how many people living there — those go in the column beside it, as numbers only. There's no column for faces."

I heard that and glanced at her ledger. There really were only number columns. No name column, no face column. Looking at that ledger, I thought, for a moment, that every piece of paper in this city eventually resembled that ledger.

The third person was a format reviewer.

"…I only check whether the list follows the prescribed form. Whether every field is filled in, whether the numbers run in order."

"…You don't look at the content?"

"…I do look at the content, but not to judge it — to confirm it matches the form."

"…So it passes as long as the form is right?"

"…Yes. Not a single list that's come to my department in the last three years has been rejected for wrong formatting. The regional management office is that meticulous about it."

*Hearing that answer, I understood that this review stage was, in effect, purely formal. Formal didn't mean bad. It just meant nothing was actually filtered at this stage. Nothing being filtered didn't mean this person was lazy. There was simply nothing there to filter.*

---

The fourth, fifth, and sixth people were all from higher departments. Their offices were spread across three different floors, and I had to be shown the way freshly each time. All three said much the same thing. "We just consolidate what comes up from below." "We don't create anything new." "We're the seat where the confirmation stamp gets put."

The fourth had been doing consolidation work for over ten years. "…This is my first time seeing a matter this scale. But the way I consolidate doesn't change. Growing in size doesn't change what I do." The fifth was younger, and pointed at the regulation binder tacked up at his seat. "…It's all right here. I don't do anything outside of this."

To the sixth I asked something a little different.

"…Before you put the confirmation stamp, did you ever notice anything that felt strange?"

"…Honestly, yes. The three temporary-designation sites."

I felt a flicker of hope. Something's finally caught, I thought.

"…And?"

"…But the temporary designation itself is a procedure that's in the regulation. It's explicitly stated that something can be temporarily designated before final confirmation. So it felt strange, but it wasn't a violation of the regulation."

"…Couldn't you have stopped it, when it felt strange?"

"…That's outside my authority. I only have the authority to confirm whether something matches the regulation."

"…So when you felt it was strange, who did you say that to?"

"…I didn't tell anyone separately. Strange and a regulation violation are different things. If I raised an objection every time something felt strange to me, our department wouldn't get anything done."

*Listening to this answer, I saw clearly, for the first time, the distance between the feeling of "this is strange" and the fact of "I have authority to stop this." There was a person here who had felt it strange. But that feeling wasn't this person's job.*

*I know this answer might sound cold. But it didn't sound cold to me in that room, hearing it. If anything, it was the voice of someone who knew exactly where they stood. If this person had tried to handle something outside her own authority, in this city that would itself have been an overreach. I nodded along with her words even as some part of me stayed uneasy.*

---

After meeting these six, I sat on a bench for a while to rest. Not because my legs hurt — because my head did. Walking home that evening, for the first time I briefly thought about giving up on all this. It wasn't that it was too hard. It was that I couldn't see an answer anywhere.

*I didn't hold onto that thought for long. If I gave up, the four hundred ten would simply go to a place that didn't exist. If I didn't give up, the outcome would probably be the same. But between giving up and not giving up, there seemed to be a difference — at least to me. What that difference was, I couldn't have explained then.*

The seventh person was the support-fund calculation officer. Her desk was covered in a calculator and sheets of formulas.

"…The math is accurate. I double-check it myself, too."

"…Is there actually somewhere those support funds can be used?"

"…That's the destination-site management department's responsibility."

"…Even if it's not your responsibility, weren't you curious?"

"…I was curious. But when I asked, I didn't get an answer. They just said it's still not finalized on their end either."

I understood that this person had already asked the exact same question I was asking. She'd simply asked before me, and been stopped before me too.

The eighth person belonged to the destination-site management department, but as it happened wasn't actually assigned to items 3, 7, and 12.

"…Those three aren't my responsibility. I only manage confirmed sites."

"…Then who's responsible for those three?"

"…Probably no one, yet. Since they're not confirmed."

"…So until someone's assigned, those three just stay as they are?"

"…Probably. Once someone's assigned, someone will move on it."

"…Who assigns that?"

"…I don't know that either. Personnel placement's a different department's business."

*In this answer I saw a strange hole for the first time. Because it wasn't confirmed, there was a place with no one assigned yet. With no one assigned, no one was looking after it, and with no one looking after it, it stayed unconfirmed. This too was a loop. The same shape as the loop that produced unregistered residences in the first place. Only this time it wasn't land circling inside the loop — it was responsibility.*

---

The ninth and tenth people handled convening for the committee. They set meeting dates and distributed materials.

"…I really only convene. I don't get involved with the substance of the agenda."

"…Do you know when you convene whether an agenda is big or small?"

"…I can see the scale. But it doesn't affect my work. The convening procedure is the same no matter the scale of the matter."

The eleventh person was the record keeper. She wrote the minutes.

"…I write down exactly what's said. I don't add my own opinion."

"…Reading the minutes, no one seems to say their own opinion."

"…Right, everyone just talks about passing it on and putting it in order, so I have no choice but to write exactly that."

"…Has anyone ever raised a different opinion?"

"…Sometimes. But that's written into the minutes exactly as it happened too. 'One member raised the need for a re-review.' And then it moves straight to a vote."

"…What was the vote result?"

"…This matter had a re-review proposal raised, but the original stood after the vote."

I later found and read that minutes record myself. Viewing was never blocked, as always. It really was written just as she'd said. Someone had raised an objection. Who that person was didn't appear in the minutes either. It only said "one member."

*I became curious, at this point, for the first time. Who was that "one member"? It meant that someone besides me had felt something was strange. But that person, too, had lost the vote in the end, and hadn't even left a name behind. I wanted to find this person, but I had no way to search for a name that wasn't in the minutes.*

---

The twelfth person was the one who had first brought this entire matter forward. I thought this person, at least, might be different. He was the one who'd raised the agenda in the first place.

"…All I did was confirm that the cumulative count had crossed the threshold, and bring it forward. Once it crosses the threshold, bringing it forward is my job."

"…Who set the threshold?"

"…It was set a long time ago. I only apply it."

"…Have you never thought about whether that threshold is appropriate?"

"…I've thought about it. But that's not something I can change either. Amending the threshold is a different procedure."

*I'd hoped this person would be my last chance. He was the one who'd brought it forward, so surely he'd know something. But he too had only confirmed that the threshold had been crossed. Whoever had made the threshold itself was somewhere else again.*

I asked him one last thing.

"…So who is the person who actually decided, in this matter, 'let's do it this way'?"

He thought for a long while. It was a genuinely thoughtful expression. He wasn't trying to give a careless answer.

"…I don't know. I brought it forward, the committee deliberated, and the Consensus decided. Inside all of that, the one person who decided 'let's do it this way'... doesn't seem to exist."

"…No one?"

"…No. Not that I know of."

*I wrote that answer down on paper exactly as he said it. Doesn't seem to exist. None of the twelve had said it quite this precisely, but every one of them had, in their own way, been saying this same thing. No one decided. And yet a decision exists. I thought I'd already known this sentence from the start — that day was the first time I truly felt its weight.*

---

By the time I'd met all twelve, two weeks had somehow gone by. To all twelve I'd asked the same question. "Do you think this is wrong?"

All twelve answered similarly. "The part I saw wasn't wrong." "I don't know about the whole." "It's outside my authority."

None of them got angry. None of them found me a bother. None of them lied. And none of them felt ashamed. A few of them even offered me coffee or tea.

*Not being ashamed might sound strange to hear. At first I thought it was strange too. But after meeting a few of them, not being ashamed came to seem natural. There was no one among them who had done anything to be ashamed of. Each had done their own share, honestly, exactly.*

*Meeting these twelve, I let go, one by one, of the expectations I'd first carried. At first I went in thinking, "maybe this person is the culprit." By around the third person I'd given up on that hope. From about the sixth on, I started to feel, if anything, sorry for these people. Everyone was diligently doing their own part in their own seat. That this diligence, all gathered together, had become the decision that sent four hundred ten people to a place that didn't exist — that was the part I found hardest to bear.*

*A few of the twelve, when we parted, asked me back. "Do you think this could change?" Every time, I answered that I didn't know. Because I really didn't.*

---

On the way home I finished marking the list. All twelve lines, *confirmed.* Beside each I wrote the same words every time. *No wrongdoing. Doesn't know the whole.*

I asked my aunt.

"…I met all twelve. Not one of them did anything wrong."

"…Isn't that a good thing, then?"

"…But the result stays the same. Four hundred ten people are still going to a place that doesn't exist."

My aunt was quiet for a moment and said,

"…That's the strangest part of all. No one did anything wrong, and the result is still bad."

"…Yeah. That's the strangest part."

*That night, for the first time, I wondered whether I was looking for the wrong people. It seemed like meeting all four hundred thirty-two would probably get me the same answer. I decided to go find Tyche the next day. I thought Tyche might be able to explain this structure to me.*

My aunt asked one last thing, clearing the rice bowls.

"…Hora. Going around meeting all these people — is it good for them, or bad for them?"

I couldn't answer that question for a long moment.

"…I don't know. A few of them seemed uncomfortable, if anything."

"…So will you stop?"

"…No. I'm going to keep going."

My aunt didn't ask any further. That night I ran through the twelve faces one by one in my mind. The surveying officer, the format reviewer, the person who'd put on the confirmation stamp, the one who calculated the support funds, the convening officer, the record keeper, and the one who'd first brought the matter forward. Not one of them had a bad face. That was what held onto me longest of all.

*The next day I told Tyche all of this exactly as it happened. She heard me out to the end and then began explaining, very calmly. That explanation would become the most difficult scene in this whole book to deal with. I still haven't managed, to this day, to fully rebut it.*
