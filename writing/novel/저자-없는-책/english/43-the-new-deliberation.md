# Chapter 43. The New Deliberation

> _Previously_ — Review went on with no fixed rule. Six days, ten days,
> six days again. Tyche asked, "And you'll keep going anyway?" Hora
> answered, "Yeah," and Tyche asked no further.

About two weeks after the eighth review, a notice came.

This one was a different kind. Not a request to appear, but a notice of
outcome. *Seventh Classification reexamination matter, deliberation
result confirmed.* I looked at that sentence and for a long while
couldn't open the envelope.

*I had imagined this moment many times. In my imagination, the hand
opening the envelope trembled. In reality it didn't tremble. I simply
opened it. That my hand didn't tremble felt a little strange to me, in
the moment — but thinking about it now, it was only natural. I'd
already been holding on to this one document alone for two hundred-some
days. I'd grown as used to it as a person could.*

---

The result document ran forty-two pages, with eighteen more pages of
supporting materials attached. I didn't start with the summary. I read
it from beginning to end.

The front section explained the reexamination procedure. It set out how
the new deliberation I'd proposed had been convened, what materials had
been reexamined, and how many people had taken part. This time it
wasn't four hundred, but six hundred and twelve. More people had been
involved in the reexamination than in the original deliberation.

*I paused for a moment at this number. I couldn't tell, at the time,
whether more people having looked at it was a good sign or a bad one.
I'm still not certain, even now.*

The result appeared on the twelfth page.

*Regarding the above reexamination matter, the following is hereby
confirmed. Among the list of relocation-target sites, the unregistered
residences falling under items 3, 7, and 12 are converted to
formally registered sites, the grounds for their provisional
designation having lapsed. Residents of these residences are removed
from the Seventh Classification, and released from relocation-target
status.*

I read that sentence three times.

---

Even read three times, the sentence stayed the same. Items 3, 7, and
12.

I looked further back to find the number of people covered under those
three items. There was a table on the sixth page of the supporting
materials. Ninety-one residents under item 3, sixty-eight under item 7,
fifty-four under item 12. Added together, two hundred and thirteen.

*I sat with this number for a long time. It wasn't a small number. I
knew these people would now go on living without hearing the words
unregistered residence attached to their names. That much was real. It
wasn't false.*

I turned the page.

*Excepting the above items 3, 7, and 12, the remainder of the list
retains the original decision. No grounds for reexamination were
confirmed.*

---

Two hundred and thirteen out of one thousand one hundred and forty.

The remaining nine hundred and twenty-seven stayed as they were. The
document didn't write anything new about those nine hundred and
twenty-seven. It only said "the original decision is retained." There
was nothing after that one line. It didn't re-explain the grounds, and
it didn't apologize. It meant there was no reason to make a new
decision about something already decided.

*I sat for a long time that day with those two numbers side by side.
Two hundred and thirteen, and nine hundred and twenty-seven. I knew I
was supposed to feel glad, looking at the side of two hundred and
thirteen. And I really was, a little glad. But I couldn't help looking
at the side of nine hundred and twenty-seven too. That number just sat
there. It hadn't been erased.*

Reading this result for the first time, I think I'd expected something
dramatic. Someone who'd done wrong being exposed, that person being
punished, and not just two hundred and thirteen but all one thousand
one hundred and forty coming back. There was no such scene.

*I should be honest here. I thought of the old stories I'd heard as a
child. Stories where someone who drove an innocent person to ruin ends
up hanged on the very gallows they built. I did, a little, expect
something like that. I hoped some such name would appear, somewhere in
this result document. Who had first designated item 3 as provisional,
who had decided to leave the rest as they were. No such name ever
appeared, to the very end. There had never been a place for one to
appear. This document had no one who had driven anyone to ruin, so
there could be no scene of that person being punished, either.*

*I thought again that this was this city's most honest face. Without a
villain, there is no scene of a villain falling. That fact held me for
a long time.*

---

After closing the result document, I walked straight to the area under
item 3. It was a place I'd never been. Low ground, not far from the
water channel. There were a few houses, and laundry hung out, no
different from any other day. I think I'd expected something to have
changed there. A flag hung up, or people gathered and celebrating.

There was nothing. One person was splitting firewood in a yard, and
gave me only a light nod when they saw me. I had no way of knowing
whether that person knew that their name had just been removed from
the unregistered list. I wanted to ask, but couldn't. It felt like a
question I had no right to ask.

*Walking past that yard, I thought about when and how this news would
reach each of the two hundred and thirteen people. It would come as a
notice. On the same kind of paper as any other notice, in the same
kind of print. Maybe those people, too, would receive it and be unable
to open the envelope for a long while, the way I had been. Or maybe
they'd just open it, read it, and be done. Either way, I wouldn't see
that moment. I would stop here, and each of them would receive that
paper in their own place, on their own.*

---

The Secretary called me in that afternoon.

"…You'll have seen the result."

"…Yes."

"…This reexamination proceeded according to procedure, and has been
confirmed. If you have an objection, you may raise it again the same
way."

"…What about the remaining nine hundred and twenty-seven?"

"…No grounds for reexamination were confirmed for them. No new
evidence came forward."

"…Why didn't any evidence come forward? They're the same kind of
people."

The Secretary looked at his papers for a moment.

"…There was evidence that items 3, 7, and 12 had been provisional
designations. The other items were formal designations from the start.
That's a different matter."

*Hearing this answer, I couldn't find anything to argue back with. The
Secretary hadn't lied to me. He'd told me exactly and fully why those
three items alone had been resolved. That was this city's way. It
doesn't hide anything. And yet I still felt some kind of wronged
feeling. I couldn't put my finger on the shape of that feeling, that
day.*

---

"…So what more can I do now?"

"…You can open another new deliberation for the remaining matter. If
you find new grounds."

"…What more grounds would be needed?"

"…I don't know that either. That's for the reexamination committee to
judge."

Hearing that, I remembered an earlier moment, long before. The
Secretary had answered the same way, then. When I'd asked what happens
if you write your name, he had looked away for the first time. This
time he didn't look away. He was simply saying as much as he knew.

"…What do you think of this result, Secretary?"

The Secretary paused a little at this question.

"…That's not mine to think about. I only convene and pass things on."

"…Even so. Just as a person."

The Secretary looked at me for a moment. There was something in that
look, but I couldn't read what it was, that day.

"…Two hundred and thirteen is a good thing. That much I do think."

"…And the rest?"

"…The rest — it was put in order that way."

I felt a brief catch in that sentence. Very short. I didn't miss that
catch, but I didn't press further, that day. The Secretary moved on to
the next document, and I asked nothing more.

---

Tyche came that evening. Her face said she'd already seen the result.

"…Two hundred and thirteen."

"…Yeah."

"…That's not a small number. I honestly thought it might not happen at
all."

I was surprised by that.

"…You thought it wouldn't happen?"

"…Yeah. Looking at the statistics — cases where a reexamination
overturns even part of the original decision — even across ten years
of records, there are hardly any. And usually only when a procedural
flaw is obvious. This case wasn't that obvious."

"…But it happened."

"…It did. I still can't fully explain why. I can say it happened
because there happened to be grounds about the provisional
designation, but I don't know if that's all there was to it."

*Hearing this, I looked at Tyche again. She, too, was admitting there
was a part she couldn't fully explain. But that admission didn't lead
her straight to a different conclusion. It was the same as before.
Tyche left the hole a hole.*

---

"…And the rest? What do you think about that, Tyche?"

"…That just stays as it is. Because no grounds came forward. I don't
think that's a bad thing. Overturning something without grounds
wouldn't be fair either."

"…But they're people. Nine hundred and twenty-seven of them."

"…That's right. That stays as it is. I don't feel nothing about that
either."

Tyche's expression didn't change as she said this. She was someone who
could say something was sad without wearing a sad face. I knew this
wasn't hypocrisy. Tyche really did carry sadness in exactly that way.

"…I don't know whether I should call this winning or losing."

"…I don't think it's either."

"…Then what is it?"

Tyche thought for a moment.

"…Just that two hundred and thirteen people changed. I don't think
there's anything more than that."

---

Tyche started to get up, then sat back down.

"…But Hora, I did learn one thing from this."

"…What?"

"…I've told you almost everything can be explained by a vacancy, a
number, and probability. I still think that. But looking purely at
probability, this reexamination was really unlikely to happen. The
procedural flaw wasn't obvious, and there was no guarantee that more
people being involved would flip the result."

"…But it did flip."

"…Yeah. So I file this away as a low probability that happened to be
realized. Low isn't zero, after all. But… "

Tyche paused for a moment.

"…But what?"

"…Every time a low probability gets realized, calling it just
probability feels strange sometimes. I know it's strange of me to say
that. But if I don't file it that way, I fall apart. So I file it that
way."

*I sat with this for a long time. Tyche wasn't saying her own logic was
wavering. She was saying that the reason she held onto that logic
wasn't the certainty of the logic itself, but the fact that she'd fall
apart if she didn't. I think that was the most honest thing Tyche ever
gave me.*

---

That night I told my aunt the result. She listened quietly, then asked.

"…So is it good, or not good?"

"…I think it's both."

"…What does that mean."

"…Two hundred and thirteen came through. Nine hundred and twenty-seven
didn't."

My aunt said nothing for a long while, then spoke.

"…So that's the result, then."

"…Yeah."

"…It doesn't feel satisfying."

"…No, it doesn't."

*I want to set this exchange down exactly. My aunt didn't try to
comfort me. I didn't try to reassure her either. We simply set those
two numbers side by side and left it there.*

---

That night I took the result document out again and looked all the way
to the last page. There, at the bottom, was a signature box. It was
empty. Empty again, this time too. In the end, no one at all had
confirmed the reexamination, either.

I didn't copy out the list of the nine hundred and twenty-seven names
separately. I wasn't sure I had the right to copy them out. Instead I
wrote that number at the top of the paper in the drawer. *927.* I meant
not to erase it.

*Writing this chapter, I keep asking myself again. Was this a victory.
I don't think so. If it were a victory, something should have ended.
This hasn't ended. Two hundred and thirteen came back, and nine hundred
and twenty-seven stayed as they were. I won't erase that remaining
number from this record. The moment I erased it, I felt this book
would end up doing the same thing this city has always done.*

---

The next morning, on my way to work, I passed the water channel again.
The water was flowing as usual. I stopped in front of it for a moment
and tried to put yesterday's events in order in my mind. It didn't
come together well.

Two hundred and thirteen people no longer hear the words unregistered.
Nine hundred and twenty-seven still do. I hadn't drawn that line. I had
only opened a new deliberation, and how far that deliberation would
reach hadn't been mine to decide. The three items — 3, 7, and 12 —
had existed first, and the people caught under those items had existed
first. I had only opened a door; who would walk out through that door
wasn't something I got to choose.

*Thinking this, I remembered again what Temel had once said. That there
are still things you can do, even in a seat you got only because of a
number. I did what could be done from that seat. That the work only
reached two hundred and thirteen people was part of that work too.*

I carried this thought with me through the whole day's work. I handled
the other deliberations assigned to me. I put things in order, passed
them on, confirmed them. Like every other document in this city, those
documents too had signature boxes, and they too were empty. Looking at
those boxes, I wondered whether a day would come when someone filled
them too. That day had not yet come.

At lunchtime, Tyche asked again.

"…So what will you do now? About the remaining nine hundred and
twenty-seven."

"…I don't know. I'll have to keep looking for grounds."

"…And if there aren't any?"

"…I'll still look. And if there aren't any, then there aren't any."

Tyche looked at me for a moment, then nodded. She didn't try to stop
me, and she didn't push me on either. Her face simply accepted that
answer as it was.

*That evening I opened the drawer and looked again at the paper marked
927. The number I'd written that I wouldn't erase. I left that paper
where it was, at the top of the drawer. I didn't want to put an
unfinished number away as if it were finished.*
