# Chapter 10. Numbers and Categories

> _Previously_ — Hora learning that, on average, four hundred people are
> involved in a single case. Long ago, she was told, about ten people
> decided things, until one acquaintance tipped an outcome, and the number
> grew to what it is now. Her aunt asked whether a good person's share
> gets diluted along with it too, but that day Hora didn't hold onto the
> question for long.

I first found the way deliberation subjects were referred to strange not because it was strange, but because it wasn't strange at all.

That day I was reading a ration-adjustment case. A matter of deciding who got a little more and who got a little less. Nowhere in the full text was there a person's name. Only notations like "Third Classification, 217" or "Fifth Classification, 40." I assumed that was simply how it had always been and read on without much thought. About five pages in, it suddenly occurred to me to wonder why it was set up this way.

---

I asked the person next to me.

"…Why don't these people have names?"

"…They never do. It's only issued by classification and number."

"…Why?"

They answered more seriously than I'd expected.

"…If there's a name, other things keep creeping in. Oh, that's that family's kid, oh, that person had that thing happen before, that kind of thing. With only a number, none of that creeps in at all."

I nodded, hearing that. It sounded right. I kept turning through the document for a while even after that answer. Table after table of nothing but numbers and classifications went by, and strangely, my reading speed never slowed. Having no person's name made it possible, if anything, to read more calmly.

---

I asked Tyche again that afternoon.

"…Why do deliberation subjects only ever come out as numbers and classifications?"

Tyche paused mid-sort of some material.

"…Do you know the history behind this, Hora?"

"…No."

"…Up until about thirty years ago, apparently, names went out plainly. But looking at the records from back then, there were quite a few cases where two people with the exact same conditions but different names got different results."

"…Different how?"

"…For instance, people whose names carried a certain district weren't likely to get their objections accepted. That district had had some trouble in the past, apparently, and people quietly looked down on it just from seeing the name. Even when the person had nothing to do with it at all."

Hearing that, my heart grew heavy.

"…How many classifications are there?"

"…Nine now. There used to be twelve, but it dropped after some got merged."

"…What's the basis for dividing them?"

"…The reason for the application and the conditions. Ration-related goes into the ration classification, housing-related into the housing classification. Within that, it splits further by number."

I found that system striking for being simpler than I'd expected. That there were only nine boxes to sort people into was, if anything, a relief. I'd have thought more boxes would mean more room for people to be seen differently — but nine seemed to leave little room for that.

"…Is that why names were taken out?"

"…Yes. Once they started using only numbers and classifications instead of names, that kind of gap nearly vanished. From the statistics I learned, the district-by-district variance in how often objections were accepted was cut in half."

---

I told my aunt about this that evening.

"…Auntie, apparently names used to go out plainly, back when deliberations happened. But people from some district ended up worse off because of it."

My aunt stopped what she was doing and looked at me. Her face seemed more serious than usual.

"…I remember that. That's from when I was young."

"…You went through it, Auntie?"

"…Not went through it, exactly, but that's the kind of talk that went around back then. If a name that lived in a certain place got printed plain on a document, everyone somehow looked at it a bit more harshly. Thinking about it now, I can't say for sure whether that was really true, but it's true that people believed it."

"…So that's why it changed to numbers?"

"…Yes. After it changed, that kind of talk went completely quiet. I think that was a genuinely good change."

Listening to that, I felt what I'd learned overlap with what my aunt had actually lived through. Something I'd only seen as statistics was filled in by my aunt's memory.

---

A few days later I looked up the records from before and after that revision myself, in the reading room. I laid a few pre-revision deliberation documents side by side with a few post-revision documents of the same type and read them.

The pre-revision documents had the applicant's name and home district written from the very first line. Reading through one of them, I saw the phrase "given the nature of that district" slipped, almost casually, into the objection review comments. After that, the objection had been rejected with no further grounds given.

*I stopped for a long while, looking at that sentence. "Given the nature of that district" wasn't grounds. And yet at the time, it had passed for grounds. I thought this was frightening. It hadn't been written by a bad person. It had simply been written by someone who thought the way people thought, back in an era when everyone thought that way.*

I found a few more documents from applicants out of that same district and laid them side by side to read. Their conditions were all different, but strangely, their grounds for rejection were similar. "Difficult to accept given the circumstances." "Rejected upon comprehensive judgment." There were almost no numbers. The calculation-grounds table that never failed to appear in documents from other districts was missing from about half of this district's documents. I wanted to believe that was chance, but past five cases, it was too uniform to call chance.

I copied those five cases into my notebook. Not to reach any particular conclusion. It just caught my eye, so I wrote it down. I didn't think, at the time, that this notebook would ever be used in any way later on.

The post-revision documents didn't have a single line like that. Every rejection and acceptance came with nothing but numbers and grounds attached. Things like "This application's standard-fulfillment rate is 72 percent, falling short of the threshold for acceptance." No district, no name, no hint of what kind of person this was. I found that dryness, if anything, a relief.

I also looked separately at records from the first few years right after the revision. There had been pushback in the early days, it noted. Several objections had come in arguing that being called only by number felt like being treated as an object. The Consensus's responses to those objections were on record too, and they all carried the same substance: "This measure is not intended to harm the applicant's dignity, and is instead meant to prevent disadvantage arising from factors outside the stated conditions." Reading that response, I could understand the hearts of the people who'd pushed back, and I could understand the intent behind the Consensus's answer too. Neither side had been wrong.

The objections from a few more years on had an entirely different character. Objections to being called by number at all had nearly disappeared, and what remained were only objections that a number had been assigned incorrectly. I found that shift striking. People seemed to have stopped resenting the number itself, and started caring only about the number being used correctly.

---

Within that same week, I asked the Secretary the same thing too. I'd gone to the counter to hand in paperwork, and there happened to be a quiet moment when I could ask.

"…I hear names used to go out plainly on deliberations."

The Secretary paused mid-sort of a folder and looked at me.

"…That's right. When I first started this work, I saw paperwork with names on it too."

"…Is it very different, from then to now?"

"…Very different. Back then, if I was flipping through paperwork and an unfamiliar name — a name I knew — came up, my hand would stop without my meaning it to. That doesn't happen now. A number gives no reason to stop."

I was a little surprised by that. That the Secretary, too, had had moments like that felt striking, all over again.

"…What changes, when your hand stops?"

"…It changes. The longer you stop, the longer you end up looking at that paperwork, and the longer you look, the more you want to add something to it. Even without bad intent. That's giving this one piece of paperwork something the others don't get. I think that's the most dangerous thing there is."

I nodded at that explanation. Only then did I feel, a little more in my bones, why this city had gone to such lengths to erase names.

"…So is it easier for you now?"

The Secretary thought for a moment before answering.

"…It's easier. There's nothing to stop for."

Hearing that answer, I sensed that the word "easier," coming out of this person's mouth, carried some other weight. I didn't know, at the time, exactly what it was.

"…Do you remember that paperwork from back then too? From the days when names were still on it?"

"…A few of them, I remember. Not all of them. There was less paperwork back then. Probably only possible because there were fewer people."

"…Was it better then, or is it better now?"

This time the Secretary answered without hesitating.

"…Now is better. That much I'm sure of."

I was glad of that certainty. I'd seen this person speak this plainly, about anything, almost never before that point.

---

I confirmed this again with Tyche the next day.

"…So is there really no gap like that anymore?"

"…Almost none. I can't say completely none, but compared to before, it's not even close. It's been checked by district, by gender, by age group — all of it comes out nearly even."

"…Is all of that made public?"

"…Yes. It goes out as statistics every year. I can show you all five of the last five years, if you'd like."

Tyche really did bring me those statistical tables the next day. I flipped through them one page at a time. It really was true. The acceptance rate by classification and by district all fell within a similar range. There was no section unusually low or unusually high.

"…Seeing this really makes me feel, all over again, what a genuinely good city this is."

Tyche smiled.

"…I like this table too. It reassures me every time I look at it."

---

Of course, I also thought, that day, about what had been lost behind that ease. Being left as only a number also meant no one knew what kind of person that was. I said as much to Tyche.

"…But if there's only a number, doesn't that mean no one knows what kind of person that is?"

"…That's exactly what makes it fair. Not knowing what kind of person it is means judging only by the conditions."

"…But isn't it a little sad? That person must have had a name."

Tyche looked thoughtful at that.

"…If I had to choose between sadness and fairness, I'd choose fairness. If having a name is such a good thing, we could go back to a world with names like before — but then the gap comes back with it."

I couldn't argue with that. It was true. In this city, names and the gap had disappeared together.

---

That day, leaving the meeting room, I ran into someone in front of the counter. Someone who'd come to have their classification confirmed, requesting a correction because their number had been written wrong on the paperwork. I waited beside them while the counter clerk fixed it, and once the correction was done, I spoke to them.

"…Is it a big hassle, when a number gets wrong?"

They looked at me for a moment and answered.

"…It's a hassle. But hearing about how it used to be, this is nothing at all. In my mother's time, apparently, it wasn't classification — people were just split by what neighborhood they lived in. Someone from one neighborhood, whatever they applied for came out late; someone from another, it came out fast. There's none of that now, is there. As long as the number's right, everyone's handled exactly the same."

I nodded, hearing that. They said it without any particular resentment. If anything, their face looked satisfied with how things worked now.

"…So this way is better?"

"…Much better. I don't care if no one knows who I am, at all. As long as my conditions get looked at properly."

I turned that answer over for a long time. For this person, losing a name wasn't a loss — it was a gain. Hearing that, I found myself weighing, all over again that day, just how much of a relief the system of numbers and classifications must genuinely have been, for so many people.

After that person left, I read, once more, the notice posted on the counter's bulletin board. "This system protects every applicant from factors outside their stated conditions." That day was the first time I truly read that one line. I must have passed it dozens of times before, but that day the sentence reached me differently.

---

On the way home, I called back to mind the statistical table I'd seen that day. A bar graph stretching evenly across every district. I liked it. No district stuck out unusually far, and no district was unusually pressed down.

Passing by the water channel, it occurred to me, suddenly, that this water had no name either, no matter where it had come from. It was only ever called the water channel. People, too, were each called by number and classification, but that water didn't even have that much — it was simply "the water channel." I found the thought amusing and laughed a little to myself. I couldn't decide whether to call that treatment worse than a person's, or freer than a person's. I let the thought go without dwelling on it long, that day.

My aunt asked, as she was setting dinner out, "…What did you learn today?" "…About numbers and classifications. That taking out names made things more fair." My aunt nodded. "…That's right. That one's a genuine improvement. That's one I can confidently say got better."

I held onto that for a long while. It was rare for my aunt to speak with this much certainty. Leaning on that certainty, I closed out the day as a very good one.

After we'd finished eating, my aunt suddenly asked, "…But Hora, do you know your own number?" I was caught off guard for a moment. I knew my committee assignment number, but I'd never once looked up my classification number as a citizen. "…I don't know. I should look it up." My aunt laughed. "…Knowing it won't really do much for you. But it's fun to know. It's seeing how you're written down as a figure, inside this city." Hearing that, I thought, for the first time, that I too might already exist, somewhere in some document, with no name at all, only a number. That thought wasn't frightening to me, that day. If anything, it sounded fair.
