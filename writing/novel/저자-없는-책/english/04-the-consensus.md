# Chapter 4. The Consensus

> _Previously_ — Hora, through her first deliberation. Nineteen of the
> twenty-two seats handled six cases. No one called anything their own
> opinion, and even running the session only came around in turn. That
> night she caught a flicker of a thought — that "it will be put in order"
> had no subject — but took it for nothing more than a manner of speech.

Ask me to explain exactly what the Consensus is, and I still can't do it well. Most people born and raised in this city probably can't either. It's a bit like being asked what air is. It's always there, so no one ever bothers building the words to explain it.

I'll try anyway. Only as much as I know.

---

The Consensus doesn't hand down decisions — it gathers them.

I first learned that difference at school. The teacher explained it by comparing the Consensus to a scale. Put weight on one side of a scale, and the other side tips. The heavier side wins. The Consensus, she said, is not a scale.

"…The Consensus doesn't load anything on. It only gathers. Every record of everyone living in this city, every precedent, every statistic, every objection. It gathers it all and finds the center of that weight. No one decides where the center is. It simply appears there on its own, because that's how the things are arranged."

I liked that comparison at the time. I liked the phrase "center of weight." A point that comes into being on its own, without anyone pushing. A scale shows you who won. A center of weight doesn't show you who won. It's simply there. I believed, then — truly believed — that this difference explained everything about this city.

*I still call that comparison to mind sometimes, even now. And every time, I run into the same question. For it to be true that the center of weight appears on its own, it matters, from the start, how the things were arranged. Who put those things there. I couldn't ask that question that day. I was ten, and the comparison was pretty. That's all it was.*

The teacher drew that comparison on the board too — a picture of a scale, and beside it a scatter of dots with one small dot marked at the center. "This dot in the middle is the Consensus." I copied that drawing into my notebook. That notebook must be somewhere still; I've never gone looking for it. I'm a little afraid that if I did, I'd only confirm all over again how little I doubted, back then, taking it all down.

At that same moment, some kid raised a hand and asked, "Who put those dots there?" The teacher answered, smiling, "All of us did. Every record we leave behind, living our lives — that's the dots." I thought, at the time, that answer was enough. All of us. I didn't know then how wide that phrase was, and at the same time, how empty.

At break I talked to that kid again. They still looked unsettled about something. "…But if you say all of us, doesn't that end up meaning no one in particular?" I wanted to argue with that, but I didn't really have anything to argue back with. "…What's the difference between no one and all of us?" I asked back. They couldn't answer either. We left that question sitting there that day and moved on to the next class. I never once pulled that question back out again for years afterward. A question left sitting doesn't vanish — it only gets put off.

---

The first time I heard the sheer volume of material the Consensus handles, I was startled.

Every application in this city, every meeting record, every complaint, every statistic goes into the Consensus. Material from ten years ago, I was told, and from a hundred years ago too. Nothing, they said, had ever been erased.

I liked that phrase, "nothing erased." I'd heard that elsewhere, old records tend to disappear, or get quietly removed whenever it's convenient for someone. Here there was none of that. Look up an old record, and it really was all there.

Once, for a school assignment, I looked up a ration record from a hundred years back. It really existed. What had been short that winter, who received how many units, even how that decision had come out — all of it remained. That was when I truly felt, for the first time, that this city hides not one thing of its past.

*Hiding nothing, and knowing nothing — those are different sentences. I used those two sentences as if they meant the same thing, for a long time. A great deal of this book is the process of learning that they're actually different.*

Working on that assignment, I also tried to look up the name of the person in charge of rations a hundred years ago. There wasn't one. Only the department's title. I didn't think that was strange, at the time. A hundred years had passed, I figured, of course the name wouldn't survive. But when I asked the teacher, she said names had never been kept, not even a hundred years ago. "…It's been that way from the start. Leave an individual official's name on record, and eventually someone ends up resenting that person, or praising them." I nodded at that explanation. Records with neither resentment nor praise in them. That was how this city had kept itself for a hundred years.

I got a good mark on that assignment. I'd copied the department title and the procedure exactly. Other kids got similar marks. No one pointed out that there was no name for the person in charge. There was no reason to point it out. Writing down that something wasn't there, when it truly wasn't there, was never grounds for losing marks. Walking home that day, I felt, all over again, how entirely natural it was that no one wondered about what wasn't there.

---

There's a separate reason I came to respect the Consensus.

The Consensus has never once claimed to be right. Whenever a deliberation's result came out, the grounds for it were always attached beside it. Not "This decision is correct," but "Gathering these materials produces this result."

And when an objection came in, the Consensus never defended itself. It recalculated. From the start it assumed that a result could change if new material came in. I thought this was an extremely humble attitude. That the possibility of being wrong was something the system itself acknowledged.

*It might sound strange to attach the word humility to a system. But I still can't take that word away from it, even now. The Consensus has never once said, "I decided this." That it never says that struck me, at the time, as a virtue. That not saying it might actually mean it couldn't say it — that never once crossed my mind, at the time.*

I used to compare this humility to stories of other cities. Whenever kids who'd come from outside talked about where they used to live, a name always came up. That person decided it, it turned out this way because of that person, if not for that person it would have gone differently. Every time I heard stories like that, I felt tired. I kept wondering why someone had to be dragged into it every single time. Here, there was no need for that. Even when a result didn't sit right, there was no face to resent. Having no face to resent felt hollow at first, but once I got used to it, it felt freeing instead. I enjoyed that freedom for a long time. I haven't entirely given it up, even now. But that this freedom carried a price — I only learn that later in this book. Having no face to resent meant, also, having no face to apologize. At the time, I had no need to look at the reverse side of that sentence.

---

I once asked the person next to me how the Consensus actually calculates. They said they didn't know the exact method either.

"…I don't know the calculation method itself. I can see all the underlying material, but how it's all combined to produce a result…"

"…No one knows that?"

"…It's less that no one knows, more that there's no need to know. If there's an objection to a result, you just look at the material again. What matters is being able to verify the result without knowing the process."

I nodded at that answer, and then thought about it again on the way home. Whether being able to verify a result without knowing the process, and no one knowing the process, are really the same thing. I reached no conclusion that day.

For a few days after, I went looking for material that explained how the Consensus calculates. The library had a few introductory volumes. I read them all, and every one of them, however detailed about how a result was verified, blurred over how the calculation happened before that verification with a single phrase — "through a complex process." I didn't think that came from carelessness — I thought the people who'd written it simply didn't know any more than that either. No one had hidden anything. Everyone had written exactly as much as they knew. The problem seemed to be that no one knew — but at the time, I didn't even think to call that a problem.

---

I also wondered whether there was anything the Consensus couldn't handle.

One day I asked the person running the session, "…Is there anything the Consensus can't decide?" They thought for a moment and answered,

"…It can't decide something that's never been brought up as a case."

"…Why wouldn't something get brought up?"

"…Because it only gets brought up if someone brings it up."

I let that answer pass without much thought, at the time. It sounded like the obvious thing to say. You need a case before you can handle it — how would you handle something that doesn't exist. I didn't think of the water channel, in that moment. I truly didn't think of it, that day.

*Writing this down again now, I know that exchange was one of the most important in this book. I didn't know it then. That I didn't know is the truly frightening part of this book. It isn't that I missed something important — I had no material to even judge whether it was important or not.*

I asked the person running that session one more thing. "…Then who can bring something up? Can anyone?" They answered. "…Yes. Anyone. Write your name and your reason, and it's accepted." "…Why does a name have to be written?" "…So you know whose case it is. Without a name, it isn't accepted." I thought, at the time, that this rule was fairly fair. That anyone could bring up a case at all. Why the condition of writing a name existed, I let go as nothing more than administrative procedure. I still didn't know how rare a thing it was, in this city, to write down a name.

Walking back to my seat that day, I suddenly wondered. Of the nineteen people sitting in this room, had any of them ever brought up a case under their own name. I didn't ask. Even if I had, I wouldn't have caught the weight of the answer, not then.

---

A few days later, I looked up one of the Consensus's calculated results — not the summary this time, but the full text of the grounds behind it. There was no particular reason. It was just my habit of reading.

The underlying material was vast. More than twenty related cases, an opinion paper from each district, five past decisions of a similar kind. It took half a day to read it all. And on the last page was a list of everyone who had handled this deliberation. About four hundred people. I paused for a moment, looking at that number.

Four hundred people had been involved in this one matter, and there was no one who had decided it. Each of the four hundred had done something, but adding up all of it, there wasn't a single person who said, "I decided this."

*This is the first time I set this number down here. Four hundred. This number comes up again later. When it does, it comes back far heavier. Right now it was only a curious number. That so many people could be tied up in one case and still no one carried the responsibility — that didn't feel bad to me. It felt safe, if anything.*

I looked more closely at that list. The titles were all different. Intake, cross-checking, confirmation, passing on, putting in order, convening. About six titles, divided across four hundred people. Each person's job was exactly that one title, nothing more. Whoever handled intake did only intake; whoever cross-checked did only cross-checking. Passing through all six stages was what produced a decision — and yet, among all six stages, not one of them carried the title "decision." I only found that curious, at the time. If you asked who did the job of deciding, this list couldn't answer precisely. And yet the decision was plainly there, right at the end.

---

That night, I told my aunt about the Consensus.

"…Auntie, I get why the Consensus is good. Nobody claims to be right."

My aunt looked up from her meal.

"…Why's that good?"

"…Then no one can be cornered for being wrong either. Everyone just gathered it, after all."

My aunt thought for a moment and said,

"…No one being wrong, and no one being right — isn't that a hair's difference apart?"

I let that pass, that day, without giving it much thought. My aunt sometimes said cryptic things like that. I finished my meal, and that sentence sat quietly inside me for a while.

*Sitting quietly doesn't mean it disappeared. I end up pulling that sentence back out, months later, in a place entirely different from this one. My aunt, eating her meal that day, probably didn't know how precise a thing she'd said. Neither did I.*

Finishing her meal and clearing the bowls, my aunt added one more thing. "…But that's not to say it's a bad thing. I've lived here over forty years myself, and I've never once been badly wronged." That remark stayed with me more than the last one had. No one being right wasn't a frightening thing to my aunt. The fact of never having been wronged outweighed that fear, and then some. I thought, that day, that her arithmetic was sound. Forty years without being wronged. I thought that might be enough, all on its own.

I lay down and went back over, in order, what I'd learned that day. The Consensus gathers. It doesn't measure. It's humble. It hides nothing. Four hundred people can be involved and there's still no one who decided. Laid side by side, these sentences were strangely harmonious. Consistent, as if written by the same hand. I liked that consistency. Every explanation this city gave never contradicted another. I believed, at the time, that consistency and truth were the same thing.

The days that followed were no different from before. I sat in the meeting room and read the summaries, and once everyone else had left, I read the full text to the end. The result always matched the grounds. That reassured me. The Consensus had never once told a lie.
