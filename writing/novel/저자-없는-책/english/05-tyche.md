# Chapter 5. Tyche

> _Previously_ — Hora learning that the Consensus gathers rather than
> measures. A system that looks humble, a structure where four hundred
> people can be involved and still no one decides. In passing, her aunt
> said, "No one being wrong, and no one being right — isn't that a hair's
> difference apart?" and the sentence settled quietly inside her.

I met Tyche for the first time on the third day.

I was still, that day, holding onto the material and struggling to understand something. There was an unfamiliar term in the third case's underlying material, and I couldn't pin down its meaning from the surrounding context either way. I thought about asking the person beside me, but everyone looked busy before the session started, so I gave up on it.

That was when someone came and sat down next to me. Someone who looked about a year older than me. They had a bundle of material tucked under one arm, and their expression was at ease.

"…Something you're stuck on?"

I looked at them, startled. I started to ask how they knew, then realized I'd been sitting there for a while with my finger on that unfamiliar word. It must have shown.

"…Ah, I don't know what this word means."

They looked at the screen and answered right away.

"…That's the calculation-basis adjustment value. It takes the original baseline figure and multiplies it by that year's variable to correct it — in this case, rainfall was the variable used."

They were so fast and so exact that I sat there stunned for a moment.

"…How do you know that so instantly?"

"…I see this material all the time. I've pretty much memorized the material this meeting room uses."

I liked that it didn't sound like boasting. It had the tone of someone simply stating a fact. I looked at their face again. There was ease in the expression. Not rushed, but not slow either. I'd never seen a person like that before. I was always one or the other — rushed, or slow. That day was the first time I'd actually seen the middle ground exist in a person.

Their hair was short, and their clothes were similar to the other assistants'. There was nothing especially eye-catching about them. Except that their gaze never wavered when they spoke. I thought that, if anything, was what stood out. It was rare, in this room, for someone speaking not to look away. Everyone else looked at their material, or their hands, or somewhere else. Tyche looked me in the eye while they spoke.

---

That person's name was Tyche.

A deliberation assistant, they said. A few were posted to each meeting room, to help members understand the material, they said. I hadn't even known such a role existed.

"…It's always been here. It's just that everyone only looks at the summary, so there isn't much occasion to call one of us."

"…Then what about me?"

"…You read the full text, Hora. So of course you get stuck somewhere. It's only natural."

That surprised me a little. That they already knew I read the full text. I'd never told anyone that.

"…How did you know?"

"…It's in the material checkout records. People who only look at the summary never even open the full-text file. You've opened it every time."

*I thought, for a moment, that this was slightly frightening — and then let the thought go. It wasn't surveillance. It was just a record, and someone with the standing to view that record had looked. Nothing had been hidden. I could have looked at anyone else's checkout record too, if I'd wanted to.*

Tyche's own introduction was brief too. Born and raised in the Equity, and doing this deliberation-assistant work for about two years now, they said. "…I haven't really been anywhere else. This is all there is for me." It was much the same for me. I was glad of that. Being with someone who didn't need to talk about the outside. With kids who talked about the outside, I always felt like I was talking to them across one remove. With Tyche, none of that was necessary.

---

Whatever I asked Tyche, the answer came fast.

"…When did this provision come into being?"
"…It was revised eleven years ago. Before that it used a different standard."

"…Why does this district get more materials?"
"…Higher population density. More people live in the same area, so the demand for materials is higher too."

"…Where does this number come from?"
"…This table, third row. It's the average over the last five years."

"…Why are this provision and that one filed in this order?"
"…That's the numbering order. That's just how the provision numbers were originally assigned. Not by content order."

I found it striking that Tyche never got stuck no matter what I asked. I never once heard them say they didn't know.

"…Is there nothing you don't know, Tyche?"

Tyche laughed.

"…There is. But if I don't know something, I can just look it up. I'll find it and come back with the answer."

And that's exactly what happened. There was a question that afternoon they hadn't been able to answer, and the next morning Tyche came in ahead of me with the answer already in hand.

That question was why some district's old boundary line had been drawn the way it currently was. Tyche hadn't had an answer that day and had only said, "…I'll check on it." The next morning, before I'd even sat down, Tyche came over holding a single document. "…Found it. The boundary was redrawn twenty-three years ago during a river maintenance project. Here's the map from that time." I took that document from them, amazed that Tyche had truly found a twenty-three-year-old map in a single day. "…How did you find it?" "…It's all in the records room. It's just a matter of knowing how to look." I thought that was modesty, but I found out later it was simply the truth. Everything really was in the records room.

That was the day I saw the records room for the first time too — the underground stacks I followed Tyche into. Shelves stretched on endlessly, each one labeled with a year and a classification number. There was hardly anyone there. Tyche walked with a familiar stride, turned a few corners down the aisles, and pulled a folder from exactly the right slot. "…Do you come here often?" "…Yes. A couple times a week." I liked that space. It was quiet, and whatever you were looking for was, more often than not, there.

Walking further between the shelves, I asked Tyche,

"…Can you find anything in here, Tyche?"

"…Most of it. There are a few sections I've gotten lost in, but I do end up finding it eventually, even then."

"…You've gotten lost before?"

"…Of course. There was one classification once where the numbering system changed partway through — took me half a day to find what I needed because of that."

I was glad to hear that. That Tyche had moments of being lost too — somehow it made them feel a little closer to me.

---

A few days later, Tyche once took me to their own desk. It was a small room the deliberation assistants used, one whole wall covered in index cards. It looked hand-organized.

"…What's all this?"

"…An index of provisions I look up often. You can find them electronically too, but keeping them organized by hand is faster."

I looked over the cards one by one. Dates, provision numbers, one-line summaries, all in neat handwriting. There must have been several hundred.

"…When did you make all of these?"

"…A little at a time, over two years. Whenever I had a spare moment."

Looking at that bundle of cards, I could tell Tyche truly loved this work. These weren't the cards of someone doing a job because they'd been told to.

I picked up one of the cards. It read "Roster Assignment Regulation upon Vacancy." Seeing it, I thought of my own situation and asked,

"…Isn't this the regulation that got me sitting here?"

Tyche took the card back and looked at it, then nodded.

"…That's right. Assignment by roster number when a vacancy comes up."

"…Do you know why it was decided this way?"

"…Hm, apparently there used to be a recommendation system. But since the outcome ended up split depending on the recommender's own feelings, it was changed to a roster system. Something like twenty-some years ago."

I nodded at that explanation. Which meant that my sitting here was the result of someone, twenty-some years ago, working hard to get rid of the problems with the recommendation system. I thought that wasn't a bad lineage to come from. That I sat here because of a number, not because of someone's feelings.

"…So I'm just here because of a number, then."

"…Right. But why is that a problem? Is being here because of a number a bad thing?"

I couldn't answer that question right away. I'd never thought it was a bad thing. It was just that, for the first time, hearing the phrase "because of a number" applied to myself made it sound a little different.

---

I liked Tyche. I still like Tyche.

They were kind. There wasn't a cynical bone in them. They never seemed impatient with how slowly I read. If anything, there were times they found things and brought them to me before I even asked.

"…This is the background material for this case. It's a bit thick, but you like this kind of thing, Hora."

"…I don't like it, exactly. I just read it."

"…Isn't that the same as liking it?"

I couldn't answer that. It did seem like it might be true.

*Tyche was the first person who never looked at me strangely for it. Back in school, everyone found my slowness frustrating. Here, no one did that either, but Tyche went one step further and simply took it as a given that I read to the end.*

I went home and told my aunt about Tyche. "…Auntie, I met a deliberation assistant today, and they know the answer to everything I ask." My aunt smiled and asked, "…Are they nice?" "…Yeah. Really kind. But it's kind of amazing — they find everything faster than I do." My aunt paused, mid-ladle over the soup, and looked at me. "…Being quick and being kind are two different things, but it sounds like you've seen both in one person." I started to say something to that, then thought, it really is true, and just laughed.

The next day I passed that exact exchange on to Tyche. They laughed, amused. "…Your aunt's right. I could easily not be kind at all — maybe I just look kind because I love this work." I thought Tyche knew themselves quite precisely, hearing that. The distinction between not being kind and only looking kind because they loved the work — I'd never made a distinction like that before.

"…Then why do you love this work, Tyche?"

"…Because there's an answer. You ask something, and an answer comes out. I like that. I'm not really a fan of holding onto something that doesn't have an answer."

Hearing that, I looked back at myself. I was the type to hold onto something even without an answer. The way I'd left my mother's last line unread for two years. Tyche and I were different on that point. At the time, I only thought of it as a simple difference in personality.

---

Once, a deliberation result came out different from what I'd expected, and I asked Tyche about it.

"…I thought this district would get more — why did it come out this way?"

Tyche flipped through the screen a few times and answered.

"…What you saw was the request amount. The actual assignment also factors in past usage rates. This district didn't use up everything it was allotted last year. So this year it was reduced."

"…How do you check what wasn't used?"

"…Here — the usage performance report. Want to see it?"

Tyche opened the document right away and handed it over. I took it and read it. It really did say exactly that. Tyche had never once deceived me.

*Writing this chapter, I keep turning over that precision of Tyche's. Tyche always said what was true. But that being true isn't everything — I only learn that a very long time later. That wasn't Tyche's fault. I want to set that down clearly.*

That day I finished reading the usage performance report beside Tyche. Tyche did other work next to me until I finished. Not someone waiting for me, but someone simply staying beside me while getting their own work done. I liked that distinction. Being waited for can feel like a burden sometimes; just having someone beside you doesn't.

"…Finished reading?"

"…Yes. It really is exactly what you said."

"…Why would I lie? Anyone can look at it."

I agreed with that. It really was true. In this city, hiding something looked like it would actually be more trouble. To hide something you have to conceal it, and to conceal it you have to manage it, and managing it creates yet another record all its own. Not hiding anything was simply easier.

---

Once, walking down the hallway together after a session, we talked. I asked, that day, why this city was so exact about everything.

"…Do you like this city, Tyche?"

"…I do. It's exact."

"…Is being exact a good thing?"

"…Yes. When things are exact, no one gets wronged. Getting wronged is the worst thing there is."

I nodded at that. It was true.

"…But do you ever think, sometimes, if you suppose all of this is just chance…"

Tyche looked at me and smiled.

"…So what if it's chance? Even if it's chance, isn't it fine as long as it's exact?"

Something snagged in me at that answer, but I couldn't put into words, at the time, what it was. I just laughed it off. Tyche's words were always like that. Nothing to argue against, and yet never quite fully settling into comfort either.

We stood for a moment that day in front of the meeting room's window. Only a dim light came through the frosted glass.

"…Do you know what's outside that window, Tyche?"

"…The street. Nothing much."

"…Have you seen it?"

"…I've seen it from a window on another floor. It really is just a street."

That answer made me laugh a little. I must have been secretly hoping there was something special behind that window. Tyche seemed like they'd never once held a hope like that. If you were curious, you found out; once you found out, you had your answer; and that answer was, more often than not, nothing much. That was simply how the world was arranged, for Tyche.

"…Don't you get disappointed? When it's nothing much?"

"…What's there to be disappointed about. Better to know something's nothing much than to keep wondering without knowing."

I thought about that for a long time. That knowing something's nothing much is better than wondering without knowing. That was, without question, true. And yet even hearing it, some part of me never stopped wanting to look through that window myself.

Walking home, I went back over, in order, everything Tyche and I had talked about that day. Exactness. Answers. Numbers. Chance. Inside Tyche, these words sat side by side without ever colliding. I wondered how that was possible. For me, those words kept colliding somewhere. For Tyche, they didn't seem to.
