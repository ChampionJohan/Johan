# Chapter 12. The Empty Box

> _Previously_ — Hora learning that the water channel, having come right
> into the western edge of the city, has never once been brought up as a
> case, and so administratively doesn't exist. Tyche answered precisely:
> "If it's never been submitted, it doesn't exist. Not hidden." But her
> aunt asked back, "Isn't being treated as not existing the same thing
> either way?" Hora fell asleep without resolving that difference.

Starting the next day, I began looking at the signature box longer than I usually did.

It wasn't that I was trying to confirm anything in particular. My aunt's question from the night before had simply stayed with me, and I was someone who couldn't settle until I'd checked whatever stayed with me. It was the one habit in this city I'd never managed to change.

The moment I opened my eyes that morning, I thought again of what my aunt had said the day before. "Isn't being treated as not existing the same thing either way?" I turned it over even while eating breakfast. My aunt was ladling out soup as if it were nothing at all, but that one question from the night before kept circling in my head.

I thought about it walking to the meeting room too. Being treated as if the water channel doesn't exist, and the water channel truly not existing. I knew those were different things. Tyche had told me exactly that. But knowing they were different didn't settle my mind. I wanted to know where that unsettled feeling was coming from. So that day, I decided to look at the signature box again. It had nothing to do with the water channel, and yet somehow, in my mind, the two kept landing in the same place.

---

That day, once I'd finished reading the case I'd been given in the meeting room, I looked, as always, at the bottom of the last page. The signature box. Empty.

I'd seen that box dozens of times before. Before, I'd always just passed over it. That day, I looked at it longer.

A long, narrow horizontal box. Small letters above it read "the signature box," and beneath that, nothing at all. I read the small print beside it again. "This document does not require a signature." Does not require. Not the same as forbidding. I'd read that sentence once before. I'd let it pass then.

That day, for the first time, I pulled out other cases too and checked only the signature box.

Empty.

I looked again at the intake stamp beside it. The stamp was clear. The date was clear. The handling progress was recorded in close detail. The list of underlying material was attached, nothing missing. The objection field and the response field were both filled in. Nothing was missing from this document. Nothing except that one box.

It occurred to me, suddenly, to compare this box to all the others. The date box had a date in it. The stamp box had a stamp in it. The intake-number box had a number in it. Every box in this city existed to be filled. Every one, except this one.

*That day was the first time I had this thought. More precisely, that day was the first time I noticed that this thought had occurred to me at all. It had probably crossed my mind before, a few times, but I think I'd let it slip past without holding onto it, back then.*

---

I pulled out the next case. This was one I had no part in, from the Second Deliberation District. The signature box.

Empty.

I went to the reading room and requested three more completely unfamiliar cases. I checked all three.

Empty.

I pulled out the twenty-year-old material again too. The same material I'd seen once before. The signature box.

Empty.

---

From that point I decided to go about this a little differently. Instead of just looking at a lot of cases at random, I varied the kind. Ration cases, housing cases, objection-handling cases, old ones, recent ones, evenly across the First through Sixth Deliberation Districts.

I checked them one by one, writing the list down in my notebook.

First District, ration adjustment, three years ago. Empty.
Third District, housing relocation, eight years ago. Empty.
Fifth District, objection re-review, last year. Empty.
Second District, road widening, eleven years ago. Empty.
Sixth District, materials assignment, two years ago. Empty.

I kept going this way. The reading-room librarian noticed me filling out one new application after another and said something.

"…Looking for anything special today?"

"…No, just something I need to check."

The librarian didn't press further and kept handing me material.

At one point, flipping through the twelfth document, I stopped breathing for a moment. Something faint showed at the bottom box. I carried the document to the window and held it up to the light. It was a pressure mark, from age — where the paper had been pressed by something. Not writing. Just a trace left by another document pressed against it. My heart raced for a moment, thinking I'd found something, and then, once I confirmed the mark was nothing, I felt strangely deflated instead. I didn't know, myself, what I'd been hoping for.

*I want to write this moment honestly. I was looking for something, at the time. Without even knowing what I was looking for. This wasn't an investigation. An investigation needs a purpose, and I had none. My eyes just kept going to that box.*

I looked at a few more cases after that. The form had changed slightly, era to era. The very old ones were handwritten tables; the recent ones were printed. The shape of the box had shifted a little too — some square, some a long horizontal line. But that the box always sat at the very bottom of the last page, and that it was always empty — not one of those two things had ever once changed.

Curious whether this was something the regulations had set down, or just something carried on as custom, I looked up the form's history of changes. The regulation said only, "A confirmation field shall be placed at the end of the document." It didn't say exactly when the term "confirmation field" or the term "signature box" had first come into use. It didn't say who had first created this box. Not even the name of whoever had made this box remained, anywhere, now.

---

By that afternoon I'd checked twenty cases.

All twenty — the signature box was empty.

I looked back through my notebook. The districts were different. The kinds of cases were different. The years were different. Some were three pages, some over forty. There was exactly one thing in common.

The box at the very bottom was always empty.

I stared at that list for a long while. I thought it was strange. But exactly what was strange about it, I couldn't put into words. I closed the notebook, then opened it again, and read it through from the start, in case I'd missed something. There was nothing. The twenty lines stayed exactly twenty lines.

---

I went to Tyche and showed them the notebook.

"…Look at this. All twenty — the signature box is empty."

Tyche flipped through the notebook and answered.

"…Yeah, that's how it always is. I know that."

"…Doesn't that seem strange?"

"…Hmm, why would it be strange? It says right there that a signature isn't required."

"…No, but not required and no one writing it are different things. If it isn't forbidden, couldn't at least one person have written something?"

Tyche looked thoughtful at that question.

"…That's true, I guess. But it's empty because no one's written anything. What's strange about that?"

I didn't know what to say back to that. Tyche wasn't wrong. That it was empty because no one had written anything was simply a fact.

"…But is there anything in the regulations that says not to fill it in? Did you check?"

Tyche searched the screen for a moment.

"…No. Nothing comes up, even searching. There's no line saying not to fill it in."

"…Then why does no one fill it in?"

"…Huh. I've never thought about that, myself."

Tyche really did fall into thought for a moment, right there. I would go on to remember that expression for a long time. That was the first time I'd seen Tyche wear the face of not knowing an answer.

"…But Hora, why does this bother you so much? The document's effect is the same whether it's filled in or not."

"…It's not about the effect. It's just that there's a box, and it's never filled, and that's a little…"

I couldn't finish the sentence. Tyche looked at me for a moment and shrugged.

"…I don't really know. But if it bothers you, maybe that's just how it is."

I couldn't tell whether that answer was meant to comfort me or just to let it go. Tyche went back to their own work after that. There wasn't a trace of looking at me strangely. Just a face a little sorry for not having an answer to give. Watching that face, I wondered, briefly, whether Tyche had ever found this box as unfamiliar as I did. I didn't ask.

"…I don't know. It's just that all twenty were the same."

"…You could look at two hundred, two thousand, and they'd all be the same. Because that's just what this box is."

I thought that was true too. It was true, and still something caught.

---

That evening I showed the Secretary the notebook too, on my way to return material at the counter.

The Secretary looked through the notebook carefully. Page by page, taking their time.

"…You confirmed all of this yourself?"

"…Yes. I was curious."

The Secretary closed the notebook and looked at me. Their expression showed no particular change.

"…That's right. They'll all be empty."

"…Do you know why?"

The Secretary thought for a moment.

"…Because there's no one to fill it in."

"…Not because it's not allowed?"

"…It isn't that it's not allowed. There's no one for it."

Hearing that answer, I thought of what I'd heard at the water channel a few days before. If it's never been submitted, it doesn't exist. If there's no one to fill it in, it doesn't get filled in. I sensed, dimly, that the two statements shared the same shape. Exactly how they were the same, I couldn't put into words.

"…But why is there no one for it?"

This time the Secretary was silent for longer.

"…That much, I don't know myself."

That day was the first time I noticed the words "I don't know" coming out of this person's mouth. Up to then, the Secretary had answered whatever was asked. Either they already knew, or, not knowing, they went and found the answer. This time they only said they didn't know.

I was about to put the notebook back in my bag when I noticed a blank form lying on the counter. The same signature box at the very bottom. I looked at that box for a long while. A pen sat beside it. I reached out and picked up the pen.

The Secretary was watching me. They didn't stop me. They just watched.

I touched the tip of the pen to the box for a moment, and then, in the end, set it down without writing anything.

"…Why not write anything?"

The Secretary asked. Not a scolding tone. Just a curious one.

"…I don't know. It just doesn't feel like a place I'm meant to write."

"…Even though no one's ever decided that?"

"…Yes. Even though no one's ever decided that."

Saying that answer, I thought it strange myself. Knowing it wasn't forbidden, I still couldn't put my hand to that box. Why I couldn't — that, too, I didn't know, that day.

---

That night I sat for a long while with the notebook open in front of me.

Twenty lines. District, case name, year, and in the last column, the three words I'd written in myself — "empty" — repeated twenty times.

I never did manage, that day, to work out why this unsettled me. It wasn't hidden. Tyche had been right about that. It wasn't forbidden either. The Secretary had been right about that too. I didn't think either of them was wrong.

*I remember this night precisely, even now. I reached no conclusion that day. Writing this record, I've felt the temptation, more than once, to attach some plausible sentence to this passage. Something like, "That day I sensed, in my gut, that something was wrong." But that would be a lie. I sensed nothing, that day. I was simply unsettled, and I didn't know why. That was all there was to it.*

I put the notebook away in the drawer. The same drawer that held my mother's pages and my first notice. Closing the drawer, it occurred to me, suddenly, to think again of the last line of my mother's twelve pages. It crossed my mind, briefly, whether there might be some connection between having left that line unread for two years, and these twenty empty boxes going unfilled, year after year, by anyone at all. It felt too forced a connection, and I pushed the thought away quickly.

My aunt opened the door a crack and asked, "…Still up?" "…Just about to sleep." "…Something wrong?" I hesitated, then answered. "…No, just — there was something a little strange, and I don't know what's strange about it."

My aunt came in and sat for a moment on the edge of the bed.

"…What did you see?"

"…I looked at twenty documents today, and the signature box was empty in every one. But I don't even know why that bothers me."

My aunt thought for a moment and asked,

"…Is it strange, the thing itself? Or does it just start feeling strange from looking at it too long?"

I couldn't answer that right away. I genuinely didn't know. Whether it was strange, or whether it only felt strange because I'd stared at it too long — I couldn't tell the two apart.

"…I don't know."

My aunt nodded.

"…Then that's not getting answered today. Go to sleep."

"…Okay."

Before leaving the room, my aunt added one more thing. "…But Hora, when something keeps bothering you, there's usually a reason. You just haven't found it yet." I closed my eyes at that and didn't answer. I had nothing to say back.

My aunt looked at me for a moment and said, "…Then look at it again after you've slept. Everything looks strange at night." I laughed at that and turned off the light.

Lying there in the dark, I kept calling those twenty empty boxes back to mind. None of it was wrong, and none of it sat entirely easy either. I fell asleep that night still not knowing why it unsettled me, and in that state, I slept.
