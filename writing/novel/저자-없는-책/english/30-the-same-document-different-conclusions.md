# Chapter 30. The Same Document, Different Conclusions

> _Previously_ — Tyche said she too wished there were a meaning. "But
> wanting there to be one isn't evidence that there is." I couldn't
> refute her. The next day, we decided to read the same document side
> by side.

The document was a newly assigned deliberation matter. A rezoning to
consolidate distribution warehouses in the city's eastern district. On
its face it looked like an ordinary piece of administrative
housekeeping. About forty pages. I planned, as I always did, to read
it start to finish, and Tyche planned, as she always did, to start
with the summary and the conclusion. This time we decided to do it
side by side, at the same table.

"…We're really doing this together?"

"…Yes. I want to see what looks different."

"…Sounds interesting. I've never done a comparison like this."

Tyche's face was genuinely interested. Not teasing me — curious,
because she'd never tried a comparison like this before.

*Writing this chapter, I'm being careful. The temptation keeps coming
to write that I saw more than Tyche did, here. But that isn't true. I
only spent more time. Spending more time doesn't mean seeing more
accurately. I'm trying not to forget that, through this whole
chapter.*

It was afternoon in the meeting hall. Light came in through the
window. Everyone else was away for lunch, and only the two of us were
left. Two copies of the same document lay side by side on the table.

---

We agreed to each read in our own way and meet again after. We hadn't
set an order. Tyche simply read first, in her own way. It took her
less than ten minutes. Tyche checked the summary, the conclusion, and
the progress table.

"…I've gone through it."

"…What do you see?"

"…Intake, review, confirming those involved three times, gathering
opinions twice, convening four times. All handled by regulation. No
objections filed. Confirmed."

"…That's all?"

"…Yes. Not a single procedural flaw. The confirmation dates all
check out, the number of those involved exceeds the requirement, and
the convening notice periods were all observed."

Listening to her, I could tell Tyche had read this document with
genuine accuracy. Tyche wasn't lying. The document really did say
exactly what she'd said.

"…This is a well-made matter, as far as that goes. Consolidating
three warehouses cuts the management staff needed and shortens
distribution times. It's good for people."

"…Nothing bad in it at all?"

"…Procedurally, no. What I can see is procedure. Whether the outcome
turns out good or bad takes time to know, but as far as this document
goes, there's no basis to call it bad."

Tyche set the summary down on the table as she said this. I picked up
the forty-page original stacked beside it.

---

I began reading the forty pages from the start, after that. Tyche
waited beside me, sorting other papers. She glanced at me now and
then, but didn't rush me.

It took about two hours. The consolidation itself was no problem.
Three warehouses were being merged into one, and the distribution
procedure would change only slightly.

The problem was on the twenty-eighth page. It said that of the three
existing warehouse managers, two would lose their posts in the
consolidation. A reassignment procedure was specified, but it said
only "according to the individual's wish." How that wish had been
confirmed wasn't stated.

I read that part over a few times. That no method of confirmation was
stated wasn't itself a flaw — there was no clause requiring the
regulations to specify a method.

The three managers' names weren't given. They were listed only by
number. One would stay in the new consolidated warehouse; the other
two were classified as "reassignment subjects." Of the two
reassignment subjects, one would move to a warehouse in another
district, and the other would be moved to office work related to
distribution.

I stopped there and looked out the window a moment, then read again
the part about the one being moved to office work. Managing a
warehouse had originally been physical work, and office work was an
entirely different kind of job. Whether this person truly wanted the
move, or had gone there simply because no other post was left, the
document alone couldn't tell me.

*I stopped a long while at this part. If this person had truly wanted
the move, there was no problem at all. But whether they had truly
wanted it, or whether it had been written that way because there was
no other option, this document didn't tell me. That it didn't tell me
doesn't mean I could conclude it was the bad case. I know that too.*

I kept reading through the rest of the pages. Budget items, changes to
logistics routes, adjustments to distribution hours. All unremarkable.
Aside from page twenty-eight, there was nothing that caught in my mind
in this document, either. I understood why Tyche called this a
"well-made matter." Most of it really was well put together.

I wrote earlier that reading all forty pages took two hours; more
precisely, half of that time went into reading and rereading those
twenty-eight pages several times over. The remaining thirty-nine
pages I read in an hour.

---

Having read through to the last page, I looked at the bottom field.

The signature box. Blank.

"…Finished?"

"…Yes."

"…What did you see?"

I told her about page twenty-eight. The reassignment of a manager,
the phrase "according to the individual's wish," the absence of a
confirmation method.

Tyche opened to that page again.

"…This? I saw it too. But this isn't a flaw. Confirming the
individual's wish is left to each department's discretion. That the
regulations don't specify a method is deliberate. Situations differ by
department."

"…But then there's no way to confirm whether that person really
wanted it."

"…It's not that there's no way — it means the department handled it
on its own judgment. That it isn't written in this document doesn't
mean it wasn't done."

---

I stopped for a moment, hearing that. Tyche wasn't wrong. Not written
down doesn't mean not done. I knew that too. And still that gap kept
catching at me.

"…So if I look at that department's documents, would it be
confirmed?"

"…There should be one. Should I look?"

Tyche really did get up and go to another reading terminal. She came
back about ten minutes later.

"…Found it. A distribution-office placement confirmation form. Here,
instead of the individual's signature, there's a record of a verbal
confirmation. It says the official spoke to the person directly, and
they said they were fine with it."

"…A verbal confirmation?"

"…Yes. That's a valid method of confirmation here too."

I took the confirmation form and read it. It really did say that. No
official's name was given — only "confirmed by official." Who that
official was, or how they'd asked, wasn't stated.

"…Do you think this is fine too, Tyche?"

"…Yes. It's procedure. A verbal confirmation is one of the official
methods. It's in the regulations manual too."

"…But even seeing this, I still don't know whether that person
really wanted it."

"…That isn't a question the document can answer, Hora. You'd only
know that by asking that person directly."

That was true too. I'd never met that person. I only knew them by
number.

"…Don't you ever want to meet that person, Tyche?"

"…Not particularly. What I can see is this far, and I don't think
going further is my job."

"…I want to meet them."

"…That's very like you, too."

Tyche wasn't saying that to be sarcastic. She was simply stating a
fact. I always wanted to go past the document, and Tyche did exactly
what could be done within the document. Both were work this city
needed.

"…But this document alone doesn't tell me whether it was really
confirmed or not."

"…No, it doesn't. But that isn't this document's problem — you'd
have to look at more documents from the related department to know.
This document accurately wrote down its own part. There's no reason
this document has to take responsibility for another department's
work too."

"…So you think this has no problem, then, Tyche?"

"…Yes. By my standard. There's a procedure, it was followed, and
there was no objection. That's everything this document needs to
confirm."

---

Hearing that, I pointed at the signature box again.

"…What about this, though? How do you read this?"

Tyche looked at the box.

"…It's blank."

"…Doesn't that seem strange?"

"…What's strange about it. Every document here is like this."

"…That's exactly it. That they're all like this is what seems strange
to me."

Tyche looked at me for a moment and said,

"…What do you see in this blank space, Hora?"

I couldn't answer right away. I hadn't worked out what to say I saw.

"…No one, where someone ought to be."

"…But why ought there to be someone? This is a place where the whole
decides, by design. It's not that an individual needs to write it —
being blank isn't the problem."

---

*I've called this conversation back to mind more than once. Tyche saw
the procedure's precision. I saw the blank space. We both looked at
the same document, and neither of us lied. Tyche couldn't be called
wrong. I couldn't be called wrong either.*

We sat a moment in silence. Tyche spoke again.

"…Hora, I don't think this city's way is bad. I think it's good,
actually. Leaving even a verbal confirmation on record is better than
leaving nothing at all."

"…That's true. I agree with that too."

"…But you want more than that, don't you? A signature. Something
written in that person's own hand, their actual name."

"…I think so."

"…Why?"

I couldn't answer this question right away either. It was hard to
explain why a signature was better, what made it different from a
verbal confirmation.

"…I don't know. I just feel like if there's a name, it means that
person was really there. A verbal confirmation feels like someone
speaking for them, saying they were there."

Tyche nodded.

"…I understand that feeling. But that's separate from whether it's
actually true or not."

"…I know. But knowing something and it setting your mind at ease seem
to be different things."

"…Tyche, I'm not trying to say this document is wrong."

"…But?"

"…Just, it feels like something's missing. The procedure's all
correct, but it feels missing."

"…If something's missing, that's a regulatory violation. If it isn't
in the regulations, it isn't missing."

"…Can't something be missing even if it isn't in the regulations?"

Tyche went quiet at that question for a moment. It was the first time
she hadn't had an answer right away.

"…That… I'm honestly not sure, myself."

---

That short silence was the most striking moment in the whole afternoon's
conversation. Tyche always had an answer. But she paused for a moment
in front of this question.

"…You're not sure?"

"…No. Whether something not in the regulations can be called
missing — that doesn't seem like a question about the regulations. It
seems like some other question. But I can't explain what that other
question is, either."

"…So do you think there's something to this blank box too, then,
Tyche?"

"…No. I don't go that far. I still think this document, this blank
box, has no problem. It's only that the question itself, the one
you're asking, stays with me a little."

Tyche went on, sorting papers again.

"…Doing this work for a long time, I've come to see that people want
to fill a blank space with something, once they see it. Whether it
means something good or something bad. But a blank space is, most of
the time, just a blank space."

"…Is that your conclusion?"

"…Yes. About this document. Another document might be different.
I don't think the same way every time either."

"…So this isn't even a certain conclusion for you."

"…It's certain. As far as this document goes. Certain doesn't mean
certain forever."

That was a small comfort to me. Tyche was judging each case fresh,
too. It wasn't that the answer came out automatically.

"…Have there been times something caught at you too, looking at a
blank box like this?"

Tyche thought a moment and said,

"…There have. Not often. But when it does catch, and I check it, it's
usually nothing much. So now, even when it catches, I check first."

"…You checked this one too."

"…Yes. I checked, and by my standard, it was fine."

"…By my standard, something's still left over."

Tyche looked at me and smiled slightly.

"…Then we're both right. We just confirmed different things."

That was the end of that day's conversation. I hadn't won against
Tyche. Tyche hadn't persuaded me either. I came to understand, after
that day, that winning or losing hadn't been the point for either of
us.

*Writing this chapter, I've decided to leave this conversation without
a conclusion. Drawing a conclusion would mean siding with one of us. I
don't want to do that. Tyche read that document accurately. I read it
accurately too. We simply saw different things in the same document,
and that difference wasn't due to either of us making a mistake.*

On the way home, I thought about the person moved to distribution
office work. The one I only knew by number. If I ever met them, I
wanted to ask — had they really wanted the move, or had there been no
other option. I knew it was a little strange, thinking that without
even knowing their face.

*I didn't meet that person, then. Actually meeting someone in this
city who's known only by a number happens much later than I imagined
at the time.*

We didn't talk further, there. We had read the same document
together, and looked at the same blank box together. And in that
blank box, Tyche saw a regulation, and I saw a person. We had both
read that document accurately.
