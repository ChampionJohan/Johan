# Chapter 31. For Such a Time

> _Previously_ — Tyche and I read the same document side by side.
> Tyche saw the procedure's precision, and I saw the blank box. Both
> of us read it accurately. No conclusion came of it. That evening, I
> went to find Temel again.

Temel was due to leave this city before long. His application was
nearly finished, and the person he'd been looking for, he said, had
gone unfound after all. Hearing that, I felt a rush of urgency. There
was still something I wanted to ask.

That evening, instead of the north market, I went to the lodging house
where he was staying. It was a small inn. Temel was sorting his
belongings when he saw me, and looked a little surprised.

"…What brings you here, this hour?"

"…I heard you're leaving tomorrow."

"…Day after. But I tend to pack ahead of time."

---

We sat on the wooden bench in front of the inn. The evening air was
cool. The smell of dinner cooking drifted from the mouth of the alley.
Temel had come out without finishing his packing. His bag was still
half open.

"…You can finish packing, if you want."

"…It's fine. Not much left, anyway."

"…Where are you headed next?"

"…Not sure yet, exactly. I'll probably decide once things here are
wrapped up."

"…You really didn't find the person you were looking for?"

Temel nodded.

"…Right. Must not have been here after all. Or I got here too late."

"…Doesn't that trouble you?"

"…It does. But being troubled and giving up are two different
problems. I'll have to look again in the next city."

Listening to him, I wondered what would happen to the three names he'd
put down here, once he left.

"…What happens to the names you wrote here, once you go?"

"…No, that doesn't end. If the matter's here, the review keeps coming
from here. Wherever I am."

"…So they call you in even if you're in another city?"

"…Yes. If a summons comes, I have to answer it. Not answering
becomes a record too."

"…Does that go on forever?"

"…As long as that matter's still open. I don't know when that ends,
myself."

Hearing that, I finally understood that this man kept dragging
something behind him even as he moved from city to city. I'd thought
until then that review was something that only happened inside this
city.

*Writing this passage, it's strange to me now that I didn't press
further on this, that night. I think some other question was more
urgent to me that night.*

I hesitated, then asked.

"…I still don't know why I ended up in this seat."

"…We talked about that before. Because of the number."

"…Yes, I know that. I'm asking if there's some other reason, besides
that."

Temel looked at me for a moment, then leaned back against the bench.
And his way of speaking changed.

"…Hora."

It was the first time he'd said my name like that. Not the polite
form. I tensed for a moment, but it didn't feel like rudeness — it
felt like a signal that something was about to turn more serious.

*I hadn't particularly noticed, until then, that Temel always spoke to
me formally. Even with the difference in our ages, he had. But that
night, I clearly felt the moment his speech changed. It felt like some
formality had been set aside. Not in a bad direction — toward
something closer.*

---

"…Maybe you're sitting here only because of a number."

"…Yes."

"…But there are still things you can do in a seat you got only
because of a number."

I sat still a moment, hearing that. It was a simple thing to say, and
that simplicity was what made it heavy.

"…What does that mean?"

"…The reason you ended up in that seat was a number. That's true. But
what you'll see there, what you'll read, what you'll come to
know — the number didn't decide that. That's what you do."

---

"…But I don't even really know what I saw. The empty box. I still
don't know what it means, either."

"…You saw it, though, not knowing."

"…I did see it, yes."

"…Then that's enough. Saying what you saw, the way you saw it — that's
what you can do."

"…What's so remarkable about that? It's just something I read."

"…It doesn't have to be remarkable. It doesn't need to be remarkable
to mean something."

"…But isn't that just because that's the kind of person I am, in the
end? I've got the habit of reading to the end. That's not something I
chose either. It's a habit I got because of my mother."

Temel nodded at this.

"…That's right. That might not be something you chose either. Habits
usually aren't."

"…So isn't it all just one coincidence linked to the next, then? A
vacancy, a number, a habit. Not one thing I actually chose."

"…Could be. But Hora, those coincidences linked up, and now you're
sitting here on this bench asking me this question."

*I was caught wordless for a moment, hearing that. I didn't know what
to refute it with. Temel hadn't denied my logic. If anything he'd
granted that all of it was true, and simply set the next sentence on
top.*

It occurred to me to wonder how Tyche would take this. She would
probably say something like this — that a chain of coincidences was
still just coincidence continuing, and that attaching meaning to it
was my own doing. I still thought that was right. And yet Temel's
words sounded equally right to me. That the two could both be right at
once was what confused me most, that night.

"…Tyche would probably argue with that, if she were here."

"…How?"

"…She'd probably say a chain of coincidences is still just
coincidence, and that attaching meaning to it is on me."

Temel nodded.

"…Could be. That could be right too."

"…But what you're saying is right too."

"…Both can be right, Hora. That isn't strange. It's the same thing,
seen, and standing differently."

Hearing that, I thought of the afternoon I'd spent reading the
document beside Tyche. The same document, different conclusions. This
time it was the same chain of coincidences, different conclusions.

"…Was it like that for you too? Back then, when you said you fell
apart?"

"…Yeah. I thought it was all coincidence, back then too. That I'd
ended up in that place, that I fell apart — I thought it was all just
how things happened to go."

"…And now?"

"…Even now, I can't say it wasn't coincidence. But what I did
afterward wasn't coincidence. That, I chose."

"…What did you choose?"

"…Deciding to start over. Deciding to write my name first. Nobody
told me to do that."

"…Why did you decide to start over, then?"

Temel was quiet a moment, then answered.

"…Running kept getting tiring. Even while I was running, something
always got left behind somewhere. There's the trace you leave without
writing it down, and the trace you leave by writing it down, and I
found the trace of not writing it more frightening."

"…Why?"

"…If I didn't write it, later on I couldn't even explain to myself
what I'd done. That was the scariest part. Not other people's
judgment — my own."

"…So that's why you started writing it?"

"…Yeah. At first, that was all it was. Not because it had a meaning —
just because it felt like I needed to be able to explain myself to
myself."

"…And now? Is that still the reason?"

Temel smiled a little.

"…A few more reasons have piled up since. But the very first one is
still the biggest."

I liked that answer. It was more believable for not being some grand
reason.

Listening to him, I was still curious about whatever had happened to
this man when he was young. But I didn't ask further. Tonight I wanted
to ask something else.

---

"…So does that mean there's a meaning to it?"

I held my breath a little, asking this. I really wanted to hear the
answer.

Temel didn't answer right away. He looked at the evening sky a moment,
then looked back at me.

"…I don't know."

"…What?"

"…I don't know either."

---

Hearing that answer, I felt strangely deflated. And at the same time,
strangely at ease. It was odd that the two could come together.

"…If you don't know, who does?"

"…Maybe nobody does. Or maybe it's just me who doesn't."

"…But everyone in this city lives as if they know. Tyche does too."

"…That's a different matter. There's saying you know something you
don't, and there's not asking at all because you don't know. Both
happen. I find something lacking in both, if I'm honest."

"…Are you neither of those?"

"…I just keep asking without knowing. That's the most exhausting way
to do it."

"…Then why say what you said? That there are things I can do. When
you don't even know."

Temel smiled a little.

"…Not knowing doesn't mean I can't do anything. I said it before,
didn't I. Not knowing isn't a reason not to write it."

*In that moment I felt I understood why that line had stayed with me
so long, inside me. This man truly didn't know. And not knowing, he
wrote his name every single time. Not knowing whether there was a
meaning or not, he was doing the thing he could do.*

I brought up review again. I'd remembered the day I'd followed him
through the confirmation rooms at the market.

"…What I saw that day, at the confirmation room. Is that still going
on?"

"…Yeah. There was one today too, a little while ago."

"…Do they call you in even before you leave?"

"…Yeah. Leaving needs confirming too. A departure notice, I think
they call it."

"…Do you write your name for that too?"

"…I do. There's another box to write in."

Hearing that, a laugh came out of me. It wasn't something to laugh
about, but I laughed anyway.

"…You really do write your name wherever you go."

"…I do. I get tired of myself sometimes, too."

"…And still you'll write it again."

"…Yeah. I'll write it again."

There wasn't a moment's hesitation in that answer. I found that
striking. I learned, right then, for the first time, that not
hesitating and being certain aren't the same thing.

---

"…Aren't you scared? Living like this all the time, when there might
be no meaning at all."

"…I am. But being certain there's no meaning scares me just as much.
Either way, being certain scares me more."

"…But there are plenty of people who are certain. Tyche too."

"…There are. But I don't envy that certainty so much as it scares me,
a little. Once you're certain, you don't have to look anymore after
that. You already think you know."

"…Do you dislike that?"

"…Not dislike it, exactly — I just can't seem to do it. I have to
look again, every time. It's tiring, but if I don't, I feel like I'll
miss something."

"…Miss what?"

"…I don't know that either. But if I let myself be certain, I'm
scared that even the not-knowing itself will stop being visible to
me."

"…How do you live without being certain?"

"…I just do today's work. Past that, I don't really know either."

I felt that this was similar to what Tyche had said, and also
different. Tyche too had said she lived liking what she did today.
But Tyche set that on top of a conclusion of "there isn't one," and
Temel was setting it on top of a conclusion of "I don't know."

*I couldn't explain that difference exactly, that night. In the next
chapter, I think about it for a long time.*

---

"…Hora."

"…Yes."

"…What I said before, about you sitting here maybe just because of a
number. I didn't say that to comfort you. I meant it could really be
true."

"…I know."

"…But the sentence after that is just as true. There are still things
you can do in a seat you got only because of a number. That isn't
comfort either. It's just a fact."

I sat a long while with those two sentences set side by side. That it
might be only a number, and that there were still things you could do
even so. They weren't opposites. Both could be true.

The day had fully gone dark. I said goodbye and stood.

"…You're going day after tomorrow?"

"…Yeah."

"…Will I get to see you again?"

Temel thought a moment and said,

"…I don't know. I don't know that either."

Strangely, that didn't make me sad. I smiled and turned away.

---

On the way home, I took the long way on purpose. The road that passed
the western water channel.

The water was still making its sound. I stood beside it a moment and
brought back the words I'd heard that day.

*Maybe you're sitting here only because of a number.*

*But there are still things you can do in a seat you got only because
of a number.*

*I don't know. I don't know either.*

Set side by side like this, the three sentences strangely sounded like
one. Clearer put together than they had been, heard one at a time.

*I copied these three sentences onto paper that night. I put them in
the drawer with my mother's papers, alongside the two names and the
one sentence already there. That drawer had grown fairly heavy by
then. There was a weight I felt in my hand, every time I opened it.*

My aunt was still awake when I got home.

"…You're late."

"…Yes. I went to see Temel."

"…He's leaving day after tomorrow, you said."

"…Yes."

"…What did you ask him?"

"…Why I ended up in this seat."

My aunt looked at me a moment and asked,

"…Did you get an answer?"

"…No. He said he doesn't know either."

My aunt gave a short laugh.

"…Why ask something he doesn't know?"

"…You have to ask, to find out whether he knows or not."

My aunt didn't answer that. She just turned off the light. I lay in
the dark and read those three sentences over once more, inside myself.

*I learned that night, for the first time, how long the words "I don't
know" could hold onto a person. Tyche's "there isn't one," and Temel's
"I don't know." Both short sentences, but they settled inside me at
entirely different weights. Why they weighed differently, I couldn't
explain, then.*
