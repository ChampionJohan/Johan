# Chapter 15. The Summary Was Accurate

> _Previously_ — Hora read the Seventh Classification deliberation to the end. Four hundred ten of the eleven hundred forty were being relocated to land that did not yet exist, and once the procedure was complete, the classification itself would be deleted from the roster. None of this appeared anywhere in the summary.

The next morning I took out the summary again and laid it side by side with the full text.

*I want to set down this act of comparison precisely. This will likely be the most tedious chapter in this book. I know that. But without this tedious comparison, I would have gone on to misunderstand everything that happened next. So I write it out plainly, tedious or not.*

*I spent the entire day on this comparison. The other members had already moved on to the next matter. I was the only one still sitting on the Seventh Classification case. No one told me that was strange.*

---

The summary's first sentence read:

*The Seventh Classification (unregistered residents) residential clearance and relocation matter.*

Identical to the document name on the cover of the full text. Not one character different.

The second sentence.

*Subject: 1,140 people (7-001 to 7-1140).*

I counted the roster. Exactly eleven hundred forty. The numbers matched too.

The third sentence.

*Reason: A need for consolidated clearance has arisen from the accumulation of unregistered residences.*

I checked it against the background section of the full text. The same phrasing appeared there, unchanged. The circular structure I'd read — not registered because it never came up as an agenda item, not managed because not registered — compressed into this one sentence. It was compression, not distortion.

*Up to these three sentences, I already knew everything. I wasn't looking for something wrong here. What I really wanted to confirm was what came after.*

I looked at the third sentence once more. The phrase "a need... has arisen" caught at me. Arisen — as though it were weather, something that simply happened rather than something anyone made. I checked whether the full text used the same word. It did. The full text also said "arisen." The summary had simply carried over the full text's own phrasing.

*I sat with this one word for a long while. "A need has arisen" and "someone judged this to be necessary" are different in meaning. But this city's documents always used the former. I wondered whether that was a habit of this one document, or a habit of every document in this city. I confirmed later that it was the latter.*

---

The fourth sentence.

*Action: Residence recovery and relocation. Relocation support funds and moving assistance to be provided.*

This too was accurate. The support-fund clause, the moving-assistance clause, all exactly as they stood in the full text. I checked the support formula again. The summary had no formula, but the word "provided" was true. It hadn't claimed something existed that didn't.

The fifth sentence, the last.

*Implementation date: as specified in the timeline.*

I paused on this sentence. The full text had a concrete date. The summary only said "as specified in the timeline." I wondered whether the date had been hidden, and checked again — the date was right there in the full text, available to anyone who looked. The summary simply hadn't repeated it, since it was already in the full text.

*This was a common enough practice. A summary isn't meant to replace the full text — I'd learned before that it's meant to be a signpost pointing toward it. A signpost has no obligation to show everything at the destination.*

---

The summary was exactly five sentences. It fit within a page and a half.

I read the whole summary through again from the start. Not a single wrong sentence. Not a single exaggerated one. No missing numbers. Nothing like the support-fund matter left out when it should have been there, nothing like the number of people affected differing from the real count. None of that.

I took out a sheet of paper and copied the summary's five sentences on the left and the corresponding sections of the full text on the right. I drew lines connecting them and checked each pair one by one. When I finished, between the five lines on the left and the five paragraphs on the right, there was truly not one place where the meaning had shifted. The right side was simply longer.

*I kept this sheet of paper for a long time. I later used it as an attachment to my objection filing. Even now, writing this record, I keep it beside me. This comparison chart is the only thing in this entire book I can call "evidence." And what that evidence proves is only that no one did anything wrong.*

*I sat for a long while in front of that fact. I think I had wanted, somewhere, to feel deceived. Then I would have had someone to be angry at. But no matter how many times I compared them, there was no deception. The summary was accurate.*

---

I went to find Tyche and asked her.

"…Why is the summary so short?"

"…Summaries are always short. Everyone makes their judgment from it, so."

"…Then why doesn't it have the roster-deletion clause?"

Tyche took the summary and skimmed it.

"…That's a supplementary provision. Supplementary provisions don't usually go into the summary."

"…Why not?"

"…The body's action clauses all go into the summary, and supplementary provisions are about the implementation method for that action. Put every supplementary provision in, and the summary stops being a summary."

I heard that and couldn't say anything back. There was nothing logically wrong with it.

"…But this is an important clause. A whole classification is getting deleted."

Tyche looked at me for a moment.

"…Isn't it up to the reader to decide what's important? I'm not the one who decides what goes into the summary and what stays out."

"…Then who decides?"

"…The regulation decides. Five sentences, it says."

"…Who decided on five sentences?"

Tyche thought for a moment and said,

"…I don't know that either. It was already that way before I ever got here."

Something in that answer caught at me. The person who had written the rule for five sentences seemed to not be there anymore either. Only the rule remained.

"…So the summary being short means people end up judging things carelessly?"

"…Not carelessly — accurately. Because the summary is accurate. You just confirmed that yourself, didn't you? Nothing wrong, right?"

"…Right. Nothing wrong."

"…So what's the problem?"

I couldn't answer that question. *I left the room that day without an answer to it. It would take the remaining thirty-some chapters of this book to find my own answer to that question.*

*Hearing that answer, I knew Tyche hadn't meant it unkindly. She really had only followed the summary's form. Following a form couldn't be a crime.*

---

After leaving Tyche, I went to the archive and looked up a few more summaries from old small-scale clearance cases. All of them were similar. Five sentences — subject, reason, action, implementation timing. One case had involved seventeen people, and reading the summary, all seventeen appeared to have relocated to new residences with no particular trouble. I checked the full text — that really was the case. That case had no temporary designations like this one.

I looked at a few more besides that one. A thirty-two-person case, a nine-person case. All of them matched their summaries. I couldn't find a single case with anything like a temporary designation attached. It might have been because this Seventh Classification matter was especially large. Or it might simply have been bad luck.

*Doing these comparisons, I learned one thing. The summary wasn't the problem. The summary's form was always identical. It was just that, this time, the content that went into that form happened to be content in which four hundred ten people's place could not fit. The form was innocent. It was only that the gap between what the form needed to hold and what the form could hold happened, this time, to be unusually wide.*

I asked the archive clerk whether the summary being this short had ever caused a problem.

"…I couldn't say. I've never heard of anything like that."

"…Not even once?"

"…I've been here six years, and this is the first objection like this I've seen."

Hearing that, I felt, faintly, that I was doing something new. It wasn't a good feeling. New, in this city, generally meant unfamiliar, and unfamiliar generally meant uncomfortable.

---

I went to the reception desk and looked up the regulation for drafting summaries. In one corner of the regulation book was the guideline for writing them.

*The summary shall include: subject, reason, action, and implementation timing. As a rule, the summary shall not exceed five sentences.*

Five sentences. I looked at the number and understood. That this summary had come out to exactly five sentences wasn't a coincidence. The rule had set it that way.

*Curious why this rule had been made, I looked up the stated reason attached beside it. "Excessive length increases the review burden on members and causes delay in deliberation," it read. This too wasn't a rule made out of ill will. If anything, it had been made out of consideration. Because everyone was busy, because everyone had to process many matters, it had been made so people could read short and judge.*

*But between "short" and "less important," whoever wrote this rule had never drawn a line. The shorter something is, the simpler what remains looks. There was no place for four hundred ten people within five sentences. There being no place for them and their having been hidden were different things — and I was, for the first time, sitting with that difference in my hands.*

Beside the regulation book was a section on the summary-review procedure. Once a summary was drafted, it said, the department in charge confirmed it, and then it was distributed to the committee. The word "confirm" caught my eye, so I read that procedure closely. *Confirmation examines whether the summary is consistent with the full text. The length or emphasis of the summary is not subject to confirmation.* So confirmation checked only for accuracy. What was kept and what was cut lay outside the confirmation procedure.

I read that sentence twice. There was confirmation. Confirmation had done exactly what it was meant to do. It was only that what it was meant to do differed from what I had hoped it would do.

I also looked up which department handled the confirmation. It said that department confirmed dozens of summaries this way every week. Each confirmation probably took very little time. It wasn't the fault of the people in that department. They had been taught to look only at accuracy, and they had looked only at accuracy.

---

Leaving the archive, I thought again about the summary itself. A summary wasn't inherently a bad thing. I'd written many summaries myself — ration application summaries, school assignment summaries. Writing short wasn't laziness — it was a skill. A well-cut summary got more people to read, not fewer.

*The problem was that in this city, no one failed to read the summary. Everyone read the summary and nothing else. So what wasn't in the summary became the same as what no one knew. The summary being short wasn't the bad part — the bad part was that everyone reading only the summary meant the summary's gaps became the whole city's gap.*

*This wasn't the fault of the person who wrote the summary. It wasn't the fault of the people who read only the summary, either. Everyone had simply acted reasonably, each in their own way. And yet when all of those reasonable acts gathered together, a decision came out complete, with four hundred ten people missing from it.*

I came home and told my aunt about all this. She listened while getting dinner ready.

"…So no one lied. That's what you're saying."

"…Right. Really — no one."

"…Then why is that a problem?"

I couldn't answer right away. After a long moment I said,

"…I don't know. But something about it feels like a problem."

"…Even without knowing what the problem is?"

"…Right."

My aunt looked at me without laughing.

"…Hora. You're going to keep going like this until you figure it out. Aren't you."

I didn't answer. She knew that was the answer.

She ladled the soup and said,

"…I thought you'd found some huge wrongdoing. But hearing it, it sounds like everyone just did their own job."

"…Yeah. Seems like it."

"…Then isn't that just how it is? If everyone did only their own job and it turned out this way, that's nobody's fault."

I lifted my spoon and stopped.

"…But then who do those four hundred ten people even ask?"

My aunt didn't answer right away this time either. After a long while she said,

"…I don't know that one."

We ate the rest of the meal in silence for a while after that. *I still remember that silence. It wasn't that my aunt had gone quiet out of anger. She, too, like me, had gone quiet looking for an answer and not finding one. That I wasn't the only person in this city stuck in front of that question — I learned that for the first time that day.*

*I slept poorly that night too. Confirming that the summary wasn't a lie had, if anything, made me more frustrated. If someone had lied, I could have gone and confronted them. Since no one had lied, I had no one left to confront. All that remained was the feeling that something was strange.*

*I tried to give that feeling a name. It wasn't a sense of being wronged — that's a feeling you get when someone has done wrong to you, and no one had done wrong to me. It wasn't anger either — you can't be angry with no one to be angry at. If I had to name it, it was simply a feeling that something was empty. The very same feeling I got looking at the signature box.*

We cleared the table and I went to my room, where I pulled out the comparison chart again. Five lines on the left, five paragraphs on the right. However many times I looked, it stayed the same. I put that paper away in the drawer along with the notice. The same drawer that held my mother's papers.

*I decided the next day to file an objection. I already knew, from having read it, that there was a channel for filing one. I thought that if there was a procedure, following that procedure would be enough. How naive a thought that was, I would learn over the days that followed.*
