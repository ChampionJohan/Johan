# Chapter 40. If I Perish

> _Previously_ — Tyche asks, "If you write it, what changes?" Hora
> answers, "Maybe nothing changes. But it won't be empty." Tyche
> stays close, still unconvinced.

I slept well that night.

I know it's strange, even as I write this sentence. That I slept well
knowing what I would do the next day. But that's really how it was. I
lay down in bed and fell asleep not long after, and I didn't wake up in
the middle of the night.

*I've thought about this many times since. I write it knowing how
strange it sounds. I'd thought the night before a great decision was
supposed to be spent wide awake. At least, that's how it always goes in
stories. I didn't do that. Maybe it's because the sleepless night had
already come and gone before this. That night, back then. Maybe it had
all been used up then.*

*Just before falling asleep that night, I thought about one thing only.
What Temel had said to me. "Maybe you're sitting here only because of a
number. But there are still things you can do in a seat you got only
because of a number." I turned that over in my mind again and again.
Then I fell asleep.*

*Whether there was meaning in it or not, I still didn't know, even that
night. All I knew was that there were things I could do. That was
enough, I think I told myself, just before falling asleep.*

---

I got up in the morning and washed and dressed as usual.

My aunt was making rice in the kitchen. The same time as always, the
same smell as always. I sat at the table, and my aunt ladled out the
soup.

"…You're heading out early today."

"…Yeah."

My aunt didn't ask any further. I didn't say anything further either.

*That morning I'd thought I should tell my aunt something. That I
should let her know my decision. But I didn't. I opened my mouth to
speak and then just closed it again. Even now I can't explain exactly
why. Only that it felt right, that morning.*

*A declaration is for someone. It's made for the person listening.
That morning I felt there was no one for me to declare this to. Telling
my aunt wouldn't have let her do anything for me instead. The Consensus
isn't a structure that any one person can block or help. So saying it
would only have made my aunt carry a weight.*

*This wasn't out of not trusting my aunt. If anything it was out of
caring for her. Not telling her was the one kindness I could give her,
that morning.*

After eating, I got up from the table. My aunt looked at me once. Was
there something different in her eyes from usual — I didn't notice it,
at the time. Looking back now, I think maybe I did know.

"…I'm off."

"…All right."

That was all. Closing the door behind me, I didn't look back. I never
did, on any other day, so there was no reason to do it differently that
day.

---

On my way to the deliberation district, I passed the water channel.

The same channel I'd first seen at the very start. It now reaches deep
into the city. I wrote earlier that people had started drinking that
water; that morning too, a few people were sitting by the water's edge.

*I didn't stop walking as I passed it. I hesitated over this while
writing this chapter. It felt like something symbolic should have
happened here. The water speaking to me, or giving me some kind of
sign. Nothing like that happened. I just walked past, and the water
just flowed.*

*I'd been thinking about this water channel a lot, around that time.
Water that administratively doesn't exist, because it never came
before an agenda. And yet people drink it. It was fairly real, for
something that doesn't exist.*

*That morning I thought this water channel and what I was about to do
were headed in opposite directions. The channel kept flowing even
though no one had named it. The signature box stayed empty because no
one had put a name to it. One existed without an agenda; the other
looked, without one, as if it didn't exist. I didn't fully understand
that difference, that day.*

One of the people sitting by the water glanced at me. I looked back at
that person. Neither of us said anything. We just passed each other.

In my drawer at home was the second page of the work guidelines. I
brought it out with me. There'd been no real need to bring it — there
would be a fresh form at the office. But I brought that paper anyway.

*I don't know why. Maybe it was because it was the same paper I'd
rubbed with my fingertip, that night. Or maybe it was just habit.*

---

The office was the same as always.

A few people had already come in. No one looked at me any differently.
I sat at my seat and checked the documents I had to handle that day. The
same as usual.

*I worked as usual all through that morning. Whoever reads this chapter
will probably think I went straight to the signature box. I didn't. In
the morning I handled the other deliberations assigned to me. Putting
them in order, passing them on, confirming them. I have to admit, in
this chapter, that I too was a person who used that grammar.*

*This fact bothered me strangely. What I was about to do soon after was
break that grammar. And yet that morning I still worked exactly by it.
I put things in order, I passed them on, I confirmed them. I don't
want to call this hypocrisy. It was simply how it was. A person can't
just stop doing everything they do every day inside a system, on a
single day. You have to eat, and the work assigned to you has to be
handled.*

*I thought, that day, that it must have been the same for the
Secretary. He too lives speaking the same grammar every day. I
understood, for the first time that day, from his position, that a
person can't shed that grammar overnight.*

Tyche came at lunchtime. She sat beside me as usual.

"…You're a little different today."

"…How so?"

"…I don't know. Just a little quiet."

I didn't answer. She didn't ask again either.

We ate lunch together that day. We didn't talk about anything special.
Tyche talked a little about the materials she'd organized the day
before, and I just listened.

*I remember that lunch exactly, even now. What Tyche ate, what she
talked about. Even though it was such an ordinary lunch. Probably
because it was the last lunch I spent as an ordinary person. From that
afternoon on, I became a slightly different person.*

---

In the afternoon I took out the Seventh Classification document.

I opened the last page. For the twenty-some-th time. The signature box
was there.

*I've thought for a long time about how to write this moment. There was
a temptation to write it dramatically. My hands trembling, my heart
racing. To tell the truth, none of that happened. My hands didn't
tremble. My heart might have beaten a little faster than usual, but
that might just be me dressing up the memory now.*

I flipped through the whole document once more. Over forty pages. The
summary, the evidence, the record, the notes of those involved. Even
reading it for the twenty-some-th time, my eyes still followed the
same order.

*Writing this chapter, I thought about what was different about this
twenty-some-th complete reading from the ones before it. Nothing about
the content had changed. The summary was the same, the evidence was
the same. What had changed was only that I knew, this time, what I was
about to do while reading it. Before, I'd read it to understand it.
This time it felt like reading it to say goodbye to it.*

*Goodbye might sound like too strong a word. But that's really how I
felt that day. I was about to part with myself as a person who simply
read this document. Now I was about to become a person who had put a
hand to it.*

I looked at that box once more. Still smooth. Still empty.

*I still didn't know what would happen if I filled it. I knew about
Temel's review, and I knew the story of that person eleven years
before. But exactly what would happen to me, I didn't know. That not
knowing is the most honest fact of that day.*

---

I didn't pick up the pen.

Instead I got up from my seat and left the office. I didn't fill it in,
that day.

*I need to write this part honestly. That afternoon I didn't steel
myself and pick up the pen right away. I got up from my seat and went
out. And I walked. I walked with nowhere set in mind.*

I walked once around the city. It was a familiar route. A route I'd
walked since I was small. My pace was neither fast nor slow.

I passed the First Deliberation District meeting room. The building
that held the seat I'd first sat in, once. I didn't stop walking — I
just passed it by.

I passed the school too. I remembered my aunt saying, "What matters is
near the front." That day the words sounded different to me. I didn't
think my aunt had been wrong. Only that this time, it wasn't near the
front — it was at the end.

*I walked for nearly two hours. I wasn't walking to decide anything in
particular. I just walked. As I walked, I took in every corner of this
city with my eyes. Why that was necessary to me at the time, I still
can't fully explain. Only that it was necessary.*

*A city where no one queues. A city where the word favor is only used
when handing someone an object. I still loved this city. Loving it
still, and knowing that one of its most honest boxes stayed empty the
whole time — I knew both at once.*

*The two didn't contradict each other. I realized that as I walked.
Loving this city, and filling this box, were things pointing in the
same direction.*

Walking, I thought of Temel. He wasn't in the city right now. I'd heard
he'd gone elsewhere to look for someone. I thought it was fortunate,
actually, that he wasn't there.

*If he had been, I probably would have gone to find him and asked. Is
it all right to do this now, is this the right time. Temel probably
would have said, "I don't know. I don't know either." He'd already
answered that way once before. Rather than hear the same answer again,
I preferred to walk alone.*

*This wasn't avoiding Temel. I simply wanted, this once, to go without
anyone's confirmation. If you ask before you go, it becomes going with
that person's permission. I didn't want permission. That there was no
one, to begin with, whose permission I needed — that was something this
whole book had taught me.*

I thought of the Secretary too. He would probably be there tomorrow
morning, in the seat where he'd receive my signature. He wouldn't be
surprised. I was strangely certain of that.

---

By the time the sun was setting, I came back to the office.

Most people had already left for the day. Tyche wasn't there either. I
sat alone at my seat.

Outside the window the sun was fully going down. The evenings of this
city are quiet. As I've said, it's a city with little reason to make a
sound.

*I didn't go through some grand moment of resolve, that evening. The
decision hadn't arrived in a single instant. From back then to here, it
had built up little by little, over four days. Writing this chapter, I
kept telling myself I had to resist the temptation to turn it into one
dramatic scene.*

I opened the document again. I looked at the signature box. This time I
didn't look at it for long.

I picked up the pen.

The pen in my hand was an ordinary pen. One of those common pens found
anywhere in an office, nothing special about it. I found that a little
funny. A moment like this felt like it should call for some special
instrument, and what was in my hand was just an ordinary pen.

*I brought the nib close to the paper. It didn't touch. In those few
millimeters, I stayed still for a long time. I don't remember now
whether it was seconds, or minutes. Time felt strangely stretched out,
in that moment.*

*In that moment I thought of my mother. The last line of the twelve-
page paper. And I thought that this signature box, too, was in the same
way a last line. A box at the very bottom of the document, in the place
where everything ends. I had missed a last line once. This time I was
about to leave something on a last line, myself.*

---

*I didn't sign it that night. That night I only picked up the pen and
set it back down. I think it's right to end this chapter here. The
signature itself belongs to a much later part.*

*What I was trying to do that day wasn't the signature. It was the
decision. And a decision, from what I learned that day, wasn't a
declaration. It was simply going, the next morning.*

That night, at home, I said nothing to my aunt. I ate, washed, and
slept. The same as usual. My aunt didn't say anything unusual either.

*It wasn't that I pretended it was the same as usual. It really was the
same as usual. I wasn't afraid, that night. I know that's the strangest
part of this chapter. The fear came the next day.*

*I told Tyche this once, later. She said that was only natural. That
the time before a decision is the most frightening, and once it's made,
it's actually less frightening. That the real thing only starts after
that. Tyche was right. The reason I was at ease that night was that
nothing had started yet.*

*Between picking up the pen and putting it down, and actually writing
the name, there was still one more door left. I spent that last night
on this side of that door.*

*I gave this chapter its title later. I hadn't thought of these words
that night. This is a title I attached after the fact, while writing
this book. That night's self had no room to think anything so grand. I
was only thinking about going, the next morning.*

The next morning, I woke as usual. And I left the house as usual. The
weather was clear, and the sound of the water channel was exactly the
same as the day before. This city stayed the same whatever decision I'd
made. I found that steadying, if anything. Nothing was standing still,
waiting for me.

*I slept well that night. I set this down honestly too. I didn't toss
and turn, and I had no frightening dreams. I woke as usual in the
morning. I learned, then, that the night before a great day could be
this ordinary.*
