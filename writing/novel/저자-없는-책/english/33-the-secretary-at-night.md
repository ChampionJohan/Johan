# Chapter 33. The Secretary at Night

> _Previously_ — For days I held onto Tyche's "there isn't one" and
> Temel's "I don't know." No conclusion came. And Friday night,
> something happened for the first time. The Secretary came to find me
> after working hours had ended.

The Secretary was standing in the alley in front of my house. At
first I didn't recognize him. He wasn't wearing what he always wore
in the district meeting hall. He had on an outer coat, and his hands
held nothing at all — not the usual folder. That was the first time
I'd ever seen the Secretary with empty hands.

"…Secretary?"

"…I'm sorry, at this hour."

His voice was the same. The tone I always heard. Words of apology
that still didn't sound like an apology. I found it strange to
realize that his way of speaking stayed the same even outside working
hours.

"…What brings you here, this time of night?"

"…I was hoping you might have a moment."

"…How did you know where I live?"

"…It's on the roster."

That answer was so obvious it was strange, hearing it. In that moment
I felt again how much this city's roster held. No one had hidden it.
Everyone had simply never thought to open that roster and look.

*Writing this now, I notice this short exchange already summarizes
this whole chapter. What surprised me wasn't that the Secretary had
found something out. It had been somewhere visible from the start.
What surprised me was that no one but me had ever gone to look.*

---

We sat on the bench at the mouth of the alley. It was a different
bench from the one I'd sat on with Temel, but for a moment the two
nights overlapped in my mind. There was one streetlamp, and its light
was yellow.

The Secretary was silent for a long while. I didn't press him. I'd
watched, for months, how this man carried silence. He wasn't someone
who hurried. Not so much that he didn't hurry — he seemed like someone
who had no reason to.

"…This is the first time you've come to find me."

"…Yes."

"…There's something you want to say?"

"…Yes."

The Secretary answered only that, and went quiet again. I didn't think
this man was hiding anything. If there was one thing I'd learned in
months in this city, it was that these people didn't hide things. It
was only that they were slow to bring things out.

---

"…Last time, I looked away."

This came first. I was surprised. It was the first time the Secretary
had brought that moment up himself.

"…Yes. I remember."

"…What you asked me that day was what happens if you write a name."

"…Yes."

"…I couldn't give you an answer then. It wasn't that I didn't know the
answer."

Hearing that, I asked right back.

"…Then why?"

The Secretary looked at the night sky for a moment. I expected
something grand to come next. What came out was short.

"…If I gave you an answer, it would become my saying it."

"…What do you mean?"

"…If I say I know something, from then on it becomes my judgment. I'm
not someone who judges. I'm someone who passes things on and convenes
them."

---

*Hearing this answer, a line I'd heard once before came back to me.
"…How do I put in something that isn't there?" That there was no
subject. I'd thought that was an excuse, at the time. But that night I
felt again that it wasn't an excuse. This man truly was afraid of
attaching a subject to himself. Not so much afraid — he sincerely
believed that wasn't his to do.*

"…But why did you come today, then? You didn't come here not to
answer, surely."

At this question, the Secretary looked straight at me for the first
time.

"…I felt this was something I ought to tell you."

"…Tell me what?"

"…That matter from eleven years ago. The southern tract."

For a moment I felt my breath catch. It was the person I'd found —
the one who had written a name, opened a new deliberation, and whose
record had then stopped.

---

"…How do you know about that matter?"

"…I convened that one too."

That one sentence was the whole of that night. I couldn't say
anything for a long while, hearing it. It meant the Secretary had
been standing in the same seat, doing the same work, eleven years ago
too.

"…Were you the Secretary then too?"

"…I did similar work. The title was different from now."

"…Then do you remember that person? The one who wrote the name?"

The Secretary paused, very briefly, at this question. I thought that
brief pause said something, and then set the thought aside. This man
hadn't looked away, and he wasn't choosing his words. It was simply a
face confirming a fact.

"…I remember."

"…What kind of person were they?"

"…A subject of convening. Beyond that, there's nothing I can tell
you."

"…What about what became of them?"

"…I don't know."

That answer was short. But being short didn't make it sound like
evasion. I could tell this man truly didn't know. If there was one
more thing I'd learned, living months in this city, it was that
someone who truly doesn't know speaks with a different tone from
someone pretending not to know while knowing.

"…How long have you worked here, Secretary?"

"…Twenty-three years now."

"…Twenty-three years?"

"…Yes. I came in as an intake assistant, at first. And worked my way
up to this seat."

"…Have you been in this city the whole time?"

"…Yes. Never moved."

Hearing that number, I tried to gauge how long, how many convenings,
this man must have carried out in this city. I couldn't quite gauge
it. Twenty-three years was longer than I'd been alive.

"…So how many people have you convened here?"

"…I've never counted. It isn't a number that can be counted."

"…But there were exactly two people who wrote their names. The person
from eleven years ago, and Temel now."

The Secretary nodded.

"…That's right. Two, in twenty-three years."

That number held me for a long while. Two, in twenty-three years.
Between those two people lay a gap of eleven years. Through that gap,
this city had kept turning, and the signature box had stayed blank the
whole time.

---

"…Secretary, was that person put under review too? Like Temel is
now?"

"…Yes. Several matters' worth."

"…For how long?"

"…What remains on record is about half a year."

"…And after that?"

"…They dropped off the convening roster."

"…Why?"

"…I don't know. What I know is that from a certain point on, that
name was no longer on the convening list."

"…Secretary — could that still happen now, if someone wrote their
name?"

The Secretary answered this question right away.

"…I don't know. I don't know what happened to that person, so how
could I know whether the next one would end up the same way."

"…But the review keeps going. That much is certain, isn't it?"

"…Yes, that's certain. Once a new deliberation is opened, the
proposer's name attaches to that matter. As long as that matter's
alive, that name gets called into every related confirmation. This is
in the regulations."

"…You said it isn't punishment."

"…It isn't punishment. It's confirmation. But when the confirmation
never ends, I sometimes get confused, myself, about how that's
different from punishment."

*I was surprised to hear this. It was the first time the Secretary had
said he himself was confused. And even "confused" came with the
subject "myself" attached to it. That night, this was one of only a
handful of times he'd used "I" or "myself" at all.*

"…So is opening a new deliberation a good thing or a bad thing?"

"…That isn't a question I can answer. The result could be good, or it
could be bad. You only find out once it's opened."

"…What do you think, Secretary?"

"…I'm not in a position to offer an opinion."

"…This isn't working hours, though."

The Secretary paused a moment at this.

"…That's true. But even this seat is, in the end, tied to the work I
do. I can't fully step away from it."

Hearing that answer, I had no choice but to accept that this man
truly knew nothing more than this. The Consensus hides nothing. The
Secretary hid nothing either. It was only that there were things even
the Consensus didn't know, things even the Secretary didn't know, and
probably things no one in this city knew.

*In that moment I felt something hollow. I think I'd half been hoping
this man would finally unburden some hidden truth he'd been carrying.
But there was nothing like that. He truly said only as much as he
knew, and said "I don't know" for what he didn't. I only confirmed,
that day, all over again, that this was this city's truly frightening
part.*

---

"…Secretary, back then, what did you think, watching that person
write their name?"

The Secretary looked at me a moment at this question. His expression
didn't change much.

"…I thought it was unusual."

"…That's all?"

"…At the time. As the convening matters kept growing, later on I had
a few other thoughts too."

"…What thoughts?"

"…Why this person kept coming, I wondered. They came, every time, to
a seat they didn't have to come to."

"…You were the one calling them in."

"…Yes. I convened them. But being convened doesn't mean everyone
comes. There are people who don't come."

"…What happens if they don't?"

"…Processing gets delayed. That's all. That isn't punishment either."

"…But this person came, every time?"

"…Yes. Never once missed. Right up to the last convening."

---

Hearing that, I asked again.

"…The last convening? Did you have any sense, then, of what might
happen to that person?"

"…No. That convening was an ordinary one too. As far as I knew."

"…But after that, they were gone."

"…Yes."

"…Doesn't that seem strange to you?"

At this question, the Secretary was quiet a little longer than
before, for the first time.

"…It's strange. But it being strange doesn't mean there's anything I
can do."

I couldn't answer that. The Secretary didn't apologize. He didn't say
he'd done anything wrong, either. He was simply drawing, precisely,
the line between what he knew and what he could do.

---

"…Why did you tell me this tonight?"

The Secretary answered as he rose from the bench.

"…I received a report that you'd found that document. Reading room
records are kept. If I was connected to it, I thought it was right to
tell you myself."

"…You received a report? From whom?"

"…It comes up periodically from the reading room. Who has read a
document carrying a particular mark."

*Hearing that answer, a small chill went through me. But that wasn't
hidden either. That reading records were kept had been an open fact
from the start. I simply hadn't known how they were passed on, or to
whom. Not knowing had been on my side.*

"…So that's just procedure too, then?"

"…Yes. Procedure."

"…So you came to find me because of procedure, Secretary?"

At this question, the Secretary's expression shifted, slightly, for
the first time. It wasn't a smile. But it wasn't quite blank, either.

"…No. This isn't procedure. I came because I thought I should."

---

That one line stayed with me the longest of anything I heard that
night. That it wasn't procedure. Even as he said it, the word "I"
sounded awkward in his mouth. Like clothes he wasn't used to wearing.

"…Could you come like this again, next time?"

The Secretary thought a moment.

"…I don't know. I suppose that will be decided when the time comes."

With that, the Secretary said goodbye and turned back. He never did
apologize. I'd thought he had something to apologize for. But instead
of an apology, he'd given me a fact. I learned, for the first time
that night, that a fact can weigh more than an apology.

*I remember this night for a long time. I can't write that I came to
like this man. I can't quite write that I came not to dislike him,
either. But this — that he'd carried out the same work, in the same
seat, for eleven years running, and that in all those eleven years, he
had come to find me exactly once with a step that wasn't procedure —
these two facts set side by side, I still don't know how to put them
together.*

The next morning, I told Tyche briefly what had happened the night
before. Tyche listened quietly and said,

"…He told you that, himself?"

"…Yes."

"…That's surprising. I didn't think he'd say that much."

"…Do you not know the Secretary well either, Tyche?"

"…I've worked with him for years and I still don't. He's always in
that seat, but nobody seems to know what's behind it."

"…Is that strange?"

"…No. There are a lot of people like that in this city. The longer
they've worked, the more so. I think erasing yourself becomes a
habit."

"…Doesn't that make you sad, Tyche?"

Tyche thought a moment and said,

"…Not sad, exactly. I'm scared, sometimes, that I might become that
way too."

That was unexpected. I'd never thought Tyche carried a fear like
that.

"…You're afraid you'll end up like him?"

"…Yes. I like procedure. But if I like procedure for too long, I'm
scared that someday I'll be someone speaking without a subject too,
just like him."

"…You don't seem like you would, to me."

"…I don't know. He probably wasn't like that from the start, either."

With that, Tyche went back to sorting papers. I watched her back for a
moment, then thought of the seat the Secretary must have stood in for
twenty-three years.

Walking home, I looked up at the western sky once. I had no idea, that
evening, that I would soon hear about a sound coming from the western
water channel, different from its usual sound.
