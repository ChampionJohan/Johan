# Chapter 41. The Signature

> _Previously_ — Hora doesn't declare her decision. Carrying a resolve
> that built up slowly over more than three days, she meets an ordinary
> morning and leaves the house with an ordinary step.

I got to the office early that morning. A little earlier than usual.

No one was there yet. The hallway was quiet, and so was the meeting
room. I sat at my seat and took out the Seventh Classification document.
I opened the last page. The signature box was there.

*I hesitated for a long while before writing this chapter. I didn't
know how to properly carry the weight of this moment onto the page. In
the end I decided to just write it exactly as it happened. Not to
invent anything to add weight to it. That's my rule for writing this
chapter.*

The office was quiet. It wasn't yet fully light out. A pale light was
coming in through the window.

I looked through the document once more. It was habit. I didn't flip
through all forty-some pages again. I just checked the first page and
the last. The summary was there, and the signature box was there.

*Even up to this moment I felt there was still something left to check,
one last time. Whether the evidence was right, whether the procedure
was right. In fact it wasn't that. All of it I'd already checked, weeks
before. What I really wanted to check that morning was whether my mind
was still the same.*

*It was.*

---

I picked up the pen. The same pen I'd picked up the evening before.

This time I didn't stop.

I wrote my name in the name box. Hora. Two syllables. The sound of the
pen tip touching the paper was exactly the same as any ordinary time I
wrote.

*That was really all there was. Even as I write this sentence I feel
the temptation to add something more. That my hand trembled, that I
cried. There was nothing. I simply wrote my name. The same motion of
the hand as any ordinary time I wrote my name on an application.*

*I once tried to time how long it took me to write these two syllables.
Probably two, three seconds. Two hundred-some days of time, three days
of resolve, four days of preparation, all ended in a three-second
motion of the hand. I thought for a long time that this imbalance was
strange. I don't think it's strange now. A great decision didn't need
to be expressed by a great motion.*

---

Once I'd written it, I looked at the box.

It wasn't empty anymore.

*Had I expected something to happen, at this moment? Looking back now,
I think maybe I had. A bell ringing somewhere, or someone rushing in,
or at the very least the air in the room changing. None of that
happened. The room stayed the same. The light outside the window
stayed the same. The document was just a document.*

I sat for a moment and kept looking at the box. I watched the ink dry.
It took a few minutes.

Above my name, the other boxes came into view too. The date box, the
agenda number box. All boxes that had already been printed. The
signature box was the only one that had been filled by hand.

*That contrast bothered me. The printed letters had been made by the
Consensus, and the two written syllables had been made by me. On that
paper, for the first time, something that wasn't the Consensus's had
come to exist. Very small, but clearly different.*

---

I heard the sound of a door opening. It was the caretaker. He'd come to
do the morning cleaning.

"…You're in early."

"…Yes."

The caretaker walked past without much thought. He probably didn't know
what I'd done. Even if he had, he probably would have walked past just
the same.

*That moment was strangely comforting to me. That no one was looking at
me as anything special. I felt as though the world had ended, and yet
in the caretaker's unconcerned footsteps I confirmed that the world
hadn't ended at all.*

The caretaker finished cleaning and left. I stayed sitting at my seat
the whole time. The document lay on the desk.

*I don't remember exactly what I did during those tens of minutes. I
think I was in a daze. I wasn't deep in thought, and I wasn't anxious
either. Time just passed. All I remember is that the light outside the
window grew a little brighter.*

*I could have left this blank space out of the writing. But I think
this blank space matters in this chapter. Right after signing,
nothing happens. Truly nothing happens, so much that I don't even
remember what filled that time. I mean to leave this blank space as
it is, without inventing anything to fill it.*

---

When it was time for the reception desk to open, I gathered up the
document and went.

The person at the reception desk greeted me with their usual face. I
held the document out.

"…Please receive this."

The person at the desk took the document and looked it over. They
flipped through a few pages, and their hand stopped at the last one.

*I'd waited a long time for this moment. I thought this person would
say something. Be surprised, or ask a question. Nothing was said.*

The person at the desk stamped it. Then they put the document in a
file box.

"…Received."

"…Yes. Thank you."

The person at the desk tore off a receipt slip and handed it to me. It
had a date and a number written on it.

"…Keep this with you."

"…Yes."

That was all.

*I've thought many times, later, about how many more cases that person
at the desk must have processed that day. Probably several. Mine was
just one of them. There was no reason, from that person's side, for it
to be anything special. Even if it was the first time they'd seen a
document with the signature box filled, the reception procedure was
the same. Take it, check it, stamp it, file it.*

*I tried to recall again whether that person's expression had changed
at all, seeing the signature box. I don't remember. Probably there
was no expression at all. That person, too, was only doing their job.*

*I don't even know that person's name. I didn't ask. In this city, you
don't often ask such things. That person remains, to me, simply
someone who was sitting at the reception desk. And I probably remain,
in that person's memory too, simply one of the people who came in that
morning.*

---

*I gave this chapter the title "The Signature." It's the shortest
chapter in this book, coming after the longest preparation. I did that
on purpose. I know the reader will expect something dramatic at this
moment. I expected it too. There was none.*

*The weight of this chapter comes from the fact that nothing happened.
I rewrote it several times. Each time there was a temptation to add
something. To lay in description like background music, or to write as
if someone had been watching me. I decided against it. Because none of
that actually happened.*

*I've written many times that this city doesn't lie to me. That
principle held in this chapter too. The Consensus showed no special
reaction to my signature. That not reacting was itself an honest
reaction. Not ignoring it to hide something — simply that no such
reaction existed in the reception procedure to begin with.*

*Walking out of the reception desk that day, I half expected some kind
of murmur to rise up behind my back. There was none. The reception desk
was as quiet as it had been when I'd walked in.*

---

I left the reception desk and went back to the office. An ordinary day
went on as usual. I handled the deliberations assigned to me. I put
things in order, passed them on, confirmed them.

Tyche came at lunchtime.

"…Quiet again today."

Instead of answering, I showed her the receipt slip. She looked at it
for a long while.

"…Did you write it?"

"…Yeah."

Tyche didn't say anything. She just handed the receipt back to me.

"…Let's go eat."

We went to eat as usual.

At the restaurant, Tyche asked again.

"…Weren't you scared? While you were writing it."

"…No, strangely, I felt nothing at all."

"…Why not?"

"…It was just two syllables of a name. I write that all the time
anyway."

Tyche thought about that for a moment and nodded.

"…That's stranger. That such a big decision ends with such a small
motion."

"…I think so too."

We ate the rest of our meal without much more talk. The restaurant was
crowded with people as usual, and no one paid us any attention.

---

*I know this scene is strange. A person in this city had just written
her own name in the signature box, and right after, the scene was an
ordinary lunch. Tyche and I walked as usual, ate as usual. Tyche talked
a little about her own things, as usual.*

*Even by that evening, I felt no sign that anything had changed. Nor
did the next day. Nor the day after that. I woke every morning a
little tense, wondering if something had changed. But every morning
was the same as before.*

*The change comes much later. It comes quietly, without warning. It
wasn't there that day. On that day there was only the fact that one
sheet of paper had been received.*

In the afternoon I ran into someone from another deliberation district.
That person treated me as they always had.

"…Hora, could you look this over for me."

"…Yes."

I took the document they handed me and checked it. It was my usual
work, and my hand moved as it always did. That person seemed to have
no idea at all what I'd just done.

*This was, strangely, a comfort. What I'd done wasn't yet special news
to anyone in this city. For it to become news would take time. Or
maybe, in fact, it would never become news at all. That the document
had been received was itself an open fact, but there was no way to know
it unless someone deliberately went looking.*

*This city hides nothing. And yet it also announces nothing. Hiding
something and not announcing it are different things. I felt that
difference again that afternoon.*

---

That night, at home, I told my aunt.

"…I wrote it."

My aunt stopped ladling the rice. She looked at me for a moment.

"…All right."

"…Aren't you angry?"

"…This isn't something to be angry about."

She went back to serving the rice. We ate dinner as usual.

*I only understood, later, what that calm of my aunt's had been. She
hadn't been unsurprised. She must have already been expecting this
moment, for many nights before. Her calm was a prepared calm.*

Once she'd finished serving, my aunt added just one more line.

"…Do you know what happens next?"

"…No, I don't."

"…Neither do I."

We didn't talk about it any further after that. We talked about other
things over dinner. My aunt talked about a tree the neighbors had
planted in their yard lately, and I listened.

*I've replayed that evening many times, wanting to remember it exactly.
My aunt didn't show any sign of worrying about me. I didn't say
anything to reassure her either. We just ate dinner as usual. I still
think, even now, that this was the best thing the two of us could give
each other.*

---

That night, before bed, I looked at the receipt slip again.

It had only a date, a number, and the stamp confirming receipt on it.
An unremarkable piece of paper.

*I held that paper in my hand for a long while. It didn't feel like I
was holding anything grand. It was just paper. But that ordinariness
was exactly what I learned that day. A thing that changes the world
comes in a shape the world doesn't notice. At least, in this city, it
did.*

I turned off the light and lay down. Nothing happened. The sound of the
water channel drifted in very faintly through the window, and faded
before I fell asleep.

*I mean to close this chapter with that night. Writing this part, I
kept wanting to give the reader something more. A twist, a tension, at
least some sense of foreboding. But there was none of that, that
night. I simply fell asleep, ordinarily.*

*Looking back on it now, this ordinariness was the most honest face
this city has. The Consensus wasn't waiting to punish me. Nor was it
waiting to celebrate me. The Consensus had simply received it. What
happened to what was received would, in this city's own way, begin
quietly, following a set procedure.*

*I had no idea, that night, what shape that procedure would take. If I
had known, I probably wouldn't have fallen asleep so peacefully.*

---

The next morning, too, I woke as usual. I went to the office and
worked as usual. No one treated me any differently.

This went on for several days. I came to work every day, put documents
in order every day, ate lunch with Tyche every day. I kept the receipt
slip in the drawer. Beside the second page of the work guidelines,
beside my mother's paper.

*The three papers lay side by side like that. I would sometimes open
that drawer and look at all three together. All three were papers with
something written on a last line. One a line I'd missed. One a box no
one had ever filled. And now one a box I had filled.*

*I received no word at all, during those days. Nothing happened at
all. I didn't think it would last long. I just didn't know exactly
when it would end. I didn't know exactly how fast the Consensus moved.
It could be quick, or it could be slow. There was no way to know.*

I enjoyed that quiet exactly as it was. Because I knew it was the last
quiet.

Tyche still came as usual, during those days. She kept asking me if
anything had changed, and every time I answered no. It really was
nothing.

"…That's strange. It feels like something should have happened by
now."

"…I thought so too. But there's nothing."

"…Could there just keep being nothing?"

"…I don't know."

We spent several days like that. I didn't know, at the time, that
those few days were the last peaceful time I'd spend in this city. If
I had known, I probably would have tried harder to feel that peace a
little longer.
