# Chapter 46. A Document with a Name on It

> _Previously_ — Tyche asked, "…I suppose my name meaning that is a
> coincidence too. Isn't it?" And left without waiting for an answer.
> Hora, in the end, said nothing at all. A few days later, the two of
> them were back at work as usual.

About a month after Tyche left without waiting for any answer, the
reading room clerk called me over one day.

"…Hora, could you take a look at this?"

What the clerk held out was an official document from another city. It
was an exchange record between deliberation districts, the kind of
thing that circulated occasionally. This one was marked as a case study
in administrative procedure improvement.

"…What about it?"

"…Your name's in the signature box here, Hora."

---

I took the document from her right there.

It bore another city's name. Somewhere I'd never been. At the very
bottom of the document, in the signature box, was written "Hora." The
handwriting wasn't mine. The stroke order was different, and so was
the angle of the strokes.

"…I didn't write this."

"…What? Then who—"

"…I don't know. I've never even been to that city."

The clerk checked the screen.

"…The receipt date was three months ago. You were here then, weren't
you?"

"…Yes. I was here, undergoing review, at that time."

"…Then it's definitely not you."

"…Yes, definitely not me."

*I felt something cold pass through me, in that moment. It wasn't quite
the same as fear. More than that, it was simply strange to me that my
own name had been written somewhere I knew nothing about.*

I studied that handwriting for a long while. The strokes were long and
straight. My own writing tends to be short and a little crooked. My
aunt always said my writing looked rushed. This handwriting wasn't
rushed. It was neat, careful, as if it had been practiced.

*That neatness bothered me strangely. When I'd written my own name
before, I'd just written it the way I write anything, ordinarily. That
was the moment I wrote took about three seconds. This person seemed to
have written those two syllables far more carefully, far more slowly
than I had. I tried, for a moment, to imagine what that person must
have felt, sitting in front of that box. The imagining didn't last
long. I knew too little.*

---

The clerk didn't take it very seriously.

"…Probably just someone with the same name. There are a few other
Horas in this city too."

"…There are?"

"…Yes, it's not a common name, but it's not so rare either."

I nodded at that explanation. It sounded plausible. The same name,
different person. There are plenty of people in the world who share a
name. I let it go, that day.

*Writing this chapter, I don't regret not pressing further, that day.
Even if I had, no answer probably would have come of it. I simply
want to set down exactly what happened that day.*

That night I read the contents of the document again. Before, I'd
only skimmed the result and moved on; this time I read it to the end.
It was habit.

The content concerned a registration reexamination. In that city too,
there had been people bound up in some classification, a new
deliberation had been opened, and only part of them had been
reclassified. Not all — only part. I found that shape familiar.

*I realized, that day, for the first time, how closely this shape
resembled what I had gone through. Not all, only part. The same
pattern. I couldn't tell whether this was chance, or whether such
deliberations simply tended, as a rule, to end this way. I had no way
of confirming it.*

---

Then, two weeks later, another document arrived.

This time it was from a different city. A different region again from
the first. The signature box again bore the name "Hora." This time the
handwriting was different again, different even from the first.

I laid the two documents side by side. The same name, two different
hands. There was something strange about it, even setting aside a mere
shared name. I grew curious what these names were doing. I read through
the contents of both documents again.

Both cases were similar. A case study in administrative procedure
improvement. Reexamining an existing decision, and reconfirming part
of it anew. Documents received with the signature box filled.

---

I went to the reception desk and asked.

"…How far are these cities from here?"

"…Far. One's about ten days away, and the other is even farther than
that."

"…Can you find out whether anyone from those places has ever come
here?"

"…We have no way to check that. We can't see the roster of another
city."

"…Then can you at least confirm that this really wasn't me?"

"…The handwriting is different, so that much is confirmed. But beyond
that, there's no way for us to look into it further."

I stood there a moment, hearing that answer. This city hides nothing.
But this time, it wasn't a matter of something being hidden — there
simply was no way to know, to begin with.

---

That evening, the first person I thought of was Temel. As far as I
knew, he was the only person who traveled between cities.

*I held onto this thought for a long while. But I soon realized it
didn't add up. The first document's receipt date was three months
before. Temel hadn't even come to this city yet, at that time. It was
much later — the day he told us what our names meant — that he'd
arrived. There was no reason, and no time, for him to have written my
name into another city's document in advance.*

*I set down here that I suspected him. And I set down, alongside it,
that the suspicion was wrong. To this day I still don't know who wrote
those two documents. All I know is that it wasn't Temel.*

---

When I told Tyche about this, she was unimpressed at first.

"…Isn't it just someone with the same name? It's not that unusual a
name."

"…But it's two of them. In two different cities. Both filled in the
signature box. The box that nobody here fills."

Tyche's face grew a little thoughtful at that.

"…That is a little strange. Filling in the signature box is rare
enough on its own — that two people with the same name would both have
done it."

"…Right."

"…But that's not something probability can't explain either. There's
exchange between these cities, so word that one of them had filled it
in first could have spread to the other. Someone might have followed
suit."

"…Followed my name?"

"…Not your name specifically — it could just be that two people who
happened to both be named Hora each did it on their own."

"…But why now, of all times? It's been since I signed."

Tyche took a moment before answering that one.

"…That's explainable too. That you signed it, Hora, might have become
known among the people of this deliberation district, and spread to
other cities from there, and a few people followed suit. Since filling
in the signature box is so rare, word that someone had done it might
well have traveled far."

"…So it started because of me."

"…Not started, exactly — more like, triggered. You didn't tell anyone
to do it."

I thought that distinction was fair. I hadn't told anyone to do
anything. And yet the very possibility that something I'd done might
have moved someone else's hand, somewhere I'd never see, felt heavy to
me all the same.

---

I couldn't fully believe that explanation, but I had no grounds to
argue against it either. Tyche's account, once again, was the simplest
explanation there was.

*I won't try to win against Tyche on this point either. She might well
be right. Shared names, rumor, an overlap of coincidences. All of it
could happen. I only want to set down this one fact exactly. I never
wrote the names on those two documents. That's the one thing I know
for certain.*

---

I told the Secretary about this too. He studied the two documents for
a long while.

"…Has anything like this happened before?"

"…Not as far as I know."

"…So this is the first time?"

"…As far as I know. I have no way of checking the records of other
deliberation districts."

"…Is there any way to check?"

"…No. Each deliberation district manages its own records separately.
There's no place where everything is gathered together."

Hearing that answer, I thought back to something I'd heard earlier.
That no one holds the whole picture. Back then, it had been said about
the people who called Temel in. Now I was hearing the same thing said
about my own name.

"…Secretary, so there's no way at all to look into this?"

"…Not at the deliberation-district level. If you wish, you could raise
it as a matter for full review."

"…So, another new deliberation?"

"…Yes. The same procedure."

Hearing that, I hesitated. I'd already been through one new
deliberation. I knew, in my body, what rhythm review moved to. That
day, I didn't have the confidence to start that rhythm all over again
for this.

"…Are you not going to open one?"

"…I don't know. Not right now."

The Secretary added nothing to that answer. He simply nodded once and
put his papers in order.

"…Doesn't this strike you as strange, Secretary?"

"…It strikes me as strange."

That short answer surprised me. It was rare for the Secretary to say
anything carrying feeling.

"…But finding it strange doesn't give me anything more I can do about
it."

I nodded at that. In this city, the distance between finding something
strange and being able to do something about it always went uncrossed,
that way.

---

A few days later I went to the reading room and looked again into how
many records existed, in this city, of the signature box ever being
filled. The one person eleven years before. Temel. And me. That was the
entire record for this city. Records from other cities I had no way
to see, here.

*I laid those three names side by side and looked at them for a long
while. All three, without knowing one another, had each filled the
same box for their own separate reasons. I tried, several times, to
connect those three names to the unfamiliar handwriting bearing my own
name from other cities. It wouldn't connect. I decided to stop trying.*

The reading room clerk, tidying up, asked me.

"…What are you looking for?"

"…Just counting how many people in this city have ever filled the
signature box."

"…How many is it?"

"…Three."

The clerk looked a little surprised.

"…That's few. I only just learned that too, though. I've worked here
for years, and I've never once counted that."

"…Nobody ever counts it?"

"…No reason to. Everyone just passes over that box without looking,
generally."

Hearing that, I thought again of how, in this city, there are always
one of two reasons for not knowing something. It isn't written down, or
nobody asked. This time there was a third. Nobody had ever counted.

---

That night I put both documents in the drawer. Beside my mother's
paper, beside Temel's name, beside the notes of these two hundred-some
days.

When I told my aunt about it, she studied the documents for a long
while, then spoke.

"…Is this supposed to be a frightening story?"

"…I don't know. I don't even know if it's frightening or not."

"…Are you sure you didn't write it?"

"…Yes, I'm sure."

My aunt handed the documents back to me.

"…Then just write that down. That you didn't write it. The rest isn't
something you can know."

"…But Aunt, I have this strange feeling."

"…What kind of feeling."

"…Like something keeps going even without me. Like something I did is
still walking around out there, without me."

My aunt looked at me for a moment, then spoke.

"…Is that a bad feeling?"

"…I don't know. It doesn't feel bad, but it's not comfortable either."

"…Your mother probably felt that too. She probably never imagined that
last line she wrote would sit in a drawer for two whole years without
her, and then finally reach you. But it got there in the end, didn't
it."

I couldn't say anything back to that. My aunt sometimes pointed out,
just like that, connections I hadn't seen.

I thought she was right. I don't know why these two documents exist. I
don't know who wrote them. There's only one thing I know. I didn't
write them.

---

Writing this record now, I have to set down one more thing. In these
last few days, while writing this chapter, a third document arrived.
From a city farther still than the first two, different from both. The
content was similar too. A registration reexamination, a partial
reclassification, my name written in the signature box.

*Only after receiving this third document did I accept that this
wasn't a coincidence happening once or twice, but something continuing
to happen. Tyche would probably say even three times can be explained
by probability. She probably would. I can't argue against that
explanation. I only set down, alongside it, that I no longer know when
this will stop, or whether it will continue at all.*

*When I began writing this record, I already knew where this story
would end. But that a new document would arrive while I was writing
this very chapter — even I hadn't known that in advance. I decided not
to invent anything, and to put it in exactly as it happened.*

*Before closing this chapter, I want to leave this fact exactly as it
stands, in this record. The only time I ever wrote my name in a
signature box was that once, in this city, on that one document. And
yet my name now appears in at least three other documents, in three
other cities. I didn't write those names. Someone is filling the same
box. Who that is, I never managed to find out, in the end. This record
ends without giving that answer.*

*I know this is the most uncomfortable part of this book. The reader
will probably want to guess the answer before I do. I wanted that too.
But I close this chapter not knowing the answer. If there's one thing
this city has taught me, it's not to fill in what I don't know as if I
did. That's exactly like this city's signature box. When it's empty,
you leave it empty.*
