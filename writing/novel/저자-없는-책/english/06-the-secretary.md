# Chapter 6. The Secretary

> _Previously_ — Hora meeting Tyche, the deliberation assistant. Whatever
> she asked, Tyche answered exactly, kindly, without a trace of cynicism.
> "So what if it's chance? Isn't it fine as long as it's exact?" left
> something hanging inside Hora, though she still couldn't put into words
> what it was.

I met the Secretary for the first time around the end of my first week.

Before that, I'd only seen the title "the Secretary" on paper. In the sender field of notices, in the approval history of meeting material, it always simply read "the Secretary." No name. I assumed that was the formal way the title was written. I didn't know yet that this wasn't a person named the Secretary, but a person seated in the post called the Secretary.

---

That day I'd filed a material request wrong. I'd meant to request more of the underlying material from a past deliberation, but I got the form confused and wrote it in the wrong box. The counter told me to fill it out again, and then someone from inside that counter came out and offered to look at it themselves.

That person was the Secretary.

Their age was hard to guess. They looked to be around forty, but their manner of speaking was calmer than that. Their clothes were neat, nothing about them dressed up. A low voice sat in their throat, and it only struck me later how that tone never once shifted, all day. They took the paperwork, looked it over, and pointed at a couple of spots.

"…Here, this box needs the previous session's number. Right now it has this session's number in it."

"…Ah, I'm sorry."

"…No, everyone gets confused by this at first. I'll fix it for you."

And they really did fix it, right there, on the spot. I didn't even know whether that was the Secretary's job to do or not, but they did it without a second thought.

After looking the corrected paperwork over once more, they stamped it. An intake-confirmation stamp. Watching that stamp come down, I suddenly thought of the signature box. Wondering whether this stamp stood in for a name, I asked,

"…Is that your own stamp?"

"…No, it's the counter's stamp. Whoever's working, the same stamp gets used."

"…You don't have your own stamp?"

The Secretary looked thoughtful for a moment, then answered.

"…No. There's never been a need for one."

I nodded at that answer without much thought. "There's never been a need for one" sounded, at the time, like nothing more than a practical explanation.

"…Then how would you go about getting a new one made?"

"…It's applied for. But no one's applied for one yet, as far as anyone's seen. Everyone seems to think the counter stamp is enough."

I took that answer without much thought too. If it's enough, of course a new one wouldn't get made.

As the Secretary put the paperwork into the intake tray, they added one more thing. "…You can receive the material with this now. It'll take two or three days." "…Thank you." "…No need. That's simply how the procedure runs." I heard "that's simply how the procedure runs" many times that day. Every time I thanked them, the Secretary deflected it with that phrase. As if it wasn't something they'd done for me, but something that had been done because the procedure ran that way. At first I took it for politeness. But after watching a few more days, I came to see it wasn't politeness at all. They meant it.

---

"…May I ask your name?"

That was the first time I'd asked. They smiled slightly and answered.

"…You can call me the Secretary."

"…No, I mean your name."

"…Just the Secretary. That's what everyone calls me here."

I thought, at first, they were avoiding the question. I started to ask again, then stopped, because there wasn't a trace of concealment in their expression. It was the face of someone genuinely accustomed to being addressed that way.

"…But on things like application forms, there's a box for a name, isn't there? What do you write there?"

"…The title. 'The Secretary,' like that."

"…Is not writing your name a rule?"

"…It isn't a rule. But that's how everyone writes it. When I first started here, I used to write my name too, but at some point I just started writing the title instead."

I was curious about that "at some point," but I didn't press further. That day I just let it be. People's habits change over time, after all.

*From that point on, I never once called this person by a name. Not in this record either. I learn the reason later, and it isn't that this person was hiding anything. This person themselves simply took it as a given that their own name would never be called.*

I told my aunt about it that evening. "…Auntie, the Secretary won't tell me their name. Just says to call them 'the Secretary.'" My aunt nodded. "…That's how it always is. I don't know our district's Secretary's name either, even after years." "…Doesn't that seem strange?" My aunt thought for a moment and said, "…Not so much strange as — I suppose that's just what the post is. It's not like knowing the name would change anything." I had nothing to argue back with. Nothing really would have changed.

"…But Auntie, how many years has your district's Secretary been in the post?"

My aunt counted for a moment, then answered. "…Five years or so, I think. I don't really remember the one before them." "…You didn't even notice they'd changed?" "…No, I just realized it one day when the voice was different. I never remembered the face well to begin with." That answer struck me as strangely sad, but my aunt set the soup bowl down without a trace of concern.

---

The Secretary handled convening the committee and its records. Setting meeting dates, distributing material, writing up the minutes, passing confirmed results on to each department. Everything I ever asked them about got explained back to me as one of these four things.

"…Don't you ever give your own opinion on what's being deliberated?"

"…Only convening happens here."

"…But you write the minutes yourself, don't you? Doesn't that mean you're deciding what goes in and what stays out?"

"…That's written to a fixed format. It isn't chosen."

I sensed that this answer didn't come from modesty so much as from genuinely believing it. A modest person sometimes laughs and says, "Oh, it's nothing I did." This person didn't laugh. It was truly the face of someone who believed nothing had been done by them at all.

I got curious about where that stamp was kept too, and asked someone at the counter. There was one at every counter, they said, and even when the person staffing it changed, the same stamp stayed in use. The stamp had a counter number engraved on it, not a person's name. I felt, all over again, that this was the same pattern as everything else in this city. Marks that attach to a seat, not to a person.

I looked up a few of the meeting minutes and read them too. The minutes the Secretary wrote really were free of any wasted word. Who had said what, what material had been submitted, what the result was — all set down in order, nothing more. Not a single line of their own thought or judgment went into it. I thought this made for good records. Since none of the writer's own color bled into it, anyone reading it would come away with the same content.

"…How do you decide what goes into the minutes?"

"…Everything goes in. What was said, material submitted, the result. Nothing left out."

"…So there's nothing you write as more important?"

"…No. It's all written the same."

I was impressed by that answer. I thought it made for a genuinely fair record. Later I'd come to think about what price that fairness was paid at, but that day I was simply impressed.

---

What stayed with me most from that first meeting was the way they spoke.

Not "This is how I'll handle it," but "This is how it gets handled." Not "I confirmed it," but "It was confirmed." At the time, I took this for nothing more than a bureaucrat's manner of speech. Since that's how everyone talked, I figured, this person talked that way too.

"…When does this case get handled?"

"…It gets handled next session."

"…Who handles it?"

The Secretary looked at me for a moment. Not because they couldn't answer — it looked more like the question had struck them as slightly unfamiliar.

"…It gets handled by the deliberation district."

In that moment I felt like I should ask something more, but I didn't know exactly what to ask, so I just moved on to the next question. I sensed, dimly, that this answer wasn't an answer to my question. I already knew the district handled it. What I'd asked was who. But that day I let the mismatch go. It hadn't seemed like a question important enough to press for a real answer to.

For the next few days I listened back carefully, in my head, to the conversations I'd had with the Secretary. There truly was no subject. Instead of "I put it in order," it was "It was put in order." Instead of "I got in touch with you," it was "Word went out." Instead of "I confirmed it," it was "It was confirmed." In sentence after sentence, nothing stood where the subject should be. I thought this was too consistent to be one person's mere manner of speech. But I didn't hold onto that thought for long. A consistent manner of speech can exist too, after all.

Once, the Secretary slipped and put in a subject by accident. "…I got in touch with you at the time, so—" they started, then stopped, and corrected themselves. "…Word went out, but it seems it didn't reach you." I didn't let that correction slip past me. I asked, before I even meant to.

"…You just said 'I' and then corrected yourself."

The Secretary looked at me for a moment, then laughed.

"…Ah, did I? Must have been a slip of habit."

That laugh wasn't awkward, and that was exactly why it stayed with me all the more. This person truly regarded that correction as a mistake. That putting in a subject was the mistake, and leaving it out was the normal state — that day was the first time I saw it clearly, laid bare.

---

The Secretary was neither especially kind to me nor especially curt. Whatever I asked, they answered. Even when I filed paperwork wrong, they never showed irritation. I liked that manner.

Once I was late because I'd missed a change in meeting time. I fully expected to be scolded for it. But the Secretary only said this.

"…Word must have gone out late. From now on, it'll be checked ahead of time for you."

I was bewildered by that. It was my mistake — why blame the notice for it, I wondered. But thinking it over again, it didn't seem like they were covering for me — it seemed like they genuinely thought that way. That being late, too, was a result of the procedure, not something to be pinned down as my own fault.

*At the time I thought this was consideration. Thinking about it again now, consideration and avoidance may not be things this person's mind actually distinguishes between. But I know clearly that this isn't because this person is a bad person.*

Feeling sorry about being late that day stayed with me for a while, so the next day I went back to the Secretary and apologized. "…I'm sorry about being late yesterday." The Secretary looked at me for a moment and said,

"…What's there to be sorry about. The notice went out late, that's all."

"…Still, I should have checked ahead of time—"

"…It isn't that you didn't check, Hora. The very notice there was to check simply went out late. How do you check something that isn't there?"

Hearing that, I sat stunned for a moment. How do you check something that isn't there. I didn't know, at the time, that those words would come back before me later, in an entirely different weight. That day, I just thanked them and let it go.

As I turned to leave, the Secretary added one more thing behind me. "…From now on, I'll check ahead of time myself and let you know." I stopped and turned back at that. "I'll check ahead of time" had "I" attached to it again. This time it wasn't corrected. I quietly filed that moment away. This person, too, sometimes — very rarely — put themselves into a sentence.

---

A few days later, I asked Tyche about the Secretary.

"…Is the Secretary always like that?"

"…Like what?"

"…Whatever you ask them, they never say they did it themselves."

Tyche thought for a moment and answered.

"…Huh. I'd never thought about it, but you're right, they really are like that."

"…Doesn't that seem strange to you?"

"…Hmm, I don't think it's strange, exactly. That's just what the Secretary's job is, isn't it. Convening, passing things on. It's not a post where you make decisions yourself, so maybe that's why they talk that way."

That explanation seemed right to me. That day I really did just accept it and move on. The title is what it is, so of course the manner of speech would be too. I hadn't yet seen the other possibility hiding behind that sentence.

Tyche added, as if something had just occurred to them.

"…Actually, come to think of it, I heard my parents did Secretary work too. My grandmother as well. The Secretary's post has been around a pretty long time in this city."

"…So is it passed down through families or something?"

"…No, it's just a post you can apply for. But strangely enough, a lot of similar-natured people seem to end up going into it."

"…What kind of nature?"

Tyche thought for a moment and answered.

"…Hmm, people who are fine not stepping forward themselves? Apparently people who want to be the one out front don't last long in this job. It frustrates them too much."

Hearing that, I felt like I understood, a little, why the Secretary had come to speak the way they did. If someone who doesn't want to step forward takes on a job where they don't have to, maybe the manner of speech that fits that job just settles into the body on its own.

That evening I turned that conversation back over in my mind, and it kept nagging at me that there had once been a time, apparently, when the Secretary used to write their own name. This person hadn't gone without writing their name from the very start — at some point, they'd simply stopped. What had happened at that "some point," I didn't even think to ask that day. I only pull that question back out much later, and by then it's sitting in a far heavier place.

Walking home that day, I took out the paperwork the Secretary had smiled and fixed for me again. The paper where the previous session's number was correctly filled in now. There was an intake-confirmation box at the very bottom of that paper too. The stamp was there, but the officer's signature box beside it was still empty. Looking at it, nothing occurred to me. The stamp seemed like it should be enough. Why a signature would be needed separately from that — I hadn't thought about it, not then.

Putting that paperwork away in the drawer, I turned back over, one more time, the several faces the Secretary had shown me that day. Kind, exact, almost never using a subject, and once — just once — using one and correcting it themselves. I couldn't find a single reason to think ill of this person. I still can't.
