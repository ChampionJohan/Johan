# Chapter 44. What the Names Mean

> _Previously_ — The new deliberation's result came in. Of the one
> thousand one hundred and forty, two hundred and thirteen were
> released from the Seventh Classification. The remaining nine hundred
> and twenty-seven stayed as they were. It wasn't satisfying. Hora
> wrote the remaining number in the drawer and decided not to erase it.

It was about two weeks later that I heard Temel had come back to this
city.

The person at the reception desk told me. "…That person who does the
searching for people, you know. He's back again." The moment I heard
that, I dropped what I was doing and went to the north market.

*Even now I can't fully explain why I hurried so much, right then.
There wasn't really anything left for me to ask Temel. I just heard the
news, and my body moved before I thought about it.*

---

Temel was in front of the notice board. The same posture as before. The
same bag, only his shoes looked a little more worn.

"…Back again."

Temel saw me and smiled a little.

"…Oh, Hora."

"…Who are you looking for this time?"

"…No, this time it's something else. Just passing through."

"…Just passing through?"

"…Yeah. On my way to another city. Stopped by here on the way."

I didn't believe that. This wasn't a city you'd stop by on the way to
somewhere else. Still, I didn't press him on it.

"…What about your open cases? You said you had three here."

"…All finished. Found one, couldn't find the other two."

"…What about the one you found?"

"…Got word to the family. That's the best moment there is. It doesn't
come often, though."

"…And the two you couldn't find?"

"…Just left the record. Someone might pick it up and keep looking,
someday."

I felt that his way of speaking was exactly the same as before. Calm,
unhurried, never calling a loss a loss. Months had passed, and this
person hadn't changed.

"…So this time you're really just passing through?"

"…Yeah. Nothing to do in this city, this time. But I felt like I
should come here anyway."

"…What does that mean, felt like you should come?"

"…Can't really explain it. Sometimes a thought like that just comes to
me, walking along. Not often."

"…Why?"

Temel gave a small laugh.

"…I don't really know that either."

We sat beside the notice board, as before. I told him everything that
had happened. Two hundred and thirteen, nine hundred and twenty-seven,
everything I'd felt caught between those two numbers. Temel listened
to all of it. Without saying anything, the whole time.

"…That's it? That's your reaction?"

"…What more did you want?"

"…I don't know. Something like — that's amazing."

Temel shook his head.

"…Nothing amazing about it. Two hundred and thirteen is two hundred
and thirteen. No more, no less."

*This indifference stung me at first. Looking back now, that was the
most exact reaction Temel could have given me. He didn't turn me into
a hero.*

---

We sat in silence for a while. The market made its usual sounds.
Somewhere far off, someone was haggling.

"…Hora."

"…Yes."

"…You've never asked me, have you. Why your name is Hora."

I was a little surprised by the question.

"…I never thought about it. It's just my name."

"…Never wondered what it means?"

"…I asked my aunt once. She said she doesn't know. My mother chose it,
and she's gone now."

Temel looked out toward the market for a moment before speaking.

"…I met an old record-keeper once. Someone who knew the old languages.
Digging up where names came from was a hobby of his. I asked him about
a few, once."

"…Mine too?"

"…Yeah. He said it comes from a word written ὥρα. It means the hour —
the appointed time."

---

I couldn't say anything, hearing that.

*The appointed time. Hearing those words, I thought back over what I'd
spent sixteen years doing. Reading to the end. Reading to the last
line. Waiting for the last line to come. I know it's a name that was
given by chance. My mother may or may not have known its meaning when
she chose it. And yet, in that moment, I couldn't shake the feeling
that something fit into place.*

*I thought again of the twelve pages my mother left. I'd read that last
line two years too late. That a person who missed her own appointed
time should be called by the name of the appointed time felt, that
day, a little strange to me. I don't want to say the name led me
somewhere. I don't want to write this record that way. But once I knew
how closely the name and the way I've lived overlapped, I could never
again think of the two as entirely separate.*

"…You knew that, and never told me until now?"

"…You never asked."

"…Would you have told me if I had?"

"…Yes. No reason to hide it. Nobody ever asked."

I thought that line summed up this whole city. Nothing hidden. Simply,
no one had asked.

*Writing this sentence, I counted how many times I've written something
like it, from the start of this book to now. Not written down, or not
asked. In this city, the reason for not knowing something was always
one of those two. It was the same with the meaning of a name. It hadn't
been hidden. Simply, for sixteen years, no one — not even I — had
asked.*

---

Just then Tyche arrived. She seemed to have heard at the reception desk
that I'd gone to the market, and followed me.

"…What are you two up to?"

"…Talking about what names mean."

"…What names mean?"

I told her, exactly, what I'd just heard. At first Tyche's face was
unmoved.

"…That's what your name meant, Hora? That's interesting."

Temel looked at Tyche for a moment and said.

"…Want me to look up yours too?"

Tyche shrugged.

"…Go ahead. I am curious, I guess."

"…It comes from a word, τύχη. It means chance — luck."

---

Tyche laughed, hearing that.

At first it was a short laugh. Then, strangely, it went on. I watched
it from beside her. She covered her mouth lightly with one hand and
kept laughing. A little water gathered at the corner of her eyes; I
couldn't tell whether it came from laughing, or from something else.

"…Chance. My name."

She said that and laughed again. This time the sound was smaller.

In that moment I thought of Tyche's grandmother. A story she'd told me
before. Her grandmother had been the kind of person who said there was
a reason this city was one where no one is wronged, and that such a
reason couldn't possibly be chance. Tyche had said she'd grown up
hearing that and spent her whole life checking it, and that nearly all
of it turned out explainable by a vacancy and a number and
probability. I imagined, briefly, what that grandmother would have
said, seeing this moment.

I thought Tyche would say something. She always did. Tyche was someone
who put things into words, whatever they were. This time, even after
the laughter faded, no words came.

She opened her mouth once, then closed it again.

I waited. Temel waited too. Neither of us pressed her.

Tyche, in the end, said nothing at all.

*I want to render this silence exactly. Tyche gave me no answer that
day. I don't want to fill that silence with anything. What Tyche was
thinking, I don't know. I didn't ask. That wasn't a question that was
mine to ask.*

*Writing this part, I felt several temptations. The temptation to write
that something in her shook, in that moment; or, on the other hand,
that her own conviction only hardened further. I decided to write
neither. I couldn't clearly read either one on Tyche's face. Laughter,
and silence — those two things were all I saw. I won't invent anything
beyond that.*

---

A stillness settled over that spot for a moment. A sheet of paper on
the notice board fluttered slightly in the wind.

Beside us, someone in worn clothes walked past at a slow pace, unnoticed
by anyone.

The sound of the market pressed back in around us. Tyche got up first.

"…I'll go ahead. I've still got some work left."

"…Okay."

She said only that, and walked off. Her stride wasn't much different
from usual. Only I watched her back for a little longer than usual.

---

Watching her go, Temel spoke to me.

"…Guess I brought up something I shouldn't have."

"…No. Tyche's the one who asked you to."

"…That's true, but."

Temel let the end of that sentence trail off. I wondered for a moment
whether he knew something more, then realized he didn't. Temel had
only passed along a name, the same as with me. What that name would
touch inside the person hearing it — even he couldn't have known that.

"…Does telling someone something like this change a person? From what
you've seen."

Temel thought for a moment.

"…Some people change, some don't. I'm just the one who passes it on,
so I don't get to see what comes after. I pass it on and move to the
next city."

"…This time you're not moving to the next city. You're staying to
watch."

"…So I am. Might stick around a little this time, I'm thinking."

"…Why?"

"…I don't know. Just a feeling I have."

*I felt, then, that Temel was holding back his words more than usual. I
should have asked why, and didn't. Thinking about it now, I think he
too was gauging, in that moment, what he'd just opened up. He'd only
said one name, and he'd watched, right in front of him, that name touch
something inside Tyche.*

"…Do you know what your own name means?"

Temel thought for a moment.

"…Never asked."

"…Why not?"

"…Wasn't curious. I'm more used to looking for other people's names
than my own."

That answer felt exactly like him. I didn't press any further.

---

That evening, at home, I told my aunt about this. She listened quietly,
then said.

"…That's what your name meant?"

"…Yeah. Did Mom know?"

"…I don't know. I don't remember your mother liking that kind of
talk."

"…Aunt, do you think I'm really living out that meaning?"

My aunt, ladling the rice, looked at me.

"…I don't know that either. Some people live out their name's meaning,
and some don't."

I nodded at that. My aunt gave no certainty either. I found that,
somehow, a relief.

"…Why do you think Mom chose that name for me?"

"…Your mother chose it, so you'd have to ask your mother. I don't
know."

"…But if you had to guess."

My aunt set down her spoon for a moment and thought.

"…Your mother was someone who decided things ahead of time about
everything. The day to put away the winter clothes, decided ahead of
time. The day to buy rice, decided ahead of time. So maybe she picked
a name like that for that kind of reason."

"…Is that the same thing as this meaning?"

"…Well, I don't know that either. Just that she was that kind of
person."

Hearing that, I thought of the twelve pages my mother had written. My
aunt might have been right. My mother was someone who decided things
ahead of time. And yet the very last sentence she left for me hadn't
been placed somewhere fixed in advance — it had been placed somewhere
it could easily be missed.

"…Did you hear about Tyche?"

"…You just told me. Laughed, and said nothing, you said."

"…What do you think about that?"

My aunt thought for a moment, then spoke.

"…I know people like that. People who laugh and then can't speak.
That's not a bad thing. It just means there's nothing to say, in that
moment."

"…Isn't having nothing to say a bad thing?"

"…No. Having nothing to say is an answer too, Hora. Don't keep trying
to squeeze an answer out of her."

I thought about that for a long time. I think I'd been treating Tyche's
silence as if it were something lacking. Only after hearing my aunt's
words did I come to think, again, that the silence itself might be
whole on its own.

---

The next morning I went to work as usual. The meeting room was the
same as always. Tyche, too, was out as usual, sorting through
materials. She said nothing about the day before. Neither did I bring
it up first.

"…What's the schedule today?"

"…Two requests to view records in the morning, one meeting in the
afternoon."

"…Got it."

We started the day as usual, that way. On the surface, nothing about
the day had changed. I was, if anything, grateful for that
ordinariness. It meant Tyche wasn't avoiding me over what had happened
the day before.

*From that day on, I came to hear my own name differently than before.
Every time someone called out, "Hora," its meaning would flicker past,
just for an instant. The hour. The appointed time. I still don't know
whether that's true or not. Only that, after that, I could never hear
it again the way I once had, meaning nothing at all.*

*Tyche never brought up her own name's meaning to me again, after that
day. I never asked either. That silence continues on for a long time
still to come.*

*Writing this chapter, I set the two names down side by side. The
hour, the appointed time. Chance, luck. The two words look like
opposites. What is appointed, and what is not. And yet I'm still not
certain, even now, whether the two are really opposites. An appointed
hour can still, for someone, arrive as a moment of pure chance; and
chance, looked back on later, can look as if it had been appointed all
along. I won't decide, in this book, which of the two is right. I'll
only set down, exactly, that the two were spoken that day, in the same
place, from the same person's mouth, side by side.*
