# Chapter 20. To the Secretary

> _Previously_ — Tyche laid out the structure without a single gap. "…So nobody gets wronged. That isn't a bad thing." Hora couldn't argue back. Tyche had also said, "outside the structure isn't my territory." Wondering who stood outside that structure, Hora thought of the Secretary, who handled convening and the record.

That night I opened the list again and closed it again.

I already knew, having met the twelve, that meeting all four hundred thirty-two would only get me the same answer. People who'd only passed it on, people who'd only measured, people who'd only confirmed. All honest, all doing only their own share. And yet no one, from beginning to end, knew the decision that all those shares had added up to.

Except for one person.

The Secretary convened, distributed materials, wrote the minutes, and delivered the confirmed results to each department. If there was anyone in this city who saw a matter from beginning to end in documented form, it wouldn't be someone who judged — it would be someone who carried. And a carrier, at least, would know the route.

---

The next morning, walking to the reception desk, I passed the water channel. The water was still making its earth-colored sound. Passing it, I briefly wondered whether, if I asked someone about this channel too, it might all get explained someday. I already knew that since it had never come up as an agenda item, there was no one to explain it. But the Seventh Classification matter had come up as an agenda item. So there should be someone who could explain it.

I stopped for a moment outside the reception desk. I wasn't sure going to find the Secretary was the right choice. Meeting the twelve, I'd already heard "that's not my responsibility" more times than I could count. The Secretary would probably say the same. And yet I pushed the door open anyway. I'd been everywhere else — it felt like I ought to try here, one last time.

The Secretary was in a small room off the reception desk. The door was open, and papers sat neatly stacked on his desk. He greeted me first when he saw me.

"…Hora, what brings you here today?"

"…It's about the Seventh Classification matter."

The Secretary pulled out a document folder and offered me a seat.

"…Sit down. What would you like to know?"

I brought out the questions I'd prepared in order, starting with the simplest.

"…Why haven't items 3, 7, and 12 been confirmed yet?"

"…Confirming the relocation sites falls under the destination-site management department, but that department hasn't yet had personnel assigned. Personnel assignment isn't possible until next quarter's budget is approved."

This was already something I knew. I'd heard the same thing from the eighth and ninth of the twelve I'd met. I moved to the next question.

"…Then why wasn't the implementation date set for after next quarter? If there's no manpower, those three sites can't be processed."

For this question the Secretary flipped through the folder to find a page with dates and showed it to me.

"…The implementation date was set based on the processing threshold for the whole Seventh Classification matter. It had already been eight months past the threshold, and there was a review finding that delaying further would only let the unregistered accumulation grow larger. So the overall implementation date was kept as is, and only the three sites were handled through temporary designation."

"…Who reviewed that?"

"…At a higher-department review meeting. There are minutes."

The Secretary really did find and hand me that minutes record. I took it and skimmed through. Detailed dates and grounds that none of the twelve had given me were all right there. I realized this was a document I was seeing for the first time. Viewing had been possible, but I hadn't gotten that far.

---

"…Is it a problem specific to this matter being unusually big, or would it have turned out this way even if it had been a small matter?"

"…Over the past ten years, small-scale matters ran twenty or thirty people at a time. There were a few temporary designations back then too. But with fewer people, confirmation came quickly. It was a scale that only needed the department to bring on three or four more staff."

"…Why didn't that work this time?"

"…This time, at a scale of eleven hundred forty, the manpower needed for confirmation grew accordingly. Placing that manpower requires budget, and budget only moves on a quarterly basis. Something that never snagged when the scale was small snagged this time."

I heard that and understood this was, in a certain sense, an honest answer. If this had been a small matter, I wouldn't even be sitting in this seat right now. There hadn't been a problem — the problem had simply grown large.

"…So just because of the scale, people end up going to places that aren't even confirmed?"

The Secretary took a moment to choose his words for this question.

"…It's not confirmed that they're going. The temporary designation could still turn into a confirmed one, or get reassigned somewhere else. The problem is only that the implementation date arrives first, in the meantime."

"…Why doesn't the implementation date wait for confirmation?"

"…Because the implementation date for the whole matter was already set based on the other eleven hundred ten sites. If the whole thing were delayed for the sake of three sites, the other eleven hundred ten would be delayed along with it."

"…I don't think I could have found this on my own."

"…If you file a viewing request, it all comes up. It just takes time if you don't know the route to search. I can find it quickly because this is a document I handle every day."

Hearing that, I understood for the first time why the Secretary was the one person who could see the whole structure. Precisely because he doesn't judge, he ends up seeing everything. Someone who judges digs deep into only their own responsibility; someone who convenes passes lightly over every responsibility.

"…So did nothing strike you as odd in this matter, Secretary?"

The Secretary thought for a moment. It wasn't a silence meant to dodge the question. It was a genuinely thoughtful face.

"…The scale being large was visible. Eleven hundred forty is among the largest I've convened for."

"…And?"

"…But scale being large and a defect in the procedure being present are different things. What I look at is whether procedure was followed. Procedure was followed at every stage."

I heard that answer and thought it was similar to what I'd heard from Tyche. But something was different. Tyche had stopped at one point, saying "that's not my territory." The Secretary didn't stop.

"…I've heard the temporary designation is a procedure in the regulation. Who made that regulation?"

"…An amendment seventeen years ago. Back then delays before confirmation kept coming up frequently for small matters, so a method was introduced — designating temporarily and confirming later. Before that, the agenda item itself was held until confirmed, and that caused processing to run far too slow."

"…So thanks to that regulation, the three sites now remain temporarily designated."

"…Correct."

"…What if that regulation didn't exist?"

"…If it didn't exist, this entire matter would have been held until all three sites were confirmed. Then the remaining eleven hundred ten would have been processed that much later too."

---

I heard that answer and couldn't speak for a moment. What the Secretary had just told me wasn't the answer I wanted. I'd wanted to find some one broken link. Instead, what he showed me was that the regulation had been made, if anything, to process more people, faster.

"…So that regulation isn't wrong either."

"…Not in my view. Though what result this regulation produces when applied to a matter this large — the people who made it probably never fully anticipated that either."

*Something caught at me in this last sentence. It was the first time the Secretary had ever said something unconfirmed — "probably never anticipated." Up to that point, every answer he'd given had been settled fact. Dates, minutes, the basis clauses. This sentence alone was pure speculation.*

I wanted to press on that, but let it pass that day. There was still a lot left to ask.

"…What about the support funds, then? I heard the calculation was already finished even though the three sites aren't confirmed."

"…Support-fund calculation is done in advance based on the projected number of people. Even if the destination site changes, the calculated amount itself doesn't change. But actual disbursement only happens once the destination site is confirmed."

"…What if it never gets confirmed?"

"…Then disbursement never happens either."

That answer was short and precise. I found that precision, if anything, chilling.

---

I moved to a different question. That phrase I'd seen in the minutes kept nagging at me.

"…Secretary, in the eighteenth meeting's minutes, there's the line, 'one member raised the need for a re-review.' Who was that?"

The Secretary flipped his folder back to find that minutes record. It took quite a while. He paged through several sheets before finally stopping.

"…The eighteenth meeting, here it is. But the minutes only record the member number. A member from the Fourth Deliberation District."

"…What's the name?"

"…There's no name field in the minutes form. Only a member number is entered."

"…So there's no way to find out who this person was?"

"…Not necessarily impossible. Cross-referencing the member number against that day's attendance sheet might turn it up. But I've never done that cross-reference myself. It's not my job."

I heard that answer and felt a moment's disappointment. I already knew this person was the first one besides me who'd felt something was strange. And yet the Secretary didn't even know that person's name.

"…What happened to that person afterward? They lost the vote."

"…There's no separate procedure for a member whose proposed agenda loses a vote. It just moves on to the next item."

"…Nothing at all happens?"

"…No, nothing at all."

*I would come back to this answer many times afterward. That nothing happens to the member who raised the objection. No punishment, no compensation, not even a name left in the record. At the time I thought that was a fair system. I didn't yet know how differently this would come to weigh on me, in another way, later.*

---

"…Secretary, are you the one person who knows this matter from beginning to end?"

"…I don't know all of it. I know the meetings I convened, the materials I distributed, the minutes I wrote. That does overlap with most of the matter. Since the scale is large, a lot of meetings passed through too."

"…Then are you the only one who can explain this decision?"

The Secretary thought a little longer on this question.

"…There are people who can explain it. Everyone who handled each stage, all of them. I'm only someone who can see those stages stitched together. Stitching, too, is my job — but I'm not adding anything of my own into it."

"…Isn't stitching, too, a kind of judgment? Deciding what goes first and what goes after?"

"…The order is set by date. I'm not the one who decides that."

I wanted to argue with that, but couldn't find good grounds to. The dates really were just dates. It wasn't the Secretary who could change their order.

---

I talked with the Secretary for more than two hours that evening. I asked until I ran out of questions. The delayed site confirmation, the delay in personnel placement, the support-fund calculation basis, even the re-review request and the vote result recorded in the minutes. Not once did the Secretary say, "I can't answer that." What he didn't know, he said he didn't know, and what he knew, he told me all of it. Even when he said he didn't know, he explained why he didn't know.

*That day I had, for the first time, the experience of asking something in this city and not hitting a wall. Tyche was honest too, but Tyche stopped at a certain point, saying "that's not my territory." The Secretary didn't stop. Even what fell outside his responsibility, he explained along with the fact that it fell outside his responsibility. I didn't yet fully realize, that day, how rare this was.*

Finally I asked one last thing.

"…So, setting everything aside, what did you actually do in this matter, Secretary?"

The Secretary answered while tidying his folder.

"…What I did was convene."

"…That's all?"

"…Yes. Convened, passed things on, kept the record. That's all."

I heard that answer, gathered my papers, and stood. I thanked him. He returned the greeting.

Walking out the door, I retraced, in my head, everything he'd told me that day. The site hasn't been confirmed. Support-fund calculation is finished. The implementation date has been set. The regulation was amended. The meeting was convened.

On the way home I muttered those sentences to myself, one by one. Worried that talking to myself while walking would look strange, I kept my voice low. Not confirmed. Calculation finished. Convening done. As I walked, I vaguely sensed that all these sentences shared a similar shape, but I couldn't quite put my finger on what that shape was, at the time.

I told my aunt about the day's events. Hearing that the Secretary had given me the detailed answers I hadn't gotten from the twelve, my aunt looked a little surprised.

"…The Secretary told you all of that?"

"…Yeah. Nothing blocked me at all. Every question I asked got an answer."

"…So what did you find out?"

I couldn't answer that question right away. After a long while I said,

"…The procedure was all normal. The regulation had its reasons, the delay had its reasons. But somehow I feel like nothing got resolved at all."

My aunt asked, clearing the table.

"…You got every answer, so why doesn't it feel resolved?"

"…I don't know. I got every answer, but every one of those answers was passing off to somewhere else. This one's this department's business, that one's that department's business. But there was nothing of the Secretary's own in any of it."

My aunt heard that and looked at me for a moment. Her face looked like she wanted to ask more, then decided against it.

*That night, late, I kept turning those sentences over in my head, until I felt something strange. I couldn't quite put my finger on exactly what was strange, that night. It wasn't until the next morning that I found the name for that strangeness.*
