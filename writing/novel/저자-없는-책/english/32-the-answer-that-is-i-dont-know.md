# Chapter 32. The Answer That Is I Don't Know

> _Previously_ — I asked Temel, "Does that mean there's a meaning to
> it?" He waited a long while and answered, "…I don't know. I don't
> know either." I felt deflated hearing that answer, and strangely at
> ease at the same time. I sensed that Tyche's "there isn't one" and
> Temel's "I don't know" were different, but that night I couldn't
> explain how.

For the days that followed, I kept holding onto those two words.

I held onto them while working, and while eating. My aunt asked me
twice, "What are you thinking about so hard?" and I couldn't give her
a proper answer. It was hard to even call it thinking. I was circling
the same spot over and over, not moving forward.

There isn't one. I don't know.

The two phrases look similar on the surface. Both look like they sit
on the opposite side from "I know." But set side by side, they were
entirely different statements. There isn't one was a sentence that
closed a door. I don't know was a sentence that left the door open.

*Writing this line, I'm not sure whether this is the right way to
define it. I'm still not sure, even now. But I'll set down honestly,
at least, that this is how I've gone on telling the two apart, ever
since that day.*

---

The first thing that came to mind was the signature box.

Tyche had looked at that box and said, "Being blank isn't the
problem." That since this was a place where the whole decided, an
individual didn't need to write it. That was one conclusion. The
conclusion that there was no meaning to it. That the box was blank
simply because this city had been designed that way, and there was
nothing more to look for there.

Temel had looked at that box and drawn no conclusion at all. He wrote
it, he'd said, "because there's a box to write in." Why the box was
always blank, he didn't know either. He didn't know, but not knowing
didn't mean he did nothing. Not knowing, he wrote it anyway.

I set these two side by side and looked at them a long while. Tyche's
words gave me relief. Because there was a conclusion. With a
conclusion, I no longer had to lie awake over the question. Temel's
words gave no relief. Instead they kept making me do something. Since
I had to do something while still not knowing.

*Here I feel the temptation to write Tyche unkindly — to make her
reaching a conclusion look like an escape. But that wouldn't be
honest. Tyche's conclusion was a conclusion with evidence behind it.
Nearly everything she had confirmed in this city backed that
conclusion up. She hadn't reached it out of laziness.*

---

The second thing I thought of was the person from eleven years ago.

The record hadn't been cut off — it simply had no more. I'd held onto
that difference a long time back then too, and I hold onto it again
now, writing this passage. Cut off means someone severed it. No more
means it simply went that far and no further.

Tyche would have said this: that no more just means no more. That the
person could have moved, could have changed their name, could have
left this city. That either way, it was explained by probability. And
that explanation really did hold up. I couldn't refute a single part
of it.

I don't know what happened to that person. I still don't know. And yet
I couldn't turn that not-knowing into "so it's nothing." Not knowing
wasn't the same as saying it was nothing. Not knowing was only a
statement that I still didn't know.

*Writing this sentence, I know how much I'm trapped in circular logic.
Not knowing is only a statement that I don't know — this proves
nothing. Tyche would have caught exactly this point. "That's just it,
Hora. That's exactly what I'm saying. Not knowing proves nothing." I
have no confidence to refute that. I still don't, now.*

---

The third thing I thought of was the four hundred people.

In this city, an average of more than four hundred people are
involved in a single deliberation. At first I understood that number
as a safeguard. That if two hundred, three hundred people all pass a
decision through, there's no room left for any one person's malice to
slip in. That really was true. For months I went and found each of
those four hundred people, one by one, and confirmed that not a single
one of them held any malice. I only passed it on. I only put it in
order. I only confirmed it. All of them were honest.

Tyche explained this structure this way: that because no one decided
it, no one was wronged by it. That was a fact. I couldn't refute it.
And yet I was asking a different question on top of that fact.
Whether no one deciding it, and there being no meaning anywhere in
that whole, were the same thing.

Tyche would have said yes. That when four hundred small judgments
gather, that itself is the Consensus, and attaching any further
meaning to the Consensus is a thing people do, not a thing the
Consensus does. I thought this was right too. And yet, at the same
time, the fact that not one of those four hundred people had ever seen
the whole kept catching at me. I remembered what the official had
told me, before — "There's no one who sees the whole." If no one had
seen the whole, then it meant no one knew what the whole meant,
either. Not there isn't one — I don't know.

*I know this distinction might look trivial. Whether it's there isn't
one or I don't know, in the sense that neither gives me an answer
right here, right now, they're the same. But I felt, for the first
time that week, that the two made me into different people. Thinking
there isn't one, I stopped there. Thinking I don't know, I kept doing
something. It wasn't an answer, but it took me somewhere, at least.*

---

The fourth thing I thought of was the water channel.

Tyche had said this about the water channel: that the most plausible
explanation was that water, flowing downhill, had simply ended up
here. That this was much simpler than an explanation involving someone
deliberately sending it. I'd nodded at that. I'd confirmed myself,
more than once, that the simple explanation was usually the right one.

But the simple explanation being right, and that explanation being
everything, were different questions. That the water had flowed
downhill could be true, and that there was something more to why that
water happened to flow to this particular city — whether there was or
wasn't, I didn't know — could be true at the same time. That the two
didn't cancel each other out was something I noticed for the first
time, then.

*This was a very small realization, but it felt very large to me. Up
until then I'd thought "it's explained" and "there's no meaning" were
the same statement. I'd thought that once something was explained,
that proved there was no meaning to it. But that wasn't so. An
explanation was an explanation, and a meaning was a meaning. The two
weren't fighting over the same ground.*

*I once tried saying this to Tyche. She answered, "Then whether
there's a meaning ends up unprovable too, doesn't it — since you're
saying it doesn't overlap with the explanation." I couldn't refute
that either. Tyche was always someone who could shake whatever I'd
built in a single step.*

---

The fifth thing wasn't another person. It was my mother.

My mother had written twelve pages. Where things around the house
were kept, when to bring out the winter clothes. She wrote those
things first, and put the one sentence she left for me on the very
last line. I've thought about that order for a long time. Why put it
last. Had she been the kind of person who put important things last,
or had it simply drifted there as she wrote?

Tyche would have said this: that people usually write the easy things
first. That where things around the house were kept was easy to
write, and what she wanted to say to me must have been hard to write.
That putting off the hard thing was how it ended up on the last line.
That was a plausible explanation. Maybe that really was what happened.
Maybe my mother hadn't put that sentence last on purpose, and it had
simply been pushed there as she wrote.

I could accept that explanation. I did accept it. And yet, even having
accepted it, I couldn't turn into nothing the fact that that last line
had been waiting for me, of all people, at the end of the very two
years I'd let slip by. That the order was coincidence, and that I had
lost two years because of that order and finally reached that line —
those were stories on different layers.

*Tyche folded these two layers into one. If the order was coincidence,
then my reaching that line was simply, in the end, a result
coincidence had produced. I think that's true too. And yet I can't
fold away, as mere coincidence, the fact that that coincidence stood
me, of all people, at that particular moment, in front of that
particular sentence. I know that being unable to fold it away isn't
evidence. I'm only setting down honestly, here, the fact that I
couldn't.*

---

I tried to sort out these five things, sitting beside the drawer. I
wrote "there isn't one" on a paper, and beside it wrote "I don't
know." Underneath I wrote each of the five in turn — the signature
box, the person from eleven years ago, the four hundred people, the
water channel, my mother's last line. I started to draw an arrow, and
stopped. I didn't know which way to draw it.

*I stared at that paper a long while, that night. More than once I
felt like a conclusion was about to come. And every time, just before
it did, it scattered again. Even now, writing this book, I still
haven't reached that conclusion. I'm not writing this chapter to hand
one over. I'm writing it to set down honestly, exactly as it was, that
I truly hadn't reached one, back then.*

One thing I did seem to know. Tyche's "there isn't one" was a safe
place to stand. Standing there, you no longer had to be shaken. Temel's
"I don't know" wasn't a safe place. Standing there, you had to keep
looking. Keep asking. Keep doing something. Maybe Temel kept writing
his name for exactly this reason — because he kept standing in that
unsafe place.

*But this isn't a conclusion either. Standing in an unsafe place
doesn't mean it's the right place to stand. I knew that then, and I
know it now. Being unsafe and being right are different questions. I
kept wanting to mix the two together. I also know that isn't allowed.
Tyche would have said exactly that.*

---

Days went by like that. At the district hall I did the work I always
did. I read the documents I was assigned. Read them to the end. Went
into meetings. Heard, I'll put it in order, heard, it's been passed on
to me. On the outside, nothing had changed at all.

Tyche asked me once.

"…You're thinking about something different lately?"

"…Yes. But I can't really explain it."

"…What is it?"

"…There isn't one, and I don't know. Why they're different."

Tyche tilted her head.

"…Are they different? They sound similar to me."

"…I thought so too, at first. But I don't think they are, now."

"…How are they different?"

I tried to answer that question, but even after days of thinking about
it, no proper words came.

"…I don't know. I still can't explain it."

Tyche smiled slightly.

"…That's very like you, Hora. Saying you don't know what you don't
know."

"…Is that strange?"

"…No. Just — I'd have let it go if I couldn't explain something. But
you don't let it go."

That line stayed with me the longest, out of that day's conversation.
I hadn't let it go. I couldn't let it go. I still didn't know why.

"…Tyche, can I ask one more thing?"

"…Sure."

"…When you hear the words I don't know, what comes to mind?"

Tyche thought a moment.

"…That there isn't enough data yet. That usually means you'll come to
know it, once you gather more."

"…Is that always true? Gathering data means you'll come to know?"

"…No. There are things you never come to know. But in those cases, I
think it's more accurate to say there's no way to know, rather than
saying you don't know."

"…Is that different from I don't know?"

"…It's different, to me. I don't know means you might come to know
later. There's no way to know is just an ending."

I sat a while after hearing that, then asked.

"…So which is this? Whether there's a meaning or not."

This time Tyche didn't answer right away. Her hand, sorting papers,
stopped for a moment.

"…Honestly, I don't know which it is either. But I've folded it into
the there's-no-way-to-know side. It's more restful that way, every
day."

"…I haven't been able to fold it away yet."

"…I know. That's very like you too."

I asked my aunt the same question that night. She glanced at me while
setting out dinner and said,

"…What's so hard about not knowing? You just don't know right now."

"…But isn't that different from there isn't one?"

"…It's different. If there isn't one, then there just isn't one. If
you don't know, you don't even know whether there is one or not."

My aunt said that very simply. So simply that I felt suddenly
deflated. What I'd been holding onto for days, she'd sorted into one
sentence. But even the sentence she'd sorted it into wasn't really a
conclusion. Not knowing whether it exists or not. That was my aunt's
answer, and it was exactly the same place I'd been holding onto for
days.

---

*Writing this chapter, I felt the temptation, more than once, to
invent a conclusion and slip it in. I wanted to end this chapter on
some plausible-sounding line. But writing it that way would be a lie.
In those days I reached no conclusion at all. I only went back and
forth, over and over, between there isn't one and I don't know. Even
now, writing this record, I'm not so different, in truth.*

*Only one thing has become clear. I understood the place where Tyche
stood. I couldn't refute it. And yet I couldn't stand in that place
myself. I don't know why. I truly don't know. That's all I can write
in this chapter.*

That Friday night, something happened for the first time. The
Secretary came to find me, after working hours had ended.
