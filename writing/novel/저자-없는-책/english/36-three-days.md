# Chapter 36. Three Days

> _Previously_ — I met, in person, one of the Seventh Classification
> I had only ever known by number. That person had a name. I heard
> that name and passed it on to no one. I didn't put it in the
> drawer. That night I shaped it silently with my lips, without any
> sound.

The next morning, getting ready for the district hall as usual, I
looked at the documents stacked on my desk. Three new matters had
been assigned the day before. I picked one up, and set it back down.

I didn't want to read it.

*Writing this sentence, I still find it strange. Through this whole
book I've introduced myself as "someone who reads." Reading was the
one thing I was good at. And that morning, I didn't want to do the one
thing.*

Why I felt that way, I still can't explain in a single sentence, even
now. It probably had something to do with how much I'd heard in just
a few days. Tyche's there isn't one, Temel's I don't know, the
Secretary's convening from eleven years ago, and now that name behind
a number. I hadn't come to know any of it by reading. Most of it I'd
come to know by hearing it, or living through it. And yet I felt
myself wanting to sort it all back into something like a document, and
I didn't like that in myself.

*I don't know exactly why I disliked that urge to sort things, either.
Sorting isn't a bad thing. I've gotten through, all this time, by
sorting. It's only that, that morning, I wanted to choose leaving
things be, over sorting them. If you asked me for a reason, I couldn't
give one. That's the honest start of this chapter.*

My aunt saw this and asked,

"…What's wrong, are you not feeling well?"

"…No. I just don't want to read, today."

My aunt wasn't especially surprised.

"…All right. Then rest."

I hadn't meant to rest. I didn't know exactly what I meant to do,
then, either. My hand simply wouldn't go to the document.

---

The first day, I did nothing at all. I sent word to the district hall
that I wasn't feeling well. That wasn't a lie. I couldn't explain
exactly what was wrong, but something was wrong, truly.

At home that day, my eyes went to the documents on the desk more than
once. I stopped my hand from reaching for them, more than once. I
learned, that day, for the first time, how strong a habit could be.

With nothing to do, I looked for something I could do with my hands. I
sat beside my aunt and helped with her sewing. Threaded needles,
sewed on buttons. While I concentrated on my fingertips, thoughts of
letters came less often. My aunt seemed to find it curious that I was
sitting beside her, and glanced at me more than once.

Around noon I went out to the back garden and repotted some plants.
While I handled the soil, my hands stopped thinking of other things.
With soil on my hands, I couldn't touch paper anyway. I liked that. It
felt like my body was, on its own, keeping me away from reading.

"…You don't usually do this."

"…I wanted to, today."

"…Bored?"

"…No. I just wanted to keep my hands moving at something."

My aunt didn't ask further and handed me another piece of sewing. I
took it and focused a long while on the sound of thread passing
through cloth.

Tyche stopped by that evening, worried about me.

"…Are you sick somewhere?"

"…No. I'm just not going to read anything today."

"…Why?"

I couldn't answer that right away.

"…I don't know. I just want to."

Tyche looked at me a moment and said,

"…That's not like you, Hora."

"…I know."

*Saying that, I truly felt that I wasn't like myself. And at the same
time, I felt this was the most like myself thing I could do right now.
That the two feelings could exist at once was the strangest part of
that night.*

---

On the second day, I learned that not reading anything was far
harder than I'd expected.

Out on the street, letters caught my eye everywhere. A notice in a
shop window, a bulletin on a wall, a paper in a passerby's hand. I
turned my eyes away on purpose to avoid reading them. I hadn't known
how hard that was until I decided not to read.

It was harder still at home. I kept thinking of the twelve pages my
mother had left, of the papers in that drawer. I didn't open the
drawer. If I opened it, I would surely end up reading something.

On the way back from the distribution station, I walked with my eyes
half lowered. Notices, announcements, posted bulletins. All of it
looked as though it were trying to say something to me. I worked to
take in none of it, and that cost far more energy than I'd expected.
By the time I got home I was far more exhausted than usual, though I
hadn't done any physical work at all.

*I thought hard that night about where this exhaustion had come from.
My conclusion was that not reading was, in the end, an act in itself.
It wasn't doing nothing — it was continuing to choose not to read.
Choosing takes effort. I hadn't known that, until then. I'd always
thought reading was simply something I did without thinking.*

*What these three days meant to me, I didn't know exactly, at the
time. I don't know exactly now, writing this either. But I know one
thing. In these three days I decided nothing. I didn't do anything
you could call prayer. I never once asked to be shown something. I
simply didn't read.*

My aunt asked on the evening of the second day.

"…Are you waiting for something?"

"…No."

"…Then why are you like this?"

"…I don't really know myself. It just feels like not reading is the
right thing right now."

My aunt didn't press further, hearing that. Instead she spooned an
extra scoop of rice into my bowl.

---

On the third day, I ran out of things to do inside the house. I
cleaned, folded laundry, and once all that was done, the afternoon
was left.

I went outside and walked along the water channel. The water was still
making its sound. I listened to it. Listening to a sound wasn't
reading.

*Through these three days, trying not to look at letters, I ended up
listening to sound a great deal instead. The water's sound, people's
voices, the noise of the market. Things that were usually drowned out
by letters came through unusually loud, during those three days.*

Passing through the market, I walked by the notice board. It was the
same spot where Temel used to stand. A few sheets were posted there. I
walked by without reading them. My steps slowed a little, though.

People were still sitting on the bench by the water, feet in the
current. I sat there too, for a moment. I didn't take off my shoes. I
just sat and listened to the sound of the water.

*Sitting on that bench, I felt, for the first time in a long while,
what it was to do nothing at all. Not reading, not worrying — just
sitting and listening to water. I realized then how rare a time like
that had been for me. I was always reading something, or thinking
about what I'd read, or choosing what to read next.*

An old man sitting nearby spoke to me.

"…Do you come here often, miss?"

"…I've been coming often, lately."

"…Something good must be happening. You look at ease."

"…Not something good, exactly. I'm just taking a few days off."

"…Resting's the best thing there is."

The old man said that and looked back at the water. I turned that
line over in my mind. Resting's the best thing there is. I didn't
fully agree with it, but somehow, that day, I didn't want to argue
either.

I sat a long while longer, listening to the water brush over the top
of my feet. The sun leaned a little lower. No one hurried me.

---

I ran into the Secretary by chance, in front of the front desk.

"…You haven't been in for a few days, Hora."

"…No. Personal matters."

"…Are you all right?"

"…Yes. I'm just trying a few days without reading."

The Secretary looked at me a moment, hearing that. He didn't ask what
it meant.

"…Then go on that way. When you're back, I'll help you catch up on
what's piled up."

"…Thank you."

That was all. The Secretary didn't press for the reason, and I didn't
explain it. This short exchange, that day, felt strangely restful to
me.

Turning to go, the Secretary added one more thing.

"…Hora."

"…Yes?"

"…Not reading anything at all seems like its own kind of way, too."

Saying that, the Secretary went back inside the front desk. I stood a
long while, trying to work out what he meant. I couldn't tell whether
it was meant as comfort, or simply an observation. Probably, for this
man, the two weren't different things.

---

That evening, the third day, Tyche came again.

"…Are you done now? It's been three days."

"…You were counting?"

"…Yes. I was curious."

"…I still don't really know. But I'm going to stop after tonight."

"…What's changed, not reading?"

I thought a long while before answering.

"…Nothing in particular came into view. I just learned how much I've
been living leaning on reading."

"…Is that bad?"

"…No, it isn't bad. But leaning on it, and being able to get through
without it — those seemed like different questions to me."

Tyche nodded.

"…If you found that out, then three days wasn't wasted."

*I still don't quite know whether I can call these three days a fast.
I asked for nothing. There was no revelation. The sky didn't open, and
no one spoke to me. For three days, I simply didn't do the one thing I
was best at.*

Before leaving, Tyche asked,

"…But why three days, specifically? Not four, not two."

"…I don't know. Three just felt right."

"…On what basis?"

"…None. It was just a feeling."

Tyche laughed at that.

"…It's been a while since I heard you say there's no basis for
something."

"…I know. It's strange to me too."

*Writing this conversation, I laugh again. Tyche knew I was someone
who always tried to give a basis for everything. That I'd chosen three
days with no basis at all must have struck her as funny. It did me
too.*

My aunt said one thing at my door that night before bed.

"…You'll go back to reading tomorrow?"

"…Yes."

"…Good, then. I figured you couldn't keep it up much longer anyway."

"…I thought so too."

My aunt gave a short laugh and closed the door. I lay in the dark and
went back over those three days. Nothing special had happened. I
simply hadn't read for three days, and for those three days the world
had gone on turning exactly as always. The water channel had kept
flowing, the market had kept bustling, and the signature box had
stayed blank, I imagined, the whole time. Whether I read or not, none
of it waited for me. That fact didn't make me feel small, and it
didn't make me feel large either. It only let me know that this was
so.

---

On the morning of the fourth day, I picked up the document on my desk
again. My hand felt a little unfamiliar. Like something I hadn't
held in a long time.

I read the first line. The familiar feeling came right back. I read
that document to the end, as I always did. There was nothing special
about it. It was an ordinary matter.

Reading it, I noticed something strange. Three days of rest hadn't
given me some new pair of eyes. I read at the same speed as before,
still read to the end, still looked for the signature box first. Not a
single thing seemed to have changed.

*But there was exactly one small difference. Before, reading had been
something like breathing to me. It just happened on its own. Now,
reading felt like something I was choosing again, every single time.
If you stop breathing, you die. Reading was something I could live
without. I'd confirmed that in my body, over those three days. Which
meant that what I was doing now, reading, was something I'd chosen to
do even though I could have not done it.*

*This might sound like a grand realization. But I don't want to write
it that way. Just as a person doesn't suddenly become someone else
after three days without food, I didn't become someone else after
three days without reading. I'd simply done, again, after not doing
it, the thing I'd always done. Beyond that, truly, nothing happened at
all.*

*I feel the temptation to write that these three days took me nowhere.
But that doesn't seem exactly right either. I was still myself, three
days later. Only, over those three days, I confirmed — not without
choosing, but by choosing — for the first time, that I was someone who
reads. Whether that's a small difference or a large one, I still don't
know exactly, even now.*

Having read the document to the end, I looked at the signature box at
the bottom. Blank. As always.

I looked at that box a long while. A little longer than before.

Tyche, sorting papers at the next seat, looked over at me.

"…How does it feel, reading again?"

"…The same. But the same feels strange, somehow."

"…What does?"

"…Nothing changed in three days, and yet I feel like I confirmed
something."

"…What did you confirm?"

I answered without taking my eyes off the signature box.

"…That I keep looking at this. That I didn't have to, and I keep
looking at it anyway."

Tyche didn't say much in response to that. She just nodded once and
turned her eyes back to her papers. I found that silence restful.

*Recording these three days, I've decided to attach no ending to this
chapter. The temptation came, more than once, to write it as though
something great had begun. But that isn't true. What I did, after
three days, was simply pick up a document. Only, that hand, the same
hand as three days before, felt slightly different to me. I don't yet
have the words to explain that difference.*
