# Chapter 42. Review

> _Previously_ — Hora writes her name in the signature box. The
> reception desk stamps it and files the paper. Nothing happens. That
> quiet goes on for several days.

On the sixth day, I received my first summons.

A notice came. The same kind of paper as when I'd first received the
work guidelines. *You are requested to appear for the reexamination of
the deliberation concerning the Seventh Classification. Date: three
days from now.*

*Receiving this notice, at first, actually put me at ease. That
something had started felt better than nothing happening at all. How
naive that ease was, I already learned that very evening.*

My aunt saw the notice and asked.

"…What's this?"

"…A deliberation reexamination. It says to appear."

My aunt read the notice once more.

"…So it's starting now."

"…Yeah. I guess."

She said nothing more. That night I read the notice again, many times
over. The words *three days from now* stuck in my eye especially.

*I didn't know what to prepare during those three days, so I just spent
them as usual. There wasn't really anything to prepare. Why I'd read
that document to the end, why I'd signed it — those were already
established facts. There was nothing new to make up.*

---

Three days later I went to the assigned room.

It was a small room. One table, three chairs. Two people sat across
from me. Faces I'd never seen before.

"…We'll confirm a few things regarding the proposal for a new
deliberation on the Seventh Classification matter."

"…Yes."

"…Would you state your reason for proposing it."

I spoke as I'd prepared. I pointed to a few pieces of evidence, and I
spoke about the gap between the summary and the full text. The two of
them wrote it down.

"…Confirmed. Thank you."

That was all. It took about twenty minutes.

Coming out, I saw someone else in the hallway. That person, too, was
coming out of a room, the same as me. Somewhat older. Our eyes met, and
that person nodded to me first.

"…First time?"

"…Yes."

"…You'll get used to it."

That person said only that and walked on. I couldn't tell, at the time,
whether that was comfort or a warning.

*Looking back now, I think it was both. In this city, comfort and
warning often come in the same sentence.*

I never met that person again. I've written before that meeting the
same person twice in review is rare — the same was true among the
people undergoing review with me. A different face every time. Whether
this was chance, or itself some kind of design, I never confirmed, in
the end. Confirming it would have meant going and asking someone else
again, and by then I didn't have much energy left for that.

---

*I thought this was the end of it. I'd stated it, it had been accepted,
and I thought I'd move on to the next stage.*

*I was wrong.*

Ten days later, another notice came. A different room this time,
different people. I was asked the same questions again. I answered the
same way.

"…We're confirming whether this matches your earlier statement."

"…Yes."

This one, too, took about twenty minutes.

*At this second review, I noticed something strange for the first time.
Both the room and the people were different, but the questions were
identical, down to the last word. "Would you state your reason for
proposing it." I learned, then, that this was a line fixed like a
script. The people in charge changed, but the question didn't.*

*In that moment I felt this was a cold system, but by the time I left
the room, I'd revised the thought. It wasn't cold. It was just fair.
That the same question is asked no matter who conducts it. I realized
this was the same way as everything else in the Equity. A way in which
no one takes sides — that same way was being applied to review too.*

---

Next, another notice came, not in ten days but in six.

I went again. I was asked the same questions again. I gave the same
answers again.

*The third time I went, I asked something for the first time.*

"…How many more times will this happen?"

The person across from me glanced at a screen for a moment and
answered.

"…There is no set number. You'll be convened whenever it's needed."

"…Who decides when it's needed?"

"…That's the procedure."

I didn't ask any further. I already knew that asking further wouldn't
bring a different answer.

On my way home I counted my review notices for the first time. I'd been
collecting them in the drawer each time one arrived, and by that day
there were three. Laying the three side by side, the gaps between the
dates stood out to me. Ten days, then six. No pattern.

*I learned that day that the lack of a pattern was, if anything, more
of a weight to carry. If the interval had been fixed, I could have
predicted the next date and prepared my mind for it. Because there was
no pattern, I started each morning thinking a notice might arrive that
day.*

---

*The Secretary had once explained review to me, long ago. It was not
long after I'd first met Temel. What Temel went through. Not
punishment, but review. I thought, at the time, that I'd understood
that explanation. I hadn't.*

*Understanding something and living through it are different. I only
know the difference in my body now. When I was told about it, review
was a single fact. Living through it, review becomes a rhythm. Six
days, ten days, six days again. Being called out again and again at
unpredictable intervals.*

---

I met different people at every review. A different face every time. I
almost never met the same person twice.

*I learned later that this wasn't coincidence. The people conducting
review were meant to change every time as well. If one person kept
handling me, there was a risk that person would become the one
responsible. A structure meant to keep anyone from becoming responsible
was being applied even to the people who called me in.*

*I couldn't bring myself to hate this structure. Because the person to
hate kept changing. This was how this city treated me. A way of
offering no place for anger to land.*

*At first I thought this was a cunning move by the Consensus. A
structure that blocked resistance by never giving you anyone to be
angry at. But thinking about it again, this wasn't cunning — it was
simply this city's founding principle, applied to review in exactly the
same way. The principle that no one takes sides, and no one remains
the responsibility of any one particular individual. I wrote, near the
very beginning of this record, that I thought this principle was good.
Even now, I can't fully overturn that judgment.*

Once, during a review, one of the people in charge said something
personal to me.

"…Why did you do this? You could have just lived quietly."

I wasn't prepared to answer that question. That person didn't seem to
be waiting for an answer either. They just seemed to be curious.

"…No reason, really."

"…All right. Let's move to the next question."

*I remember this short exchange for a long time. That person didn't
blame me. They didn't sound like they couldn't understand, either.
They were simply curious, took my short answer as it was, and moved
on. The people of this city were mostly like that. They didn't press.*

---

One day Tyche was waiting for me as I came out of a review.

"…Went again?"

"…Yeah."

"…What did they ask?"

"…The same thing. Reason. Evidence."

"…Same every time?"

"…Almost."

Tyche walked in silence for a moment, then asked.

"…Is it hard?"

I thought for a moment before answering.

"…It's not hard, exactly. It's strange. No one's angry at me, and yet
I keep being called in."

"…That's review. I understand now, why people are afraid of it."

Tyche told me, that day, about what she'd looked into herself.

"…I looked at the statistics on review counts. On average people are
called in two or three times a month. But the variance is big. Some
people go months with nothing, then suddenly get called in over and
over."

"…Why does that happen?"

"…There's no explanation given. It's just marked as procedure."

"…I know. Everything's procedure."

Tyche laughed a little.

"…That's this city's way, isn't it."

*Tyche wasn't trying to persuade me that day either. She just brought
me a fact as it was. I was grateful for that. More than words trying to
comfort me, accurate information helped me more, at that moment.*

---

*Around this time I started thinking about Temel again. He'd been going
through this for years. I was already starting to wear down at six-day,
ten-day intervals, and Temel had been called in far longer, and far
more often, than that.*

*I thought I understood, now, why he'd seemed so unruffled. He wasn't
unruffled — he'd simply grown used to it. He must have had no choice
but to get used to it. You can't keep being freshly startled, every
time, by something that never ends.*

Around then I thought about writing Temel a letter. I didn't know where
he was now, and I had no real way to send a letter anyway. In the end I
didn't write one.

*If I'm honest about why I didn't write it: I didn't want to be
comforted by him. Whatever he might have answered, I knew, in the end,
that I had to go through this alone. It must have been the same for
him. Review wasn't something anyone could go through in your place.
That no one can take it on for you might be the most exact definition
of review there is.*

---

My aunt said nothing every time I came back from a review. She made
meals, and I ate them.

One evening she said just one thing.

"…Tell me if it's too hard."

"…I told you, it's not hard."

"…Then what is it."

I couldn't answer. She didn't ask any further either.

*At the time, I couldn't find the right word for my own state. Hard
wasn't the right word. Frightening wasn't quite right either. The
closest word was probably "ongoing." It just went on. That was the
nature of review.*

Even on days with nothing scheduled, I found myself habitually checking
the notice box. The first thing I did on waking each morning was look
at that box. If it was empty I felt relief; if there was something in
it, I just accepted it.

*I only noticed this habit had formed after the fact. At first it was
something I did without being conscious of it. One day it struck me
that checking the notice box had become the very first thing I did
every morning. If reading had once been my old habit, waiting had
become my new one.*

*My aunt seemed to have noticed this habit too. One morning, seeing me
walk toward the notice box, she said quietly, "…Nothing today. I
already checked." I was so grateful for those words that I couldn't
say anything back.*

*I thought about these two habits side by side. The habit of reading
had been something I chose. The habit of waiting had formed without
my choosing it. Both had made me who I was.*

*I might well have been angry that a habit I hadn't chosen had formed
in me, but strangely, I wasn't. This too was a result of having signed
it. When I signed it, I hadn't foreseen this habit specifically, but
just because an unforeseen result had come, I didn't want to take the
signature back.*

---

Review did not hurt the body. It did not lock anyone in a cell. It did
not take anyone's money.

The one thing review did was call people in. And not stop doing so.

*I understood, around then, why this was harder to bear than
punishment. Punishment is built on the premise that there's an end.
Serve your term, and it's over. Review isn't built on the premise of
an end. As long as even one case with my name on it remained, a reason
to call me in remained too.*

*And in this city, deliberations keep coming into being. So, in
theory, I could be called in forever.*

*It took me time to accept this fact. At first I felt it was unjust.
Having no visible end was a shape unlike any procedure I'd known
before. Nearly every procedure in this city has a start and an end.
Deliberations begin and end in confirmation. But review had no
confirmation. It was a structure that couldn't be confirmed.*

*I came to see this differently, later. At that point I hadn't reached
that place yet. Back then I was simply enduring this endlessness.*

About a month in, I felt my body starting to adapt to the rhythm of
review. When a notice arrived, I no longer felt startled. I just
checked the next date.

*I couldn't judge, at the time, whether this adapting was good or bad.
I'm still not sure, even now. Adapting means it has become bearable, but
it also means it has become normal. I didn't want to accept being
called in over and over as normal. But my body was already accepting
it.*

About a month and a half in, coming out of my eighth review, I stopped
and counted, on impulse. Eight times. Every one a different person,
every one the same question.

*The number eight, by itself, isn't very large. But if I added up the
mornings I spent, between those eight, checking the notice box every
day, that became a far larger number. Review's real weight lay less in
the time actually spent being called in, and more in the time spent
waiting, wondering if I might be. I understood this exactly, around
then.*

Around then, Tyche said something to me.

"…We still don't know if this will lead to a new deliberation. It could
just be the procedure continuing on its own."

"…I know."

"…And you'll keep going anyway?"

"…Yeah."

Tyche didn't ask anything further.

*I remember this short exchange for a long time. Tyche didn't try to
stop me. She didn't cheer me on either. She just told me one fact,
heard my answer, and let it go. That was Tyche's way of dealing with
people. I didn't feel, at the time, that it was cold.*

*I never stopped counting after that, either. The moment I stopped
counting, it felt like this would stop being something I'd chosen and
become just something that was happening to me. I kept adding those
numbers to the end of the paper in the drawer.*
