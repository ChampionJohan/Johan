# Chapter 29. Tyche's Logic

> _Previously_ — I watched Temel go in and out of the confirmation
> rooms. No fine, no prison. Only, it never ended. Tyche called it
> "just probability rising," and I couldn't refute her.

That afternoon, sorting district materials, I was left alone with
Tyche. The meeting hall was quiet. Everyone else had gone ahead, and
several unsorted folders were still stacked on the desk. Tyche brought
it up without much thought, while sorting papers.

"…You talk about that man a lot lately, Hora."

"…Temel?"

"…Yes. You go to the market every day, I hear."

"…There's a lot I'm curious about."

Tyche turned a page of the document in her hand and said,

"…I find that a little strange. What's there to be so curious about?
You've seen everything he does — writes his name, gets called in.
That's all there is."

---

That stung a little, hearing it. But there wasn't really a reason for
it to sting. Tyche wasn't angry. She was genuinely asking out of
curiosity.

Tyche had been born and raised in this city. Her parents had been born
here too, I'd heard. She hadn't been assigned here suddenly one day
like me — she'd grown up going in and out of the district hall since
she was small. Maybe because of that, Tyche seemed to have rarely
doubted this city's ways. That she didn't doubt them didn't mean she
was unthinking. It was the opposite. Tyche examined things more
carefully than anyone I knew. It was only that what she found, more
often than not, matched how this city worked.

"…I don't think that's all there is."

"…What else is there?"

"…I don't know. I just want to know why he lives that way."

Tyche stopped what she was doing and looked at me.

"…You keep trying to find a reason, Hora, but I don't think there is
one."

"…There isn't?"

"…No. He just lives that way. Maybe he learned it that way as a
child, or it worked for him in some city, or maybe it's just habit.
The reasons are all on that level. Nothing grand."

---

*I wanted to refute Tyche, at this point. But I had nothing to refute
her with. Everything she'd said could all be true. Temel himself had
said, "It's just habit, I suppose."*

I thought of the names I'd kept in the drawer.

"…What about that person we found in the reading room, then? The one
who wrote a name eleven years ago."

Tyche tilted her head.

"…I heard about that. Same thing. That person was just there, a
situation came up that called for an objection, and they wrote their
name. Not because they were someone special — the person who happened
to be there did what they did."

"…What about the record stopping afterward, though? That doesn't get
explained."

"…It didn't stop — it's just not there, Hora, you said so yourself.
Not there is just not there. They could have moved. Could have
changed their name. You're the one attaching meaning to it, not the
record."

I was caught wordless for a moment, hearing that. Tyche was right. I
had attached the meaning. The record had simply ended.

"…But Tyche, why are you so sure of that? That there's no reason?"

Tyche thought a moment and said,

"…It's not that I'm sure — it's just the simplest explanation. I
think of the simple reason before the complicated one. Usually the
simple one turns out right."

"…Always?"

"…Nearly always. That's what I've seen working here for years. Even
things that look strange turn out to be nothing much, once you dig
into them. A vacancy, a number, probability. That explains almost
everything."

---

Since it had come up, I asked what I'd been wanting to ask.

"…So is that true of my being in this seat too? Just a vacancy, a
number, probability?"

Tyche nodded.

"…Yes. Exactly that."

"…No other reason?"

"…None. The person before you quit, your number came up on the
roster, and so you sat down. It's the same for me. Me coming here as a
deliberation assistant — the person before me transferred elsewhere,
the seat opened, and I happened to apply right then. That's all it
was."

"…That's all?"

"…Yes. I've wished, myself, that there was some special reason I'm
here. But when you actually look, there isn't. There was just a
vacancy, and I fit it."

I looked out the window for a moment. The afternoon sun slanted in
across the stack of papers. It seemed like even this small thing ran
by this city's order, the way everything else did.

"…What about the water channel? Is that probability too?"

"…The water channel isn't even a matter yet, so there's nothing to
explain about it. But if I had to say — that's probably nothing much
either. Water flowed from somewhere, followed the land, and ended up
here. Nobody sent it here."

"…You're sure?"

"…No, not sure. But it's the most plausible explanation. Compared to
someone deliberately building a channel to send water all the way to
this city, water just flowing downhill and ending up here is much
simpler."

I nodded at that. Tyche was right. I'd confirmed myself, more than
once, in this city, that the simple explanation was usually the true
one. Why the signature box was blank, why four hundred people were
involved — none of it, once you traced it through, turned out to be
some complex conspiracy. It was just that the procedure was built that
way.

---

The sorting had finished by then. Tyche pulled out the next folder and
went on talking. Hearing this answer, I felt something strange. Tyche
spoke about herself without any bitterness at all. She was calm about
it. That held me even more.

"…So doesn't that make you sad, Tyche? Being here for no reason at
all?"

Tyche smiled a little at the question. Not mockingly — it was a truly
gentle smile.

"…Do you think it would? I'm not sad about it."

"…Why not?"

"…There being no reason doesn't make the work I'm doing now
meaningless. I like the work I'm doing now. Why I came here and what
I'm doing here are different questions."

*That was the first time I heard that distinction stated plainly. Why
you came and what you do being different questions. Tyche said it as
though it were nothing at all, but I held onto that line for a long
time.*

"…Then what gets you through the day, Tyche? If there's no reason."

"…Having no reason doesn't mean there's nothing to get through the
day on. I just get through it liking what I'm doing today. I liked
sorting deliberation materials yesterday, and I'll do work like that
today too. That's enough."

"…Enough?"

"…Yes. I don't want much. Not some grand purpose or anything. If
today goes well, I'm satisfied with that."

"…That sounds a little lonely, to me."

Tyche smiled slightly at that. It wasn't a teasing smile.

"…Does it? I find it restful, actually. Watching people struggle to
find some grand purpose looks harder to me. It seems like they must
always feel something's missing."

"…Me?"

"…You're a little like that too, Hora. You keep thinking there must
be something more."

I couldn't answer that. It wasn't wrong.

---

"…But Tyche, I have thoughts like this sometimes. If all of this is
just probability, isn't that a little empty?"

Tyche stopped sorting the papers and looked straight at me. For the
first time in this conversation, I watched her seriously choosing her
words.

"…Hora — I know you want there to be a meaning. So do I."

I was surprised, hearing that. I hadn't thought Tyche carried a feeling
like that.

"…But?"

"…But wanting there to be one isn't evidence that there is."

---

I said nothing for a long while, hearing that.

Tyche went on.

"…I heard things like that a lot growing up. That there's a reason
this city is a place where nobody's wronged, that it can't be chance.
My grandmother used to say things like that too."

"…Why did your grandmother think that?"

"…I don't know. She just did. I grew up hearing it, and as I got
older I kept checking it — whether things had turned out this way
because there really was a meaning, or whether they'd simply turned
out this way and people had attached a meaning to it afterward."

"…What did you find?"

"…Almost always the latter. A vacancy opens and someone fills it; a
procedure exists and things go by the procedure. It was all explained,
without any meaning attached."

"…So your grandmother was wrong?"

"…Not wrong, exactly. My grandmother said it because she wanted there
to be a meaning. I understand that feeling. I have it too."

*I remember this line exactly, even now. Word for word, without a
single mistake. Tyche wasn't saying this to win against me. She wasn't
trying to persuade me either. She was simply saying what she honestly
thought.*

"…So do you just throw that wish away, then, Tyche?"

"…I don't throw it away. I just don't use it as evidence. A wish is a
wish, and a fact is a fact. I don't want to mix the two."

"…Is it bad to mix them?"

"…If I mix them, I end up seeing only what I want to see. That scares
me. Picking out only the things that look like they have meaning, and
not seeing the things that don't. In the end that's nothing more than
making up the story I want, myself."

---

That held me for a long time. Tyche wasn't cynical. If anything, she
was a very careful person. Someone working, every single time, not to
mix what she wanted with what was true.

"…So you don't believe in anything, Tyche?"

"…No. I believe what's worth believing. That nobody gets wronged when
four hundred people are involved — I believe that. Because it's
actually true. There's evidence for it."

"…If there's ever evidence that there's a meaning, will you believe
that too, someday?"

Tyche thought a moment.

"…If there is, sure. There isn't yet."

"…What could count as evidence?"

Tyche's face turned genuinely thoughtful.

"…I don't know. Honestly, that's the problem for me too — I can't
settle on it. I can't decide what standard would let me believe
there's a meaning."

"…What if there's no standard?"

"…If there's no standard, then no matter how strange something is, I
just end up explaining it away as a strange coincidence. That bothers
me a little too. I wonder whether I'll end up not believing anything,
no matter what I see."

That answer surprised me. I hadn't known Tyche held this much doubt
about her own logic.

"…So you waver sometimes too, Tyche?"

"…Not waver, exactly — I know there could be a hole in my way of
thinking. But I can't just believe anything to fill that hole. That
seems more dangerous, to me."

---

"…Dangerous?"

"…Yes. If I go grabbing at any story to fill one hole, eventually I
won't even be able to tell what's real from what I made up myself.
That's the thing I'm most afraid of."

Hearing that, I looked at Tyche differently. She'd seemed fearless, but
she had a fear of her own kind. It just pointed in a different
direction than mine. I was afraid there might be no meaning at all.
Tyche was afraid of believing there was a meaning where there wasn't
one.

*Writing this passage, I feel again how careful a person Tyche was.
That's why she's hard to describe in this book. She wasn't a person
who didn't believe — she was a person afraid of believing wrongly.*

That evening, walking home, I kept turning this conversation over. I
wanted to find something to say that would beat Tyche. I couldn't find
it. There wasn't a single wrong thing in anything she'd said.

*Writing this chapter, I tried again to find something to refute Tyche
with. I still can't. Tyche was right. Most things really were
explained by a vacancy, a number, probability. I don't deny that.
Only, I could never quite bring myself to believe that explanation was
everything. I don't know why, myself. Not because I have evidence.*

*Tyche would have smiled at that sentence. "See — there's no
evidence." She'd be right. I still have no answer to that, even now.*

I told my aunt about this conversation, and she asked,

"…So what did you say?"

"…I couldn't say anything."

"…Why not?"

"…Because she was right."

My aunt thought a moment and said,

"…Hora, being right doesn't always mean it's a good thing to hear."

"…What does that mean?"

"…Just that — being right doesn't mean it sets your mind at ease."

I thought about that a long time under the blankets that night. Tyche
wasn't wrong. But her being right didn't set me at ease. I learned for
the first time then that the two could go together.

*Writing this chapter, I confirm again that I don't want to paint
Tyche badly. Tyche wasn't trying to persuade me. She was only saying
what she saw. And after that day, I came to like Tyche even more than
before. It sounds strange, but it was true. It was restful, being
beside someone honest.*

*I don't intend, in this whole book, to try to win against Tyche. It
doesn't seem to be a matter of winning or losing. Tyche is right, and
I feel that I'm right. Both were real. How the two are meant to stand
together, I still don't know, now any more than then.*

The next morning, I decided to read a document together with Tyche. I
was curious what would look different, reading the same document side
by side. Tyche agreed without much hesitation.
