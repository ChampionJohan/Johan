# Chapter 1. The Vacancy

There was no reason at all for how I came to sit in that seat.

That's the first thing anyone reading this record should know, so I'm putting it in the first line. I wasn't chosen. I wasn't recommended. I never sat an exam. The person who'd been sitting there quit, and the next number on the roster was mine. That's all there is to it.

*I tried, later, to rewrite that sentence any number of times. Meaning I tried to find a slightly more plausible reason. I couldn't. So I decided to leave it as it was. There are a few things in this book I left uncorrected, and this is the first one.*

---

I should talk about the Equity first.

This is a city where nobody gets wronged. That isn't something the city says about itself — it's something anyone who's lived here says. I say it too. I still say it.

Nobody stands in line. There's no one you need to know. The loud never win and the quiet never get pushed aside. In sixteen years of living here I've never once seen a person ask another person for a favor. There's no one to ask.

It's easier to understand if I explain how the word "favor" is used in this city. We only use "please" when handing something over. Please pass the salt. Please hold the door. It doesn't go beyond that. There's no occasion for it to. There's no way here of talking to a person to make something happen, or not happen.

I grew up thinking this was normal. When I was fourteen, I remember a kid who'd come from outside saying, "I'll talk to my uncle about it," and not understanding what she meant. What happens if you talk to your uncle? She heard that question and looked at me for a long time.

People from outside can't stand this at first. They say people get angry because they have nowhere to direct their anger. Then about half a year in, they go quiet. Not because they've realized there's nowhere to direct it, but because there really is less to be angry about.

*This isn't said with irony. I never once came to hate this city. If you read this record to the end, you'll see — I never managed to hate it to the end. It would have been much easier if I could have.*

What runs this city is the **Consensus**.

To explain what the Consensus is, I'd have to talk about other cities, and I've never been to another city, so I'll only write what I've heard. In some cities, they say, there's something that measures people. Weighs them, or ranks their standing, or puts a price on them. So in that city, they said, people split into those who measure and those who are measured.

The Consensus doesn't measure. What the Consensus does is gather.

When a case comes up, the Consensus gathers everything connected to it. Records, precedent, opinion, objection, statistics. It gathers it all and produces the will of the whole. That becomes the decision.

Which means no one hands down the decision. It's the will of the whole. And the whole includes me, and it includes my next-door neighbor, and it includes people I've never once seen.

*I first heard this explained at school when I was ten. At the time I thought it was an extremely humble system. Because it doesn't claim to be right. Because it doesn't call itself a judgment.*

*I still don't think that thought was entirely wrong.*

---

I have to talk about my mother, but I'll keep it short.

My mother stopped when I was eleven. She was sick a long time. While she was sick, she kept writing something. I thought it was something like a will, and I was too frightened to look at it.

I was eleven then, and I don't remember where I'd picked up the word "will." But I remember that the word frightened me. It meant the last thing a person says. I thought that once I heard the last thing, there would truly be nothing more, ever, after.

So I sat beside my mother while she wrote and looked somewhere else. My mother knew that and said nothing about it.

After my mother stopped, I was given those papers. Twelve pages. I read them one page at a time and gave up on the third page. There wasn't much to them. Where things in the house were kept, when to bring out the winter clothes, which house to buy rice from. Things like that.

At the end of the third page it said this: "That should be enough to get through winter." I read that sentence and closed the pages. I thought, so this is a paper about getting through winter. I thought that was all there was.

*I still think, even now, about why my mother wrote in that order. Was she someone who put the important things last? Or did it just end up that way as she wrote? Not being able to ask my mother is the thing I resent most about this part.*

I put it away in a drawer.

Two years later, looking for something else, I took it out again. And this time I read all twelve pages.

On the last line of the last page it said this:

"Hora, it doesn't matter to me what work you do. I only hope you'll be someone who reads to the end. I missed a great deal because I wasn't."

*I had left that line unread for two years.*

*I didn't know, at thirteen, how long two years is. I know now. My mother must have written that line thinking of me, and for two years I hadn't received that thought.*

*After that, I read everything to the end. It wasn't some great resolution. I simply never wanted to do that again.*

I can't say this is a good habit. I'm slow at everything. It takes me twice as long as anyone else to read a ration notice. People standing in line with me often walked off before I finished. There were times at school when I ran out of exam time — because I was still reading the instructions printed at the end.

My aunt told me many times, "Hora, the important part is at the front." She was right. Usually the important part is at the front.

*That usually and always aren't the same thing — I learned that two years too late.*

---

When my number was called from the roster, I didn't even know what it meant.

The notice read: *Owing to a vacancy among the assistant members of the Fourth Deliberation District, you have been assigned by roster number.*

I read that sentence three times. Even after three readings, it never said who had assigned me.

Only that I had been assigned. Not who had done the assigning — only that I had been assigned.

That day I went to the front desk and asked whether I could learn why the person before me had quit.

The person at the desk looked at the screen for a while and said,

"…The reason field is empty."

"…They didn't write anything?"

"…It's a box you don't have to write in."

I thanked them and left. That person hadn't hidden anything from me. It really had been empty.

*In this city, there are only ever two reasons you can't find something out. It wasn't written down, or you didn't ask. There is no third reason. It took me two years to work that out, and after I worked it out I had to spend a long time deciding whether that was reassuring or not.*

*This is something I only noticed later. At the time, I just assumed documents were written that way. I assumed documents simply didn't put people's names down. I assumed that was politeness.*

The notice was two pages. The first page held the assignment. The second held the guidelines for the post. I read the second page to the end too.

At the very bottom of the second page there was a box. A long, narrow horizontal box, with small letters above it reading **the signature box**.

It was empty.

I looked at that box for a long while. I had half-raised my pen, wondering whether it was mine to fill in, when I saw the line beneath it — *(This notice does not require a signature)* — and set the pen back down.

*Does not require. That isn't the same as forbids. I couldn't read that difference at the time. I sometimes wonder what would have changed if I had, but probably nothing would have. A sixteen-year-old can't work out the structure of a city from a single sheet of notice paper.*

*Still, I looked at that box that day. That I looked is a fact. That's where this book begins.*

I folded the notice and put it in the drawer. The same drawer that held my mother's pages. There wasn't any particular reason I put it there. I only had the one drawer.

---

The night before my first day, I couldn't sleep.

It wasn't fear. Starting a new job in this city isn't a frightening thing. No one was going to evaluate me, no one would scold me if I did badly, and no one would praise me if I did well. I already knew that much.

What kept me awake was something else.

I wanted to know why I had been chosen.

I knew it was because of the number. But I couldn't help wondering whether a number was really that simple a thing. Why had the person before me quit. If they hadn't, what would I be doing right now. Who had held the number before mine on the roster. Why hadn't it worked out for that person.

*That was the night I asked that question for the first time, and I don't let go of it until this record ends. This book was, in effect, written out of that one question.*

*And I still don't know the answer. This isn't a preview. It's a warning. There is no answer in this record.*

My aunt came into the room that night and told me to turn off the light. I turned it off and lay down, and then, in the dark, I asked her,

"…Auntie, why do you think it was me?"

"…It was you because your number came up."

"…I know that part."

My aunt was quiet for a moment, then said,

"…Hora, if you start thinking about that, there's no end to it."

She was right. There was no end to it.

---

On my way to work, I saw the water channel.

At the western edge of the city, I don't know since when, a place had appeared where water ran. Something like a narrow ditch, roughly set with stones. It wasn't deep, and it wasn't wide. Water was simply passing through it.

People walked past it without a glance. No one stopped, no one looked in.

I stopped, for a moment. Not because it was strange, but because of the sound.

The water wasn't clear. It was the color of soil, and I could see small stones laid along the bottom. I wanted to touch it, and didn't. I didn't know whether it was all right to, and in this city, not doing what you don't know is all right to do had become a habit.

Someone passed by beside me. I asked,

"…How long has this been here?"

The person glanced once at the water channel and once at me. It wasn't an irritated look. It was a genuinely thoughtful one.

"…Hm. Was it always here?"

And they went on.

*That person hadn't lied. They truly didn't know. The people of this city truly don't know anything about what has never come up as an agenda item. Not because they've been made not to know, but because that's the only channel there is for knowing anything at all.*

This city is quiet. The Equity is a city with no occasion for noise. But that water made a sound. A small, continuous sound.

*Later I would learn where that water came from. And I would learn that it appears nowhere in any document of this city. It hadn't been hidden. No one had simply brought it up.*

*I didn't know yet, in this city, that those two things mean the same thing.*

I walked on. I couldn't be late.

As I walked, I looked back once. The water was still making its sound. Whether or not anyone was listening.

---

The Fourth Deliberation District's meeting room was smaller than I'd expected.

There was one long table, and twenty-two chairs. Nothing hung on the walls. There was a window, but it was a window you couldn't see outside through.

A stack of papers sat at my seat. They said it was today's deliberation material.

Nineteen of the twenty-two seats were filled. Everyone was older than me, and everyone was quiet. Some greeted me and some didn't, and not greeting me didn't look rude. Relationships that require a greeting don't tend to form here.

No one asked me who I was. Whoever I was would already be written in the material. Not asking again about what's already written down is the courtesy here.

I picked the stack up and checked its thickness. About forty pages.

The person in the next seat looked at me and said, smiling,

"…The summary's up front. All you have to look at is that."

"…Yes."

"…Really. The summary is accurate."

"…Yes. Thank you."

"…First time?"

"…Yes."

"…You'll get used to it fast. Everyone does."

That person was genuinely kind. I learned their name later, but I won't write it here. There are several people in this record whose names I don't write, and I'll get to the reason later on.

*That person didn't deceive me. The summary was accurate. Not one single person in this city ever told me a lie. Not one.*

*I end up writing that sentence again, several times, in this record. Each time I write it, it grows a little heavier.*

I thanked them and read the summary.

The summary was a page and a half. It took less than five minutes to read. It set out, in order, what the case was, why it had come up, and how it was expected to be handled. There wasn't a wasted word in it.

The session began. People spoke one at a time. What they said was mostly the same. It was put in order for you, it was passed on and received, it was confirmed. No one said what they thought, and that didn't sound strange to me. It wasn't a seat for saying what you thought.

It was over in twenty minutes. The decision came out.

*That day I went home and my aunt asked how my first day had gone, and I answered, "…It finished faster than I expected." My aunt laughed. That was all.*

---

And then, after I'd finished the summary, I read all forty pages to the end.

After the meeting ended and everyone else had left, I stayed behind and read alone. It took two hours. Partway through, the caretaker came to turn off the lights, saw me, and went back out.

Reading it through, the summary was correct. Not a single character was off. There were things in the forty pages that weren't in the summary, but none of it mattered. None of it truly mattered.

*I want to set this part down precisely. There was nothing I discovered on that first day. This book isn't a story about me discovering something. I simply read, and that day, nothing happened from the reading.*

*Days like that go on for about two hundred days. Nothing happened for two hundred days. I won't write out all two hundred, but I'm noting here that they happened. So that whoever reads this record doesn't think I was carrying some kind of premonition around with me the whole time.*

*I had no premonition at all. I was just a girl who read to the end.*

There was a box at the bottom of the last of the forty pages too.

A long, narrow horizontal box, with small letters above it reading the signature box.

It was empty.

That day, I didn't think anything of it.
