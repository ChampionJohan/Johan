# Chapter 26. Temel

> _Previously_ — Eleven years ago, there was a person who wrote a name.
> That person opened a new deliberation, reversed part of a matter, and
> was afterward called in again and again, until one summer day the
> record stopped. I heard there was still, these days, a person who
> wrote a name, and I copied that name onto paper.

I didn't know how to go about finding a person. There was almost never
a reason to look for someone in this city. Whoever you needed was
always on the roster, and the roster had an address. You went to the
address and the person was there. That was all there was to it.

But the person the clerk had told me about wasn't on the roster. Of
course not, since I'd been told they weren't from this city. I went to
the front desk and asked about visitor records.

"…We don't keep separate visitor records. Only residents go on the
roster."

"…Then how do I find someone who comes and goes?"

"…Well. There's no way but to ask someone who's seen them."

*Hearing that answer, I laughed a little. For all the Consensus's
transparency, this city had no way to find a single person. I only
understood later. The Consensus was transparent about what came before
a deliberation. A person who'd never come before one wasn't, to begin
with, something the Consensus had any business with.*

*It was exactly like the water channel. If it had never been brought
up, it was as good as not existing.*

The person at the desk must have felt a little sorry for me, because
she added one more thing.

"…Oh — there is someone who's been coming and going lately. Every time
they file an application they always write their name, so everyone
remembers them."

"…Do they have a lot of applications to file?"

"…I hear they do work finding people. They apply for the reading room
often too."

I asked right then.

"…Where would I go to meet them?"

"…Hmm. Somebody said they'd seen them around the market."

I wondered whether that much could really lead me to a person. But
this city ran that way. The records were flawless, and whatever wasn't
in the records had to be found by rumor. Strangely enough, that felt
like the most human part of this city.

For three days I went from district to district asking people. I must
have asked around thirty. Most said they didn't know. Two said "I
think I've seen them." One person told me, "I think they're around the
north market these days."

---

The north market was the loudest place in the Equity. Everyone said
that even at its loudest, it would sound quiet next to another city,
but to me it was the loud one.

That person was standing at the far end of the market, in front of the
public notice board. City notices were posted there, and beside them
was one more small sheet, posted by no one I could name. It began,
"Looking for a person."

They were neither tall nor short. Looked to be in their mid-thirties.
Their clothes were worn but clean. Their face carried no particular
expression. I don't mean that unkindly. It was simply true. Not angry,
not glad to see me — they just stood there.

They had a bag slung over one shoulder, with a bundle of paper
sticking out the side. It looked folded and refolded many times, as
though it had been carried a long while. Their shoes were worn too.
Not shoes from this city. This city's shoes all came from the same
place, and those were a different shape.

*Looking back now, I remember those shoes for a surprisingly long
time. That detail stayed with me, oddly. I think I recognized a
stranger from the shoes before I did from the face.*

I went up and asked.

"…Excuse me — are you the one who writes a name?"

The person looked at me. Their face went thoughtful for a moment.

"…Write what?"

"…On a document. In the signature box."

"…Ah, I do. Why?"

Their tone was flat. Not wary of me, not curious about me either. Just
a tone confirming a fact.

"…What's your name?"

The person said a name. It was the name the clerk had told me.

"…Temel."

---

I stood there a moment not knowing what to ask. Temel didn't press me
either. He looked back at the notice board.

"…Did you put this up?"

"…Yes. I do work finding people."

"…What kind of people?"

"…People I'm asked about. When a family's been scattered, or word's
been lost, and someone asks me to find that person, I go find them."

"…Are you from this city?"

"…No. I go from place to place. This is my — I don't know how many
times already, coming through here."

The way he spoke was plain. It didn't sound like he was hiding
anything, or exaggerating anything. I found that oddly unfamiliar.
Everyone in the Equity was used to speaking with themselves one step
removed — "It was passed on," "It was put in order" — but this man
just said "I go find them." There was a subject.

*I found that remarkable, then. Looking back on it now, it wasn't
remarkable at all. He just wasn't from this city. He simply wasn't
used to this city's grammar. And still, in that moment, it felt like
meeting someone entirely different.*

I looked at the sheet on the board again. "Looking for a person. If
you know their name, their last known whereabouts, or any
identifying feature, please contact the address below." The name
written under it wasn't a name from this city.

"…Have you found this person?"

"…Not yet. I've been here three days now."

"…What happens if you don't find them?"

"…I go to the next city. Look there too."

"…What about the person here, then? The one you didn't find?"

Temel looked at the board once and said,

"…I leave the sheet. Someone might see it later and get in touch."

"…Does that happen?"

"…Sometimes. Very rarely."

He said "very rarely" as though it were nothing at all. No trace of
disappointment, no trace of giving up. He just seemed like someone
who'd lived by that kind of probability.

"…So do you file an application to find someone in this city too?"

"…Yes. Filed one yesterday."

"…Did you write your name in the signature box there?"

Temel gave me a slightly puzzled look.

"…It doesn't get accepted unless you do."

"…No, I mean — in this city, everyone leaves that box blank."

"…Blank? Why?"

I probably made just as puzzled a face, that time.

"…I don't know. Nobody writes it."

Temel looked at the board for a moment and said,

"…If there's a box to write in, don't you write in it?"

---

That was so simple, I couldn't answer for a moment.

*Writing this record, I've read that line over many times. "If there's
a box to write in, don't you write in it?" That wasn't a question. He
didn't ask it because he was genuinely curious — it was something that
came out of him because to him, it was simply obvious. It wasn't
obvious to me. That difference in what was obvious was the difference
between this city and his.*

I didn't know how to answer, so I asked something else. I felt like I
should ask more, but I couldn't get straight what to ask.

"…What happens if you write your name on an application? Some people
here are afraid of it."

Temel shrugged a little.

"…You get called in, sure. Every matter with that name on it, they
call you."

"…Are you all right with that?"

"…Not so much all right as used to it. I've been through it plenty of
times, in plenty of cities."

His face stayed the same the whole time he said it. Not resentful, not
proud. Just a face stating a fact.

"…So how many times have you already been called in here?"

"…This time? Twice. I only filed a day ago and they've already called
me in twice."

"…Twice already? For what?"

"…Once to confirm the application's contents. Once for another
department to confirm the same thing. They ask the same thing twice,
but I've come to expect it."

"…You've come to expect it?"

"…At first it annoyed me a bit. But being annoyed doesn't stop them
from calling either."

There was no resignation in that, and no patience — just a plain
familiarity. Like a rhythm worn into the body from long practice. That
frightened me. More than the irritation or the anger would have, that
flatness frightened me. I wondered how many times something had to
happen to a person before they got like that.

I thought about bringing up the name from eleven years ago, then
didn't. I still had nothing certain. Instead I asked something else.

"…Do you know why people in this city don't write their names?"

"…No idea. I'm not from here."

"…What about other cities, then? Do other cities not write names
either?"

"…Depends on the city. A place where it's completely blank, like
here — this is the first I've seen."

"…Oh — first time?"

"…Yeah. Usually a few people write it. Not everyone, but some. Here
it's really none at all, far as I've checked."

That surprised me a little. It meant this city wasn't just especially
blank — other cities still had a few people who wrote. So was this
city unusually far gone, or was it simply a different way of doing
things? I didn't get the answer that day.

"…So why do you write it, then? You don't have to here."

Temel looked at me for a moment. There was nothing particular in that
look. He was simply looking at me.

"…Let's talk about that another time. I need to go find someone,
right now."

Then he tore a sheet from the board, folded it, and tucked it away,
and walked off toward the market. I watched his back for a long while.

A woman running a stall nearby spoke to me.

"…Do you know that man?"

"…Just met him today."

"…He's an odd one. Only been here a few days and I couldn't tell you
how many times he's already been called into the office. But he never
once gets angry about it."

"…Guess he's just made that way."

"…Right? If it were me, I'd have lost my temper a long time ago."

The woman went off then to see to a customer. I stood there a while
longer. The market noise grew loud again around me — vegetables being
sold, prices being haggled over, a child crying. It was a loud place,
for this city.

*The people of this city didn't look at Temel strangely. They found
him curious, but not something to be wary of. I found that a little
strange myself. Isn't the usual thing, when you see someone doing
something different from everyone else, to grow wary first? But here
people were simply curious. They didn't feel him as a threat. Probably
because writing a name wasn't treated as anything large enough in this
city to be a threat. It wasn't that it wasn't frightening because it
wasn't a big thing — it was that no one had ever done it, so no one
knew how big a thing it was.*

---

On the way home I turned over what had happened that day. Nothing
unusual had happened. I'd met one person, and exchanged a few words.
That was all.

*And still I slept badly that night. It's hard to say exactly why.
Probably because he was too ordinary. I think I'd been assuming,
without meaning to, that someone who wrote a name would look different
somehow. A different look in the eyes, maybe, or a heavier way of
speaking. Temel had none of that. He was simply a tired man. A little
worn, a little flat, with only the plainness particular to someone
who'd done this work of finding people for a long time.*

*I thought something else, that night. If Temel wasn't writing a name
because he was something special, that meant I could write one too.
The moment that thought came, my body went a little stiff. I hurried
to put the thought away. I told myself it was still too soon. At the
time, I really believed that.*

*I've felt the temptation, more than once, to make Temel out to be
something special in this record. Someone who knew something, someone
who already had it all figured out. But that isn't the truth. Temel
was, then and now, simply someone who wrote a name. That was all he
was, and that, strangely, was the strangest thing about him.*

*I'm writing this record much later, after everything has already
passed. So there are moments when the temptation comes to slip in,
as though I knew it at the time, things I didn't actually know then.
That's especially true when it comes to Temel. But I've decided to
resist that temptation, here. The person I was that day didn't know
who Temel was. I stayed not knowing for a long time, even after
meeting him several times more. So it seems more honest to write it
here, too, not knowing.*

I told my aunt about that day, and she asked,

"…What does that man do?"

"…Finds people."

"…There's work like that?"

"…Apparently. He's not from here, she said."

My aunt wasn't especially surprised. She just said, "…That's
interesting," and let it go. My aunt was never one to be surprised by
much.

"…But Hora, why did you go to meet him?"

I thought a moment before answering. I wasn't entirely sure myself
what I'd gone hoping to find out.

"…No reason. I just wanted to see what kind of person writes a name."

"…And did you?"

"…I did, but I still don't know. He was just an ordinary person."

"…An ordinary person doing a strange thing, then."

My aunt's words stayed with me the whole night. An ordinary person
doing a strange thing. If it had been a strange person doing a strange
thing, I probably would have thought less about it. Temel had truly
looked ordinary. And yet he was doing, without a second thought,
something no one in this city ever did.

That night I opened the drawer and looked again at the paper with the
two names on it. The person from eleven years ago, and Temel. Their
handwriting was different, their ages were different, the paths that
had brought them here were different. No connection between them had
been confirmed yet. And still I stared at those two names for a long
while.

*I would go on to meet Temel several more times after this day. Each
time, I think I expected him to tell me something remarkable. But
Temel never once did that. He was simply a person who wrote his name,
got called in, and went on to the next city. That was all he was, and
it took me a long time to truly see that "all."*

The next day, I went back to the north market.
