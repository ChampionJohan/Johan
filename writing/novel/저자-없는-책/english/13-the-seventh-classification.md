# Chapter 13. The Seventh Classification

> _Previously_ — Two hundred days into her posting at the Fourth Deliberation District, Hora was still the only person who read deliberation documents to the end. The summary was always accurate, and nothing ever happened. Only the signature box at the bottom of the document — every document she opened, without exception — stayed empty.

The assignment notice came on a single sheet of paper, same as always.

*The Seventh Classification residential clearance and relocation matter — assistant member Hora assigned.* That was all of it. I read the sentence three times, and it was the same sentence all three times. Reading it a third time did not make the meaning any longer.

*I remember exactly which day I received this notice. I remember it exactly not because that day was in itself special, but because later I had to go back to that day again and again, to count something. Not to count what happened that day — to count what didn't.*

I went to the reception desk and asked what the Seventh Classification was.

"…It's a classification for residents with incomplete registration."

"…Why is the registration incomplete?"

"…Because their residence is on unregistered land."

"…Is that their fault?"

"…The word 'fault' doesn't appear anywhere in the file. It just says incomplete registration."

The clerk was doing her best to help me. There wasn't the slightest trace of impatience in her voice. I found that strangely steadying. That no one ever treated me as a bother was, in this city, simply how things were — but that day, of all days, it felt unusually kind.

I thought I understood what she meant. It was like the area near the west water channel. Just as the channel administratively did not exist because it had never come up as an agenda item, the land where the people beside it lived also administratively did not exist, for the same reason. People living on land that did not exist had been gathered into a classification that did not exist.

I thought that made logical sense. At the time.

*I hardly questioned the word "registration" itself back then. If something wasn't registered, I assumed the unregistered side was the one with the problem. It was much later that I asked why land not being registered should be the fault of the people living on it.*

---

The clerk handed me a thin information sheet along with the file. It was a list of the departments involved. Twelve departments, each with the number of staff assigned written beside it. Added together, it came to more than four hundred people.

"…This many people are involved?"

"…The bigger the matter, the more there are. That way it never comes down to one person's judgment."

"…So all four hundred of them decide?"

"…No. No one decides. Everyone just does their own share. Passing it on, putting it in order, confirming, convening. Gather all of that up, and it becomes a decision."

"…Then who do you ask, if it goes wrong?"

"…If it's wrong, you file an objection. Objections are handled the same way."

"…What does the same way mean?"

"…Another four hundred or so people pass it on, put it in order, confirm it, convene over it."

That didn't sound strange to me. That objections, too, went through a formal procedure was, if anything, reassuring to hear. It meant no one could look the other way on impulse — that even an objection went onto the same scale as everything else.

I heard that and nodded. It sounded like a safeguard. One person turning bad-hearted could never talk four hundred people into agreeing.

*I believed, at the time, that these four hundred people were a safeguard. I remember exactly how long that belief lasted. Until I met twelve of them.*

---

I ran into Tyche in the corridor. I asked if she was assigned to this one, and she said she only put together the supporting materials.

"…What exactly is the Seventh Classification?"

"…It's the seventh item on the classification table. Numbered in order — not ranked."

"…Why are these particular people grouped into one classification?"

"…On the one thing they have in common: living on residences with no basis for registration. Nothing else in common. Different ages, different work, different places they lived before."

"…Then why are they all being processed at once?"

Tyche looked at me and smiled. It wasn't an unkind smile.

"…Because processing them all at once is fair. Looked at one by one, the outcome would depend on each person's circumstances. Then some would end up looked after and some wouldn't. Grouped by classification and processed by the same standard, nobody gets special treatment."

I heard that and nodded. At the time it sounded right. *Even now I can't say the statement itself was wrong. What I didn't ask, back then, was who that fairness was for.*

---

The clerk handed me a file. This one was thick.

"…You read all of this?"

"…Yes."

"…Everyone else just looks at the summary."

"…I always read the whole thing."

She looked at me for a moment. It wasn't a strange look. Just a mildly curious one.

"…Then this'll take you a while. This one's big."

"…How big?"

"…Eleven hundred and forty people."

I heard that number and felt nothing. I'm ashamed to say so, but that's the truth. The number was just a number. The deliberations I'd seen so far had run, at most, a few dozen people, so eleven hundred and forty didn't feel real to me at all.

*I've crossed this paragraph out and rewritten it more times than I can count, writing this now. There's a temptation to write it as though I felt something I should have felt. But that isn't true. I heard the number and worried about the thickness of the file. That was all.*

---

When the matter was first brought before the Fourth Deliberation District's meeting room, the seats were about half full.

The officer in charge stood at the front and explained. Unregistered residences had accumulated over several years, he said, and this time a consolidated clearance agenda had been brought up. Someone asked what "clearance" meant.

"…Recovery of the residences and relocation."

"…Relocation to where?"

"…That's in the detailed proposal."

"…This looks like a big one — why is it only coming up now?"

"…The cumulative count crossed the processing threshold. Below the threshold, cases were handled individually. Once it crossed, it converted to a consolidated agenda item."

I sat through that exchange without much thought. The phrase "detailed proposal" was familiar. This city's documents were always layered that way. The broad shape gets stated at the meeting, the detail lives in the document — that had held true up to now.

The officer handed out the summary. A page and a half. I read it. Clearance of unregistered residences, eleven hundred and forty people affected, a relocation plan to be established, an implementation date specified. Clean.

The person in the next seat said,

"…This one's big, so read carefully, everyone."

"…Right."

"…Not that it matters — it'll all go by the summary anyway. It always does for these."

Near the end of the meeting, someone asked whether children were among the eleven hundred and forty. The officer checked his materials and answered.

"…Two hundred thirty are fourteen or under."

"…And the elderly?"

"…A hundred ninety are sixty or over."

The room went quiet for a moment. But the quiet didn't last. Someone cleared their throat, and someone asked whether we were ready to move to the next item. I didn't read that quiet as meaning anything, at the time. I just thought everyone had gone briefly solemn hearing the numbers.

*Looking back, that quiet changed nothing. It went quiet, then it moved on. In this city, quiet was a feeling, not an action.*

That man hadn't meant to deceive me. He truly believed what he said. *And in a certain sense he was right. This matter, too, ended up going by the summary. Nothing he said was wrong.*

---

The meeting ended and people filed out. I stayed behind holding the thick file.

The custodian came to lock up and saw me still there.

"…Staying late again today?"

"…Yes."

"…This one's a big one."

"…Yes, I saw."

"…I won't turn off the lights."

The custodian said that and left. I stayed alone and turned to the first page of the file.

The first section was background: the history of unregistered residences, how they arose, the relevant regulations. I read it slowly. None of the sentences were difficult. Just long.

According to the background section, unregistered residences generally arose like this. A piece of land had never come up as an agenda item. Never having come up, it went unregistered. Unregistered land got no management budget. With no budget, no one touched it. Since no one touched it, no one stopped people from moving in and living there. A few years, a few decades passed that way, and by then people were living on land that was still, on paper, nowhere at all.

Reading this circular structure, I thought of the water channel. I'd learned that the west channel, too, was treated as not existing because it had never come up as an agenda item. That land, too, could vanish the same way — I learned that for the first time reading this document.

*Something should have clicked for me at this point. It didn't. Reading through the circular structure, I just thought this city's administration was remarkably thorough. How neatly it kept records of what wasn't there, I thought.*

I gauged the thickness of the file. It looked to be more than three hundred pages. It would take several days to finish.

I looked at the bottom of the first page. There was a signature box. Empty. Of course it was. It always was.

The first fifty pages were mostly definitions and grounds — the legal definition of incomplete registration, the relevant ordinance numbers, a list of similar small-scale clearances over the past ten years. The small cases had run twenty or thirty people at a time, and each had gone through without any particular problem, the file said. This one was unusually large, it explained, only because those small cases had gone unresolved and kept piling up.

I read about fifty pages that night before going home. My aunt asked why I was so late, and I only said this one was pretty big.

"…How big."

"…Eleven hundred forty, they said."

My aunt looked at me a moment.

"…You're going to read all of it?"

"…Yes."

"…Hora, that's exactly why the summary exists — because nobody reads all of it."

"…I know. I'm reading it anyway."

My aunt didn't try to stop me any further. Instead she spooned me another bowl of rice. *Thinking back on it now, I think she wanted to stop me, some. Not because reading it all the way through was a bad thing to do, but because she may have had some sense of what I'd end up seeing once I finished. Of course, this is a thought I've attached to it afterward. That evening she just spooned out more rice. That's all she did.*

---

From the next day on, I stayed and read every day.

Nothing struck me as strange as I read. Every sentence was clean, every claim was backed, every procedure was in order. No clause was hidden. No page was blocked from view.

*I want to be clear about this part. Writing this record now, I don't want it to look as though I were uncovering something bit by bit. The file was open from the first page to the last. Anyone could have read it. I was simply reading what no one else read.*

On the second day I read the roster of those affected. No names — only numbers and classification codes. 7-001 through 7-1140. Beside each number, a brief age range, household size, and length of residence. At first I thought the absence of names was for privacy, and in fact it partly was.

Someone else was in the reading room that day. Someone from a neighboring deliberation district, skimming the same matter. After about ten minutes he pulled out only the summary, took it, and left. He never opened the full text.

I didn't think of that as laziness. Everyone knew the summary was accurate. Reading it a second time, when it was already accurate, was the strange thing to do. *At the time I thought I was the strange one. Even now, I don't think that's entirely wrong.*

On the third day, around page one hundred fifty, I came to the list of proposed relocation sites. Place names ran down the page. I picked out a few I could try to place, and some I recognized, and some I didn't.

At the end of the list was a footnote in small print.

*Of the above list, items 3, 7, and 12 are temporary designations pending detailed confirmation.*

I read the footnote and turned the page. It didn't seem all that important at the time. I noticed all three items had a small asterisk beside their place names, and I took that, too, for just a notation convention. "Temporary designation" was a phrase I saw often in this city's documents. I figured it would be finalized eventually.

*That's how I let the footnote pass. If someone asked me which is the moment I regret most while writing this record, I'd point to that night. I'm not sure "regret" is the right word. I did, after all, go on to read every page that followed, and I did, in the end, learn everything. Still — in that moment, I could have stopped once more and read that footnote again.*

I closed the file and went home. About a hundred pages remained.

I passed the water channel on my way home. The water was still making its sound. I stood beside it for a moment and wondered whether the land this water ran through could one day become an unregistered residence too. No one lived there yet, so it seemed unlikely.

*That's what I thought at the time. I can't yet say I was wrong. No one lived there. Still, the thought that there might have been a connection between that channel and this deliberation — one I failed to see — is something I haven't been able to fully shake, even now, writing this record.*

My aunt was already asleep, so I went straight to my room without a word. I fell asleep holding two things in my head: that a hundred pages remained, and that three footnotes said "temporary designation."

*Tomorrow I would finish the remaining hundred pages. At the time I thought of it as simply one more day's work left. I believed that once the day passed, this matter, too, would end just as the summary described. Exactly when that belief broke is what I set down, unchanged, in the next chapter.*
