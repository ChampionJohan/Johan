# Chapter 14. To the End

> _Previously_ — Hora is three days into reading the Seventh Classification deliberation. Eleven hundred forty people, two hundred thirty of them children. Three footnotes reading "temporary designation" at the end of the relocation-site list nagged at her, though at the time she didn't know why.

On the fourth morning I got to the meeting room earlier than usual.

The custodian was cleaning and startled to see me.

"…You're early today."

"…Yes, I'm going to finish it today."

"…That thick one? Impressive."

"…It's just a habit."

The custodian laughed and went back to cleaning. I sat down and opened to the page where I'd stopped the day before. A hundred pages remained. I made up my mind to finish all of it that day. There was no other reason. I simply didn't want to split it up any further.

On the fourth day, I read the remaining hundred pages.

*I've thought for a long time about how to write this chapter. I don't want to write it dramatically. I didn't suddenly notice something that day. I just turned one page, then another. Even when I reached the last page, I was still just turning pages.*

*Up to that point I still didn't think I was doing anything remarkable. Reading what others don't read had long been my habit — not some sense of justice. I want this made clear, because I'm worried that what happens after this will read as though I'd meant to dig something up from the start. I was only doing what I always did.*

---

The first sixty of the hundred pages were about execution procedure. Notification method, notification deadline, the channel for filing objections, the formula for relocation support funds. All of it was there. All the numbers were there too. Support funds — so much per household. Moving support — so many days. Where to file the change of residence.

The notification-method clause read: *Recipients will be notified in writing thirty days before the implementation date, and the notice will specify the destination site, the relocation deadline, and the support provided.* I read this and thought it a considerate clause. Thirty days seemed like no short amount of time.

There was also a clause for filing objections. *Anyone with an objection to this decision may submit a written objection within fourteen days of the notification date.* I thought this was a good clause too. It meant the path to filing an objection wasn't closed off.

Reading it all, I felt reassured rather than not. Something this carefully woven together seemed unlikely to let anything bad happen. That there were support funds, that there was moving assistance — all of it read as good news.

*I read every one of these clauses generously. Even now, looking back, none of these clauses is bad in itself. The problem wasn't the clause. It was what the clause pointed to.*

Around page seventy, the detailed relocation-site plan appeared. The same list I'd seen on the third day unfolded again here, this time laid out in a table with each region's capacity, infrastructure status, and expected relocation timing.

I found the three sites the footnote had called temporary — items 3, 7, and 12. I looked at the table. The capacity column held a question mark. The infrastructure status column was blank. Only the expected relocation timing was filled in, the same as everywhere else.

*I looked at this table many times over. Seeing the question mark and the blank cell side by side, I felt for the first time that something was strange. I still didn't know what, exactly, that sense of strangeness was pointing to.*

Beside the table I noticed a small note in the margin: *Final confirmation pending, following inter-departmental consultation, to be separately announced.* No date was given for when the consultation would end. I went to the archive and looked into similar past cases. A few small-scale matters had also carried the phrase "separately announced." Half of them had no follow-up announcement that the consultation had ended at all. Whether the absence meant it had been cancelled or was still ongoing, nothing anywhere said.

*This was the first time I doubted the phrase "separately announced" in this city's documents. I'm not sure "doubted" is the right word. I only confirmed, with my own eyes, for the first time, that there might be nothing at all behind those words.*

---

I looked closely at the support-fund formula too. The amount changed with household size, and a little more was added for longer residence. The formula itself was written out. I actually applied it to a few households. The math checked out. What wasn't in the clause was whether there was actually a market at the destination site where that money could be used — whether there was somewhere to buy a house with it. That having money and having somewhere to spend it were different problems was something I first thought about right then.

Around page eighty I found the number of people assigned to items 3, 7, and 12.

The three together came to four hundred ten. Four hundred ten out of eleven hundred forty were headed to places whose capacity and infrastructure had not been confirmed.

I flipped back and checked the three place names again. The names were there. I tried to place them on the city map in my head, and I couldn't place them anywhere.

One of the three names was "the West Lowlands." I paused when I saw that name. The word "west" caught at me. It was the direction of the west water channel. I wasn't sure. The west side of the city was wide — it could overlap, or it might not. There was no way for me to check right then, so I just wrote the name down.

*Whether that overlap is coincidence or not, I still can't say for certain, writing this record. I didn't compare it against the map until much later, and that result comes later in this book. Here I'll only set down that I stopped in front of that name that night.*

*This was the first time I thought about going to the archive to check the map against it. I didn't. There was no time. Writing this sentence, I regret not doing it then, though the outcome would probably have been the same either way. That place name had never been on any map to begin with.*

---

Around page ninety, I found out what these three places were. "Found out" overstates it. It was simply written in the next paragraph.

*Items 3, 7, and 12 are designated pending sites and, until confirmed, are classified as unregistered regions.*

I read that sentence three times. On the fourth reading I read it aloud. There was no one else in the reading room, so no one would hear. Spoken aloud, the meaning was the same.

The three sites were land that did not yet exist. Unregistered regions. Reclassified into exactly the state the Seventh Classification lived in — land that did not exist because it had never come up as an agenda item.

Four hundred ten people, being pushed off land for lack of registration, were headed to other land that was, itself, unregistered.

I stood up for a moment at this point and paced around the reading room. Wondering if I'd misread something, I sat back down and read the paragraph again. Same content. Three readings, all the same.

I also checked what would happen to the remaining seven hundred thirty. That side had its infrastructure status and capacity fully filled in. They were assigned to land that really existed, to plots where houses would really be built. So this matter was not entirely bad. Most of them really were going somewhere. Only four hundred ten were headed to a place that did not yet exist.

*I want to set down this distinction precisely. This deliberation did not send all eleven hundred forty people to a place that didn't exist. If it had, it might have been easier to notice. Seven hundred thirty really did have somewhere to go. So when the summary said "relocation plan established," that wasn't a lie. There really was a plan. For most of them.*

*I catch myself, once again, resisting the temptation to write this sentence so it sounds frightening. Nothing in the document itself read as frightening. It was just one paragraph. The same font size, the same style, as every other paragraph.*

---

The last ten pages were supplementary provisions. I'd heard that supplementary provisions were something nobody ever reads — formal clauses, implementation dates, transitional rules and the like.

I read the supplementary provisions to the end too.

The final clause of the supplementary provisions — the last sentence of the entire document — read:

*Upon completion of this procedure, the Seventh Classification will be deleted from the roster.*

I read that sentence and sat for a long while.

I checked the clock. It was past ten at night. I was alone in the reading room. The window let in no view of outside, so I couldn't guess the time by that — I only knew from the clock in the corridor.

*Being deleted didn't mean a person was deleted. It meant the classification was deleted. I held onto that distinction and thought about it for a long time. When a classification disappears, where do the people who were in it go? Do they get moved to another classification, or do they simply become people belonging to no classification at all? The document didn't say. Not that it left it unsaid — the document simply ended there.*

---

I closed the document and looked at the cover again. There was a viewing-record field — a box where it stamped who had opened it and when.

I opened that field. This, too, had always been public. Not a hidden menu.

There was a long record. The department in charge, related departments, the higher authority. The number of views was high. Over two hundred.

*Seeing this record, I knew one thing for certain. No one had stopped me. This document had been opened more than two hundred times. Anyone could have gone all the way to the last page. There was no reason, in this city, to hide the final clause. The Consensus hides nothing. Not hidden — simply that not one of those two hundred had gone all the way to the last page.*

*This fact made what I had to do next much harder. If someone had hidden it, I could have gone to that person. No one had hidden it. So who was I supposed to go to?*

I scrolled down the viewing record line by line. No names — only department and position. Several times from the department in charge, a few times from related departments, once from the higher authority. Each of the twenty-two committee members had a record of having opened it once. I would now be added to that list too.

I wrote the number two hundred in the corner of a piece of paper. I didn't know why I wrote it down then. Later I would find reason to pull that number out again.

*Looking at that list, I felt a strange comfort. At least I wasn't the only one who had opened this document. That it had been opened over two hundred times meant that over two hundred times, someone had sat down in front of it. But how many of those two hundred times had eyes actually reached the final clause — the viewing record did not say. It doesn't measure the distance between having opened something and having read it.*

---

Before I left, I checked the signature box once more. It had become a habit by now. There was a signature box on the very last page too, beneath the supplementary provisions. Empty.

*Looking at that box, I formed a strange question for the first time. Not that there was no one who had made this document — but that there was no one at all assigned to sign it. The decision sat here, fully complete, and beneath that decision there was a place for a name, and no one was ever meant to fill it. I felt as though the order had been reversed. Normally, someone decides, and then signs. Here, things were decided without a signature, and the signature box simply remained, out of habit.*

The custodian was the last one left, and she turned off the light when she saw me.

"…Finished it all?"

"…Yes."

"…How was it?"

I couldn't answer right away. She didn't rush me, just waited.

"…Bigger than I thought."

"…It is. It's a big one."

She said only that and locked the door. I'd expected her to press me further about my answer. She didn't. *She hadn't deceived me either. Her job was simply to lock the door.*

On the way home, my steps kept slowing. It was late, so the streets were quiet.

Passing the water channel, I felt afraid of its sound for the first time. The water hadn't changed. I had.

*I told my aunt nothing that night. I didn't know what to say. If I said "four hundred ten out of eleven hundred forty are headed to a place that doesn't exist," I couldn't guess what she'd say back. I still can't guess, even now.*

I went to my room and pulled out the summary again. A page and a half. I read it once more.

There wasn't a lie in the summary. Eleven hundred forty people, a relocation plan, an implementation date. All of it was accurate.

Only the deletion of the roster wasn't anywhere in the summary.

I folded the summary back up and put it in the drawer. The one with my mother's papers. I still had no particular reason. It was still the only drawer I had.

*I barely slept that night. I'd like to say it wasn't out of fear, but I think, in truth, I was a little afraid. Only I couldn't tell, at the time, what that fear was pointed at. At the people of the Seventh Classification? At this city? Or simply at not knowing what I was supposed to do next?*

*Staring at the ceiling, I kept returning to the same sentence. Upon completion of this procedure, the Seventh Classification will be deleted from the roster. Twenty-two characters, in the original. Behind those twenty-two characters stood four hundred ten people.*

*I remember the first thing I did the next morning. I pulled out the summary again and this time read it word by word, tracing each character with my eye. I wanted to check that the summary really had no falsehood, that I hadn't misread something. To say the result first: the summary really was without error, anywhere. I set down that checking process exactly as it happened, in the next chapter.*
