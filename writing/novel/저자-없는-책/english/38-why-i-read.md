# Chapter 38. Why I Read

> _Previously_ — On the third night, Hora takes out the signature box
> again. The box had been there from the start. No one had ever stopped
> her. The decision hadn't come yet — only the question remained.

The next morning I took out the full text of the Seventh Classification
deliberation again.

It wasn't because the three days of not reading were over. There was
still one more day left. But I decided to make that one document an
exception. I'd already read it many times, and reading it again wasn't
going to produce a new sentence.

*It was a long time before I understood why I'd opened it again that
morning. I hadn't been trying to read it anew. I had been trying to
read the reason why I'd read it so many times.*

My aunt asked, while making breakfast.

"…Weren't you not reading until today?"

"…Except this one."

"…Why."

I looked at my aunt without having an answer ready. She didn't wait for
one. She just ladled the soup. That's the kind of person she is. She
can ask a question and not feel slighted if it goes unanswered.

*That morning, at the table, was the first time I genuinely asked
myself why I was holding on to this document the way I was. Before
that, I hadn't asked — I'd only read. Asking and reading, just like
looking at the signature box, turned out to be different things.*

We ate later than usual. My aunt didn't hurry me either. The sound of
the water channel drifted in faintly from outside. That sound wasn't
any different from usual. Only I was a little different from usual.

---

I'd read this document to the end, from the front, once already. Over
forty pages. The summary was a page and a half, and it was accurate. I'd
read that summary, and I'd read the full text, and after that I'd read
it again, many more times.

*Why did I read it so many times? At first I thought it was to check.
In case I'd missed something. But no matter how many times I reread it,
there was nothing I'd missed. I'd known from the start that the summary
was accurate.*

*So what was I trying to confirm?*

I properly asked myself this question for the first time that morning.
Before, I hadn't even known the shape of the question. I'd simply kept
reading.

*I thought of Tyche. She'd once asked me why I read the way I did. I
couldn't answer, then. I only said it was habit. Habit was true, but it
was a word that put off the answer rather than giving it. I hadn't said,
then, where the habit came from.*

*I wasn't trying to hide it. I simply didn't know either. For over two
hundred days I'd used that habit every day, and never once properly
looked into where it had come from.*

---

I thought of my mother.

Twelve pages. I'd stopped reading at the third page, once. At the
sentence, "This should be enough to get you through the winter." I'd
thought that was all there was.

I read the rest two years later. On the last line of the last page,
these words were there. *I only hope you'll be someone who reads to the
end. I missed a great deal because I wasn't.*

*I already wrote this story once, near the beginning. Back then I only
wrote it as background — as the reason I became someone who reads
everything to the end. I let the story be the story, and never brought
it back up after that. I need to bring it back up in this chapter. That
it wasn't background but a reason — I'm only admitting that now.*

I'd calculated those two years many times. About 730 days. A sentence my
mother had written thinking of me had waited for me in a drawer for 730
days. I hadn't received that sentence for all that time.

*Not received — that's not exact. Exactly: I hadn't read it. Receiving
and reading are different. The paper had been in my hand. I simply
hadn't read it.*

That morning I thought again about why I hadn't read it, back then. I'd
been afraid. Afraid of the word last words. Afraid that once I heard the
last thing someone had to say, there would truly be nothing after it.

*That fear wasn't a wrong fear. If you hear the last words, there really
is nothing after them. I had a right to be afraid of that. For an
eleven-year-old, that fear was only natural. That morning, admitting
this, I decided for the first time not to blame the girl I'd been.*

*If there was anyone to blame, it might have been the me of now — the
one who, for two years, hadn't given that fear a name and had simply
put it in a drawer. But even that's hard to blame. For a thirteen-year-
old, was there really any way other than putting it in a drawer?*

---

Back to the Seventh Classification document.

Reading it over and over, I'd thought I was looking for something. A
hidden sentence, a missing word, someone's mistake. But there was none.
There really was none. The summary was accurate, the evidence was all
there, the procedure had no flaw.

And yet I kept reading it again.

*That morning I admitted this. The reason I kept rereading it wasn't
that I thought something might be hidden in the document. It was that I
was afraid I might miss something again.*

*Like the sentence I'd missed at thirteen. Like the sentence I hadn't
received for 730 days. I never wanted to do that again. I'd already
written that at the very beginning of this book. But back then I hadn't
written it knowing exactly what it meant.*

*Now I know. I hadn't read that document to the end out of a sense of
justice. I read it because I was afraid of missing something again.*

I opened the document again from the beginning. This time I wasn't
reading to check the content. I read it to see where my own reading
slowed down.

*The parts covered in the summary I read quickly. I already knew that
content. The place it slowed down was always the same spot. The short
paragraphs wedged between the supporting materials and the record of
processing. The place where each person involved had left a one-line
note. "Confirmed." "Passed on." "No objection." Lines like that.*

*I learned, that day, why I'd always read those lines so slowly. It was
because that was the place that looked most like it had nothing in it.
Lines carrying no feeling, no meaning at all. I read them slowly because
I was afraid there was something in there. More exactly, I read them
slowly because I wanted to confirm that there was nothing there.*

*And there really was nothing there. I must have checked each of those
lines a hundred times over, in effect. To confirm there was nothing.*

Doing this checking, I thought again of that time, and of those people.
The first person who'd said, "I only passed it on." The twelve more I'd
met after. All of them people who'd left one of those lines — "passed
on," "confirmed," "no objection" — as their own share.

*Back then I'd thought I was going to them looking for responsibility.
Thinking about it now, maybe I was looking for something else even
then. Whether there was something more behind these one-line notes.
Whether, the way there had been more behind my mother's third page,
there was something more behind their one lines too.*

*There wasn't, for them. There really wasn't. That one line was the
whole of it, for them. I'd been disappointed by that fact, back then.
Looking at it again now, that disappointment was my own story too. Not
their problem — my expectation's problem.*

---

This isn't an easy thing to say.

I may have wanted the reader who has read this record from the start to
believe I did all of this for a good reason. That would have been easier
to write, too. A righteous girl who couldn't bear to watch an injustice
and stood up. That kind of story is common, and it's common for a
reason. Because it reads well.

*But that isn't the truth. I didn't read that document to the end for
the sake of the people bound up in the Seventh Classification. I read
it for myself. I read it because I never wanted to become, again, a
person who misses the last line.*

*Admitting this, strangely, put my mind at ease instead. I didn't know
what that ease was, then. I know now. A person who moves for a good
reason wavers when that reason wavers. Not a bad reason, but a person
moving for a reason that's entirely their own has, at least, no reason
of that kind to waver. I know what I missed. No one can argue me out of
that.*

*I think it's only fair to write this too. This realization didn't make
me a kinder person. I still barely knew the people of the Seventh
Classification. Not their faces, not their names, not their
circumstances. All I'd come to know was my own story. Knowing myself
isn't the same thing as having helped them. I'm setting this paragraph
apart so I don't mistake the two for each other.*

*Only — knowing myself honestly made what I did next honest. That much
is true.*

---

That afternoon Tyche came again. It was the last day of the three.

She saw I had the document open, and looked a little surprised.

"…Reading it again?"

"…Yeah. I made this one an exception."

"…What's different about it?"

I thought for a moment before answering.

"…This is me reading why I read."

Tyche looked at that sentence for a long while. Her face wasn't one of
not understanding. It was closer to one that understood too well to find
anything to say back.

"…So, did you find the answer?"

"…Yeah."

"…What was it?"

I hesitated, then said it.

"…There's something I missed once, a long time ago. So I read so I
won't miss anything again. Not because of those people."

Tyche said nothing. She didn't tease me, and she didn't say it was
admirable. She just nodded once.

"…So what if it is."

I didn't fully understand, that day, what that meant. Whether it meant
it wasn't a bad reason, or that reasons were always this small and
personal to begin with, or just that it was no big deal. Whichever it
was, Tyche didn't let me down.

---

*It would sound too grand to say this realization changed me. Not much
changed. I was still the same person, and I still had the same
document. Only one thing was different.*

*Before this, I'd thought of myself as an accuser. Someone who'd seen an
injustice and meant to expose it. An accuser has to stand on the right
side. I kept having to check whether I was really standing on the right
side.*

*That checking often froze me in place. Am I really right. Am I really
doing this for these people. Are my motives pure. These questions have
no end, and they keep you from moving until you find the answer. For
those couple hundred days, I'd been stopped in front of this question,
in effect, the whole time.*

*Now I was simply someone trying to fill a certain box. Someone who
couldn't stand an empty space — the way I once couldn't stand one. That
wasn't a matter of right and wrong. It was just my own matter.*

*Someone who wants to fill a thing doesn't have to keep proving their
motive over and over. They just see something empty and their hand
moves. I felt, that night, how large that difference was. I hadn't been
unable to move, all that time — I'd been inspecting myself before I
could move. Once I stopped inspecting, I could move.*

*An accuser has to prove herself. A person who fills a box just fills
it.*

---

After Tyche left I sat by the window for a long while and looked
outside. It was the hour the sun goes down.

*In that hour I thought about why I'd been able to say all this, even to
Tyche, only that simply. "There's something I missed once, a long time
ago." It was too short a sentence to hold the real reason. But I hadn't
found a way to say it any longer, then, and even now, writing this
record, I still can't fully find one.*

*Some things don't carry over completely into sentences. I've felt this
many times while writing this book. This chapter especially. I'm doing
my best right now to explain that day's self, and something still slips
out anyway.*

*Admitting that something slips out may itself be part of reading to the
end. Setting down even the part I couldn't fully capture.*

That evening I took out the twelve-page paper again. The one my mother
had left. It had been at the very bottom of the drawer.

I'd read it all, two years before, but had rarely taken it out since.
There'd been no reason to read it. I'd already read all of it.

*I hadn't taken it out that evening to find something new. I just wanted
to touch it. It sounds strange, but I wanted to feel the thickness of
the paper with my hand. The thinness at the point I'd stopped at the
third page, and the thickness once I'd read all twelve pages — the same
paper, and yet it felt different in my hand.*

I opened the third page again. "This should be enough to get you
through the winter."

*Back then I'd thought that sentence was all there was. Reading it
again now, in my mother's own way, that sentence had been enough on its
own too. Teaching how to get through a winter isn't not love. At
thirteen I'd thought it a small thing; now I think it was love too. It
just wasn't all of it.*

*There'd been more on the last page. Not knowing there was more had
made me who I was for two years. And now, I stand before the Seventh
Classification document carrying the person those two years made.*

I put the paper back in the drawer. This time I set it side by side
with the work guidelines that held the signature box. I didn't mean
anything particular by it. My hand simply set it down that way.

*Looking back later, that arrangement was strangely exact. My mother's
last line, and this city's empty last box, lay side by side in the same
drawer. Both were places that needed someone to fill them to be
complete. One, I'd filled far too late. The other, no one had filled
yet.*

I slept deeply that night, for the first time in a while. It was the
first time in three days.
