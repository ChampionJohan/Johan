# Chapter 24. A New Deliberation

> _Previously_ — There was no procedure to undo a confirmed deliberation. Not forbidden — simply absent. No one had ever proposed that such a procedure was needed. The Secretary said there was no path to undo it, but that "there is a path to open something new." Hora went home without learning what that meant.

The next morning I went to the reception desk the moment it opened.

The Secretary was already at his seat. Seeing me, he took out a document folder as though he'd been waiting to pick up where we'd left off the day before.

"…What you said yesterday. What's the path to open something new?"

"…A new deliberation."

At first I didn't understand what that meant.

"…A new deliberation? So you deliberate the same matter again?"

"…Not reopening the same matter — opening it as a new matter. In connection with the Seventh Classification relocation case, you'd bring forward a new agenda item requesting that the confirmed portion be reviewed again."

---

"…How is that different from undoing it?"

"…The result can end up similar. Once the new deliberation opens, part of the existing confirmed content could change, through the new deliberation's result. But procedurally, it's completely different. Undoing means erasing the existing decision. A new deliberation adds one more decision on top of it. The existing decision stays exactly as it was, and a new decision gets stacked on top."

I tried hard to understand that distinction.

"…So the original decision doesn't disappear?"

"…Right. In the record, both the original decision and the result of the new deliberation stay. Both remain. It's only that in implementation, the later decision takes precedence."

"…Then in practice that's the same as undoing it, isn't it."

"…In terms of the implementation result, it can look similar. But the record is different. This city erases nothing. A new deliberation keeps to that same principle too."

*Listening to this explanation, I thought this was a solution very much in this city's own character. It doesn't erase a wrong. Instead, it stacks a new decision on top of it. The wrong stays recorded, exactly as it happened, and only its effect gets covered over by the next decision. I felt this was an honest way of doing things. And at the same time, I felt it was a frightening way of doing things too.*

"…Has there been a new deliberation like this before?"

"…There has. I've convened several myself. But usually they're small in scale. Not a matter that questions the direction of a decision itself, the way this would — that's uncommon. Not like a document correction."

"…What about at this scale?"

The Secretary thought for a moment.

"…At this scale — not that I know of."

"…Not even once?"

"…Not since I've been convening, at least. I don't know the full record before that."

I heard that and understood this might become another "first." Just as no one had ever asked about a procedure for undoing something after confirmation, no one had ever opened a new deliberation on this scale either.

---

"…So is it hard to open a new deliberation?"

"…The procedure itself isn't hard. You fill out the agenda-filing form and submit it."

"…So I could do it right now?"

"…Yes. But one thing is needed."

The Secretary took a form out of a drawer and set it on the desk. I looked at the paper. It looked like the other forms. There were fields for the agenda's name, the related deliberation number, the grounds for bringing it forward. And at the very bottom.

*Proposer's full name.*

I looked at that field. It was different from the signature boxes I'd always seen on every other form. A signature box was a field you didn't have to fill. This one looked like a field that had to be filled.

"…Do I have to fill this in?"

"…Yes. A new deliberation can't be brought forward without the proposer's name."

---

"…Why? Every other procedure works without a name. Receiving, confirming, convening — all of them just use department and position."

"…A new deliberation is different. It's about reopening an existing decision. Someone has to step forward and open it. It's not a department opening it. A person opens it."

"…Why does it have to be a person? Can't a department name do it?"

"…A department knows its own responsibility. But proposing to open a new deliberation isn't within any department's responsibility. You can't file something outside a department's responsibility under a department's name. That's why a person's name is needed."

I went blank for a moment, hearing that. Everything I'd heard in this city, from the very start, had been about structures designed so that no one ever judged alone. And now this single form was pointing in exactly the opposite direction. Here, someone had to step forward alone.

"…Isn't that strange? This city is a place built so that one person doesn't judge alone. But a person's name is needed only when opening a new deliberation."

The Secretary nodded at that.

"…I think that's strange too. But as far as I understand it, up to confirmation, things move split among many people. But a proposal to reopen something confirmed can't be split. It doesn't work if several people each say a little of 'let's reopen it.' Someone has to say that whole sentence, entirely themselves, before the door opens."

"…Why can't it be split?"

"…If it's split, it isn't a new deliberation anymore — it's just an objection. Objections were already used up before confirmation. Saying you want to reopen something after confirmation is a much heavier statement than an objection. This city, it seems, hasn't yet found a way to share that weight."

I heard that answer and my eyes caught, again, on the word "yet." The Secretary kept using that word. Not yet, haven't found yet. I couldn't tell whether that word really meant things would be different someday, or whether it was just a word he attached out of habit.

"…So if I write my name in this field, what happens after that?"

"…It gets brought forward. Received at the desk, referred to the district, registered as a new agenda item. From there it's the same procedure as the original deliberation. Materials get gathered, a meeting is held, the Consensus reaches a decision."

"…How would my name be used in that process?"

"…It's listed as the proposer on the first page of the agenda document. And at every meeting, you'll be given a place to explain why you proposed this, Hora. Not just passing it on like the others do — a place where you'll have to step forward yourself and speak."

I heard that and had a faint sense that this would become the first time, in this city, that I placed the word "I" at the front of a document.

---

"…What happens once I write my name?"

I asked the question without quite knowing myself why I was asking it that way. It just felt like something I had to ask.

At that moment the Secretary's gaze dropped, slightly. His eyes, which had been on me, looked away from me for the first time. It was a very brief moment. Toward the form on the desk, and then toward the window that let in no view of outside. I didn't miss that movement.

*I remember this moment exactly. Up until that day, the Secretary had never once looked away from me, whatever I asked. What he didn't know, he said he didn't know and looked at me straight on; what he knew, he showed me all of it, looking straight at me. This time was different. He wasn't trying to hide something. I know that. But he was looking at something. Something I couldn't see.*

I only realized afterward that my voice had come out quieter than usual, asking that question. Those few seconds I remember with a strange clarity, even now. The way the corner of the document folder on the desk was slightly bent. The fluorescent light reflected in the window that let in no view of outside. The way the Secretary's hand lightly smoothed the edge of the form. Until just a few days before, I'd taken it for granted, in this room, that whatever I asked, an answer would come back immediately. That taken-for-grantedness broke, for the first time, in this moment.

*I want this scene, writing it, not to read as dramatic. He wasn't startled. He wasn't afraid. He simply looked, for one very brief moment, at something, and then looked back at me. But that very brief moment has stayed with me larger than every piece of precision he ever showed me before.*

The Secretary stayed that way for a few seconds, then looked at me again.

"…That..."

He started to speak, and stopped. I waited. It took some time before he spoke again.

"…Hora, could we leave it here for today?"

---

I was caught off guard for a moment. Up to that point, the Secretary had never once put off an answer. What he didn't know, he'd say, "I don't know," at once. This time was different. It wasn't even that he didn't know. It was that he wasn't going to answer.

"…Secretary, why won't you answer this one?"

"…It's not that I won't give you an answer. I have no answer to give right now."

"…Earlier you told me things even when you didn't know them. You said you didn't know, or that they didn't exist."

"…This isn't a question I can answer with 'I don't know' or 'it doesn't exist.'"

I didn't understand what he meant. But I could see, from his face, that pressing further would do no good. He wasn't trying to push me away. Something had genuinely come to an end right there.

"…Then when will you be able to answer?"

"…I don't know. This time, I really don't know."

I felt that this "I don't know" was different from every other "I don't know" I'd heard from him before. In every other moment, even while saying he didn't know, he could explain why he didn't know. This time he didn't even explain why. Or — maybe it wasn't that he couldn't explain, but that he'd decided not to. I couldn't tell the two apart.

*This is the scene I would come back to, and look at longest, in this whole book. Even now, writing this record, I still can't fully explain why the Secretary looked away that day. I do come to learn the answer later, but that's much further ahead. In this chapter I'll only set down this much: that he looked away from me for the first time.*

---

I took the form in my hand and left the reception desk.

*Proposer's full name.* That field was still empty. Walking with it in my hands, I thought about how this field was different from the signature box. A signature box was fine left empty. Leaving it unfilled was this city's habit. This field, left empty, meant nothing even started. A field that was fine empty, and a field that wasn't. That day, for the first time, I learned this city had both kinds of field.

Walking, I ran back through the faces of everyone I'd met so far. The initial receiving officer, the surveying officer, the format reviewer, the support-fund calculation officer, the convening officer, the record keeper. All of them had worked in fields without names. Department and position had been enough. Not one of them had ever, I thought, stood in front of a field like this. I felt, briefly, envious of them. Working in a nameless field carried no weight. What was always heavy was a field that carried a name.

On the way home I passed the water channel. The water was still making its earth-colored sound. I stood beside it and unfolded the form again. Proposer's full name. I thought, for a moment, about writing my own name in that field. I didn't take out my pen.

*I stood beside that water channel for a long while. I still didn't know where that water came from or where it went. Only this — that this water administratively did not exist, because it had never come up as an agenda item, and that the form now in my hand needed a name in order to exist at all — carried the same weight for me that evening. To exist, someone has to step forward. If no one steps forward, however much something flows, it might as well not exist.*

I told my aunt about the day. She took the form and looked it over for a long while.

"…The Secretary wouldn't tell you what happens once you fill this in?"

"…Right. For the first time, he couldn't answer."

"…First time?"

"…Yeah. Up to now, he's answered everything I asked. Even what he didn't know, he told me why he didn't know. This time even that didn't happen."

My aunt heard that and stayed still for a long while. I saw something on her face that wasn't usual for her. Not a worried face, not a face trying to stop me. It looked closer to a face trying to remember something.

"…Aunt, why the look?"

"…Nothing. I just feel like I've seen a field something like this once before, a long time ago."

"…Where?"

My aunt shook her head.

"…I don't remember well. It was a long time ago. So long I'm not even sure if this is a real memory or not."

I wanted to press her further, but she got up to clear the dishes and the conversation broke off naturally. I kept coming back to her expression, that night, for a long time.

My aunt handed the paper back to me and said,

"…Hora. I hope you don't decide right now what to write in that field."

"…I haven't decided yet either."

My aunt didn't say anything more. That night I put the form away in the drawer. The same drawer that held my mother's twelve pages, the first notice, the confirmation notice. The drawer had grown fairly thick by now. I didn't know, then, how many days or months that field would stay empty — or whether it might be filled sooner than that.

*Writing this chapter, I've decided to stop here. What happens once you write your name in it — I didn't know that day. I'd like whoever reads this record to stay here with me, not knowing, too. The answer comes much later. Until then, I still believe this question ought to stay just as it is — an empty field.*

*At the end of Part One, I saw an empty signature box for the first time. I couldn't explain, then, why that was strange. Now I'm standing in front of another empty field. This one is different from that one. No one has told me it's all right for this field to stay empty.*
