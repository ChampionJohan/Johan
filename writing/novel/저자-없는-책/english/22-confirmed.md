# Chapter 22. Confirmed

> _Previously_ — Hora pointed out that the Secretary's language had no subject in it. He fell silent a moment, then answered: "…How do I put in something that isn't there." Not an excuse, not an apology — a report of fact. That night, Hora accepted that this wasn't a problem she could solve by pushing him.

About ten days had passed since then.

I'd shown up at the district as usual, and there, on the bulletin board, was a notice. *The Seventh Classification residential clearance and relocation matter — Confirmation Notice.*

I stood in front of it for a long while.

Over those ten days I'd been busy with other things. I'd talked with the Secretary twice more, dug up more of his old minutes, and asked Tyche about the "one member" left in the minutes. Tyche didn't know who that was either. I'd felt those days, that whole stretch, as time spent drawing closer and closer to the center of this problem. Finding a subject in the Secretary's sentences had happened within those ten days too. I'd thought I had discovered something huge.

But while I was discovering that huge thing, the deliberation had simply been going along at its own pace. I only realized that, standing there in front of the bulletin board.

---

The notice was short. Deliberation confirmed, implementation date, contact for the department in charge. I read it three times. Three readings and the content stayed the same.

*I'm ashamed of the first thought that came to me, standing there. I found myself first recalling what I'd been doing over those ten days. Talking with the Secretary, discovering there was no subject, copying that sentence onto paper. I had been treating those ten days as time spent doing something important. And the whole time, the deliberation had simply been moving, by itself, toward confirmation.*

I ran to the reception desk and asked,

"…When did this get confirmed?"

The clerk checked the screen.

"…Four days ago."

"…Four days ago? I was talking with the Secretary then."

"…That's separate from this. The confirmation procedure runs on a set schedule."

"…Wasn't it supposed to be reviewed again before confirmation? For the objection I filed?"

"…The objection was already reviewed and the result came out. Three weeks ago. Since there was no further objection after that, it moved to the confirmation stage normally."

I heard that and had nothing to say back. It was true. The objection had already been reviewed and dismissed, and I hadn't filed a new one. I'd spent those ten days going around meeting people, not submitting documents.

---

I went to find the Secretary.

"…Why didn't you tell me about this ahead of time?"

The Secretary looked at me with a slightly startled face. That was the first time I'd ever seen him startled.

"…The confirmation schedule goes out by notice. I have no separate procedure for informing people individually."

"…But I'm the member in charge of this matter."

"…You are the member in charge, and the expected confirmation date was already written into the third-session materials. Last page."

I heard that and searched my memory. The third-session materials. I'd read that all the way through too. But I couldn't remember whether an item for the expected confirmation date had been there at all.

"…Let me check."

That evening I pulled out that material again and read it. On the last page, in small print at the bottom of the table, it read: *Expected confirmation date: 3 weeks hence.* I read that sentence and had no choice but to admit I'd read it and let it pass right by.

*I feel an urge, writing this passage, to make excuses. That table really was very small. It was crowded in among a lot of other numbers. But I also know that can't be an excuse. I was someone who read to the end. That table, too, was among the things I read to the end. It's only that I failed, that time, to read the weight of that one sentence.*

That evening I went back to find the first person I'd met at the Ninth Administrative Office. I wanted to know whether she'd known about the confirmation.

"…I heard it's confirmed."

"…Yes, I saw the notice too."

"…Did you expect it?"

"…Yes, it was always on that same schedule. I wasn't especially surprised."

"…I didn't know."

She looked at me for a moment and said, carefully,

"…I think you, Hora, were looking into this whole matter so closely that you ended up missing the schedule instead. I only look at my own share, so I always keep track of the schedule. That's the whole of my job, after all."

*This felt, oddly, painful to hear. I'd tried to look broadly and missed what was close at hand. This person had looked narrowly and, because of that, missed nothing. Looking broadly and not missing anything didn't seem to go together, in this city.*

---

"…So if, in those ten days, I hadn't gone to see the Secretary and had instead kept meeting people, or filed a new objection — could confirmation have been delayed?"

The Secretary thought for a moment on this question.

"…If you'd filed a new objection, the re-review procedure would have started again, and confirmation would have been delayed by that much."

"…So I was late."

"…You weren't late."

I looked up at that.

"…If I wasn't late, what am I?"

"…Whatever you did, this confirmation procedure would have gone ahead. If there's no new objection, it's set to automatically move to the confirmation stage once the set period passes. That happens the same way whether or not you came to see me."

"…What does that mean?"

"…It means there's no one in this procedure that it waits for."

---

I couldn't say anything for a long while after hearing that.

*At first I took this as comfort. As meaning it wasn't my fault that I was late. But the more I turned it over, the more I understood this wasn't comfort at all. This was a far more frightening thing to hear than comfort. That I wasn't late meant it wouldn't have mattered even if I'd been as fast as possible.*

"…So even if I'd filed a new objection that very night, the result would have been the same?"

"…No. I told you, if you'd filed a new objection, the procedure would have started over. That's different. But if you file nothing at all, the procedure goes ahead waiting for no one. That's the answer I can give you."

"…Then what was I waiting for?"

The Secretary didn't answer that question. It probably wasn't a question he could answer. It wasn't a question about what he knew — it was a question about what I had done.

Finally I asked just one more thing.

"…When did you know this would get confirmed, Secretary?"

"…From the beginning. The expected confirmation date gets set at the same time the threshold is set. It was already there in the very first meeting's materials, which I convened."

"…It was set from the very beginning?"

"…Yes. Though it was only an expected date, not a confirmed one. It was a date that could be delayed if an objection came in. Since there was no objection, it went ahead as expected."

Hearing that, I understood that the date set from the very beginning and the date I hadn't known about for those ten days were, in fact, the same date. It hadn't been hidden. Even when I read the third-session material, even when my eyes passed right over that sentence, I had already, all along, been holding that information in my own hands.

"…So was there anyone who registered for or against this confirmation?"

"…No. Confirmation isn't a procedure that asks for for or against. If there's an objection, it's blocked. If there isn't, it goes through, as is, once the set period passes."

I heard that and, only then, truly understood that confirmation wasn't something that happened because someone said "good." No one had approved. No one had stepped forward to oppose it either. It had simply gone through because no one stood in its way.

---

I looked up the full text of the confirmation notice and read it to the end. Forty-two pages. Grounds for confirmation, the final destination-site list, the implementation schedule. Items 3, 7, and 12 were included in the confirmed list too. With a footnote attached.

*Items 3, 7, and 12 are confirmed as the best available destination sites as of the implementation date. Detailed improvements to be carried out sequentially following implementation.*

I read that sentence three times. Three readings, and the meaning stayed the same. The three sites that had not been confirmed had simply been confirmed, under the name "best available destination site," for the very reason that they hadn't been confirmed. It wasn't that the waiting had ended. The waiting itself had ended.

At the very bottom of the last page there was, as in every document, a signature box. Empty. Looking at it, I wasn't surprised this time either. I already knew this wasn't a place that called for surprise.

The confirmed list had, listed one by one, the destination for all eleven hundred forty people. I ran my finger down the lines for the four hundred ten and counted again. The ones assigned to items 3, 7, and 12. Age range, household size, and beside those, one newly added column. *Confirmed as of implementation date.* The same typeface, the same size, as every other column. It wasn't that this line of four hundred ten was printed any differently. All of it sat together, in the same table, in the same way.

Looking at that table, I felt, for the first time, just how quietly a thing like this could pass by. No large print, no emphasis, no warning notice. Just one number in a table changing from "temporary" to "confirmed."

---

I took the confirmation notice and went to find Tyche. She already knew.

"…Hora, you heard the confirmation news."

"…Yes. I just finished reading the full notice."

"…How was it?"

"…Items 3, 7, and 12 got confirmed as 'best available destination sites.' It's not that they weren't confirmed because they weren't confirmed — the unconfirmed state itself simply got confirmed, exactly as it stood."

Tyche was silent for a moment, hearing that.

"…So it is. Even hearing it now, it sounds strange to me too."

"…You find it strange too, Tyche?"

"…Yes. But by the regulation, there's no problem. 'Best available destination site' is a phrase that's in the regulation. Used when there's nothing better available."

"…What is it that forced them to use that phrase?"

"…The implementation date. Because it wasn't pushed back."

"…Who didn't push that date back?"

Tyche thought for a while longer before answering this question.

"…No one pushed it back. There was no new reason to push it back, so it went ahead as originally set. To push it back, someone would have had to raise it — 'it needs to be pushed back' — and that never happened."

"…What if I'd kept filing objections?"

"…Then a reason to delay would have kept coming up. But you didn't file any objections in those ten days, Hora."

I couldn't say anything back to that. Tyche hadn't said it to blame me. She was simply stating a fact. Knowing that fact, I still felt uneasy.

I passed the water channel on my way home. The water was still flowing. I stood beside it a moment and wondered whether this channel, too, could someday be confirmed as some "best available" thing. Since it wasn't on any agenda yet, it seemed unlikely to ever be confirmed. Only once an item comes up as an agenda can it be confirmed at all, and only once confirmed can a way to undo it exist — I thought about that in reverse, for the first time, that day.

I told my aunt about the day. She listened for a long while and asked,

"…So what happens now?"

"…It's confirmed, so it'll go ahead on the implementation date."

"…Isn't there a way to undo it?"

I couldn't answer that question right away.

"…I'll have to look, to know."

"…Will looking turn one up?"

"…I don't know. But nothing's confirmed that there isn't one, either."

My aunt didn't say anything back to that. After a moment she asked again.

"…What was the Secretary's face like, when he said that?"

"…Same as always. Not sorrowful, not cold. Just a face stating a fact."

"…That's stranger, still."

"…I think so too."

My aunt got up to reheat the soup and said,

"…Hora. Those people — the four hundred ten. Do they know about this?"

I couldn't answer that question right away. Over those ten days I'd gone around meeting people, but I hadn't met a single one of the people actually grouped into the Seventh Classification. I only knew them by number and category. I'd never once seen a single face.

"…I don't know. The notice must have gone out to them too."

"…If it went out, what do you think they're doing right now?"

I never managed to answer that question. That night I pulled out the confirmation notice again and went over it once more, front to back. There was no mention anywhere of a procedure for undoing it. Whether the absence meant it was forbidden, or simply meant it wasn't written, I still couldn't tell the difference, even then.

*That distinction is what I set out to find, starting the next day.*

*I hesitated a long while writing this chapter. It would have been so much easier to write, "I was late, and that's why it turned out this way." That would at least give the story a cause. A story where things would have turned out differently if I'd only been a little faster. But the Secretary didn't allow me that story. There's no one in this procedure that it waits for, he told me, plainly. At first I felt comforted hearing that. Thinking it over again, I understood it was a far more frightening thing to hear. If I had been the cause, at least next time I could have done things differently. But if there is no cause, then next time, too, I could miss it the exact same way.*

*That night, before I fell asleep, I pulled out the confirmation notice one more time. A forty-two-page document. I confirmed once more that nowhere in it was there any sentence saying "I was late" or "you were late." The concept of lateness itself didn't exist anywhere inside this document. No waiting, and no lateness. There was only the arrival of a set date, and the fact that on that day, the matter was processed.*
