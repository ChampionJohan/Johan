# Chapter 28. The One Who Keeps Being Called In

> _Previously_ — Temel wrote his name on every application. "Not
> knowing isn't a reason not to write it." He said that name kept
> getting him called in for confirmation after confirmation. Tyche
> called it "a loss," and I couldn't refute her.

The next day, I went back to the market again after school. I wasn't
going to see Temel. I wanted to see what the confirmations looked
like — the ones he'd said would call him in "two more times."

I asked at the front desk about the schedule, and one happened to be
set for that afternoon. Since I had no district business at that hour,
I asked whether I could tag along. The desk clerk didn't find it odd
and said I could.

"…How long do these usually run, start to finish?"

"…Depends on the matter. Ten minutes if it's short, up to thirty if
it's long."

"…How many can be scheduled in a day?"

"…He's only got one today. But if several departments are involved,
some people have two or three in a day."

"…Does that happen often — someone like that?"

"…Not often. People who write their names are rare to begin with."

Hearing that, I confirmed again just how unfamiliar a sight this was
in this city. It seemed so unfamiliar that no one had ever seriously
thought about what it meant.

"…Is watching one of these normally allowed?"

"…It's not closed. Everything here is open."

*That answer sounded obvious to me at the time. Looking back now, all
of this city's dread is contained in that one line. It wasn't closed.
Nothing was hidden. And still it was frightening.*

*Up until then I'd thought fear came from things that were hidden.
Frightening because you couldn't see it, frightening because it was
done in secret. This city was the reverse. There were frightening
things you could see all of, in full. I saw that with my own eyes for
the first time that day.*

---

The confirmation room was small. One table, three chairs. One official
sat there, and Temel sat across from him. I sat in a chair in the
corner and watched.

Nothing was on the walls. There was one window, but like the meeting
hall's windows, it didn't show the outside. Every building in this
city was built this way. It didn't seem meant to keep the inside from
being seen — it was simply the kind of window this city used. Looking
at that window, I thought there probably wasn't another city with so
many windows you couldn't see out of.

The air was cool. Rooms like this were always a little cool. Whether
that was because no one was meant to stay long, or because there was
simply no heating at all, I didn't know.

The official opened a folder.

"…I'll confirm the application number. Your name is Mr. Temel,
correct?"

"…Yes."

"…And the filing date was four days ago, correct."

"…Yes."

"…And the application's content is a request to confirm a person's
whereabouts, is that right?"

"…Yes, that's right."

The official turned to the next page.

"…This was already confirmed yesterday, but it needs confirming again
today. I apologize."

"…That's fine."

This exchange went on for about ten minutes. The questions were almost
identical to yesterday's. Listening beside them, I thought something
was wrong, but neither the official nor Temel seemed to find it
strange at all.

---

The official asked for a signature. Temel wrote his name in the field
at the bottom of the confirmation sheet. His hand moved quickly,
practiced. Watching him, I felt again how thoroughly writing a name
had become second nature to this man.

The official stamped the sheet once and put the papers in order.

"…That concludes today's matter. Thank you."

"…Yes, thank you."

They thanked each other. I found that strange to hear. I couldn't tell
what either of them had to be thankful for, and yet both seemed to
mean it sincerely.

Once the confirmation was over, the official said,

"…The next confirmation is set for the day after tomorrow."

"…Again?"

"…This matter goes through three related departments. Each department
has to confirm it once."

"…Can't they all confirm it together, at once?"

The official checked the papers once and answered.

"…There's no procedure for doing it that way."

"…If there isn't one, can't one be made?"

"…That isn't within my authority."

That answer nearly made me laugh. Not because it was funny — because
it was too familiar. I only passed it on. I only put it in order. And
now, that isn't within my authority. It felt like every phrase I'd
heard in months in this city had gathered together, right here.

*I couldn't bring myself to hate that official. He wasn't a bad
person. He was even kind. He read the papers carefully, and when he
apologized his face was sincerely sorry. And still, the work he did
was to make the exact same thing get confirmed three times over.*

I asked the official something too.

"…Doesn't it wear on you, confirming the same thing every time like
this?"

The official looked a little flustered. It seemed to be the first time
anyone had asked him that.

"…I… I'm only carrying out the procedure I've been assigned."

"…But this is already how many times this person's come here."

"…That isn't something I have visibility into. I only see today's
matter."

"…Only today's?"

"…Yes. There's no one who sees the whole picture. Each matter is
handled separately, by its own department."

*This may have been the most important answer in this chapter. That
there is no one who sees the whole. The official only saw today's
matter, and yesterday's official had probably only seen yesterday's.
No one had the whole picture of how many times Temel had been called
in that week. I was the only one who knew. And that was only because
I had followed him around.*

---

Coming out of the confirmation room, I asked Temel,

"…Is that all? Is that really the whole of what it means, being
called in?"

"…Yes. That's all there is to it."

"…It's less than I expected."

Temel smiled a little.

"…That's right. Not much to it. But it keeps happening."

"…How long does it keep happening?"

"…As long as the application's still open. I've got my name on three
matters here, so until all three finish."

"…Do they finish, though?"

"…Usually. But if a new matter comes up, that keeps going too."

"…So it could never end?"

This time Temel didn't answer right away. He walked a moment and said,

"…Could be. I don't know either."

*I let that "I don't know" pass without much thought, at the time. I
only realized later how often, and how calmly, this man said "I don't
know." It wasn't evasion. It was said because he truly didn't know.*

"…Is it only like this in this city? Aren't other cities not this
bad?"

"…Depends on the city. Some finish with one confirmation. Some go
longer."

"…Which is this one?"

"…The long kind. I think it's probably because of how the departments
are divided. The more it's split up, the more people there are to
confirm it."

"…So what if the departments weren't split up?"

"…Then one department would have to carry the whole thing, and that
would be a problem too, probably. I'm not in a position to say which
way is better."

Temel's pace stayed unhurried even as he said this. No sign of rushing
at all. I found that strangely enviable. I think I'd have been angry
by then, in his place.

"…Doesn't it make you angry?"

"…It did, the first few times. Now I just tend to save the energy I'd
spend being angry."

"…Save it for what?"

"…For the next city."

---

That evening, Temel was called in again for another matter. A
different building this time, a different official. I went along
again. The room's layout was nearly identical. One table, three
chairs, a window you couldn't see through.

"…I'll reconfirm the application number. Your name is…"

"…Temel."

"…Yes, Mr. Temel. Apart from the previous confirmation, this
department needs to verify your identity again, please."

"…Yes."

"…What you're confirming today concerns the estimated district of
residence. Please confirm whether it matches what you told us
before."

"…Yes, it matches."

"…Thank you. Your signature, please."

Temel wrote his name again. I watched him do it once more. The same
hand movement as before. The same expression. There was weariness in
it, but no irritation.

Out in the hallway, other people were waiting their turn. They all
held papers, and they were all quiet. It was an ordinary sight in this
city. The only difference was that those people needed to come only
once, and Temel needed to come many times.

Coming out of this room, I asked,

"…How is this different from the one before?"

"…Different department. That one was the whereabouts-confirmation
department. This is the residence-district-confirmation department."

"…But they asked the exact same thing."

"…They did. But since it's a different department, they each have to
confirm it separately, I suppose."

"…How many more departments are left?"

"…One more. Tomorrow."

*Writing this passage, I count the numbers again. Three applications,
a confirmation from each department, and it repeating. How many times
a single application could get someone called in — I made my first
rough estimate that day. It looked like it would come to at least ten
times over. And that was for only one of the three matters.*

That day, I understood exactly what review was.

It wasn't punishment. There was no fine. No prison. No one laid a hand
on Temel. No one raised their voice. The confirmation rooms were
pleasant places, and every official knew how to apologize.

And yet it never ended.

As long as a document with a name on it existed somewhere, every
person and every department handling that document had to confirm
that name. Confirmation was a procedure, and the procedure was
correct. No one did anything wrong. And yet all those correct things,
gathered together, added up to calling one person in, over and over.

*I wanted to call this a punishment. Calling it a punishment would
make it clear, somehow. But it wasn't punishment. A punishment has to
be a punishment someone decided on, and this was something that simply
kept going, decided by no one. That day, for the first time, I thought
this was more frightening than a punishment.*

That day I went home and told my aunt all of it. The confirmation
room, the window, the signature, the second room, the third room. My
aunt listened quietly and said,

"…Is there a fine, or isn't there?"

"…No fine."

"…Does he go to prison?"

"…No."

"…Does anyone hit him?"

"…No. No one lays a hand on him."

My aunt thought for a moment and said,

"…Then what's frightening about it, Hora? That's just an
inconvenience."

I couldn't refute her right away. She was right. No fine, no prison,
no violence. "Inconvenience" wasn't a wrong word for it.

*And still I kept thinking about that "inconvenience," that night. An
inconvenience two or three times a day, repeated for months, years.
Could that really stay just an inconvenience? I didn't think so. But I
couldn't find the words to explain that to my aunt, then.*

My aunt asked again.

"…But why does that man keep writing it? He could just stop."

"…He said every application needs a name. That's what the work of
finding people is."

"…Then he should just not do that work."

"…Then he can't find anyone."

My aunt didn't answer that. Instead she looked at me a long while and
said,

"…You're seeing a lot of that man lately."

"…I am."

"…Why?"

I couldn't answer that question right away. It was too many visits
already to say it was just curiosity, and I wasn't even sure what I
wanted to find out, to say I was trying to learn something.

"…I don't know. I just keep wanting to see him."

My aunt didn't press further. She just nodded and turned off the
light. I lay awake a long while in the dark.

---

On the way home, Temel had asked me,

"…Why do you come watch this, Hora?"

"…I'm curious. What happens if you write a name."

"…Do you know now?"

"…A little. But I'm still not sure."

"…Not sure of what?"

"…Why this is so frightening. When nothing even happens."

Temel nodded.

"…That's right. Nothing happens. That's all there is."

Hearing that, I stopped walking. Temel stopped too.

"…But it keeps not happening. That's the problem."

*I held onto that line for a long time. It keeps not happening. Not
that something bad happens, but that nothing happening keeps
happening, over and over. I saw that day, for the first time, how much
that could wear a person down, in this city.*

That night, once home, I opened the drawer again. The one holding two
names and one sentence. I put nothing in it that night. Instead I just
looked into it for a long while.

*Writing this chapter, I confirm again why this city is truly
frightening. No one did anything wrong. And yet a person truly did
end up called in without end. I learned here that this can happen even
without a villain.*

*I bring up this drawer more than once in this book. I wrote that I
put nothing in it that night, but in truth I nearly did put something
in. The sentence was "There is no one who sees the whole." I took the
paper out, and in the end put it back without writing it. It was
because I still wasn't certain what it meant.*

The next morning, I told Tyche what I'd seen the day before. Tyche
listened quietly and said,

"…That's not a bad thing, is it. It's just that there are several
confirmation steps."

"…But it keeps happening."

"…That's probability. The more matters with a name on them, the more
there is to confirm. It isn't strange."

I couldn't answer that. Tyche was right again. And yet, standing
before that rightness, I couldn't shake the feeling that something was
missing.
