# Chapter 48. The Unsigned Book

> _Previously_ — For the first time, the Secretary came to find Hora on
> his own, not outside working hours but within them. He still spoke
> without a subject. But in one sentence, he said, "I was curious," and
> he didn't correct it.

Before closing this record, there's one name I've put off writing that
I need to set down now.

That person belonged to the Seventh Classification. One of the nine
hundred and twenty-seven. One of those the reexamination back then had
not released. I'd met that person a long time before, and heard the
name then. I hadn't written it in this record until now. I didn't think
it was mine to write.

That person's name was Jeong Su-a.

*I think back again to the day I met her. A low house near the water
channel. Laundry hung out in the yard, and she was neither wary of me
nor welcoming. She just told me to sit. I didn't ask much that day. I
asked her name, I asked her age, I asked how long she'd lived there. It
wasn't a remarkable conversation. I hadn't remembered her name that day
because she was special. I'd simply realized, for the first time that
day, that she was someone who could be called by a name, not only a
number.*

---

I wrote this name on the twelfth page of this document, in the entry
for the affected roster. The place that had only ever read 7-614.
There was no box, originally, meant to hold a name beside the number. I
didn't erase the number. I wrote the name into the margin beside it.

*I hesitated over this for a long time. Whether it was better to erase
the number, or write beside it. In the end I decided not to erase it.
The number, too, was part of what had happened to this person. I didn't
want to make it as if it had never happened.*

This wasn't a formal procedure. I didn't bring this before a new
deliberation. I simply wrote three syllables of a name, by hand, in the
margin of a document I had held onto for a long time.

Picking up the pen, I thought again of the moment I'd written my own
name in the signature box, much earlier. I'd written, then, that it
took about three seconds. This time it took longer. This time it wasn't
my own name — it was someone else's name I was writing down in her
place. I tried to copy each stroke exactly the way Jeong Su-a herself
had told it to me. The handwriting, of course, came out as mine. That
bothered me. This name belonged to her, but the writing came from my
hand.

*I still don't quite know how to settle this mismatch. I wasn't writing
on her behalf. I hadn't asked her before writing it, either. I simply
took a name I'd heard long before, and wrote it, with my own hand, into
a document I held. Whether this was the right thing to do, I never
became fully certain, to the end. I only decided to believe that it was
better than leaving her as only a number.*

---

I went to the reception desk and resubmitted the document.

"…Please receive this again."

The person at the desk took the document and looked it over. Their
hand stopped at the roster entry.

"…This is an entry not provided for by regulation."

"…I know. But it's not forbidden either, is it."

The person at the desk thought for a moment, then spoke.

"…That's right. It's not forbidden."

"…Have you never seen anything like this before?"

"…No, this is the first time. A name written beside a number."

"…Does it strike you as strange?"

The person at the desk looked at the document once more before
answering.

"…Not strange, exactly — a little unfamiliar. But unfamiliar and not
allowed are different things."

I thought that answer summed up this whole city once again. What's
unfamiliar and what's not allowed are different things.

He stamped it and put the document in the file box.

"…Received."

That was all. The name Jeong Su-a now existed, inside a document of
this city, beside a number, written by hand.

---

*I want to set this moment beside that earlier signature. Nothing had
happened then, either. Nothing happened this time either. The person
at the reception desk wasn't surprised. They stamped it, and filed it.
I think these two ordinarinesses are the whole of what I learned in
this book. A thing that changes the world comes in a shape the world
doesn't notice.*

I didn't expect this name to change anything by itself. Jeong Su-a was
still one of the nine hundred and twenty-seven. She hadn't been
released from relocation-target status. This one entry didn't change
that fact.

*I thought again of something I once said to Tyche. Maybe nothing
changes. But it won't be empty. I applied those words, this time, to
the margin beside the number. Her name being written there doesn't
cancel the relocation. But that space is no longer empty now.*

---

From there I went straight to tell the Secretary. He took the document
and looked it over.

"…Confirmed. No violation of regulation."

"…Is that all?"

"…Procedurally, that's all. Only—"

He paused for a moment.

"…Only, entries like this could go on happening, from here on. There's
no regulation to stop it."

I understood that wasn't a threat, but a statement of fact. That was
always how things were, in this city.

"…Would you like there to be more of these, Secretary?"

The Secretary took a while choosing his answer to that.

"…That isn't mine to decide. Only, it's not thought to be a bad
thing."

I knew even that much was rare from him. I thanked him and left.

---

Tyche heard about this that afternoon and asked.

"…What changes, writing that?"

This was the same question Tyche had asked me before, about the
signature box. I answered similarly to before.

"…I don't know. But now this document doesn't just have a number. It
has a name too."

"…Is that all?"

"…Yeah. That's all."

This time Tyche didn't press further. She just nodded.

"…I guess that's enough."

I was a little surprised by that. I hadn't expected Tyche to accept it
so easily.

"…Enough?"

"…Yeah. Even if nothing big changes, that much is enough."

Tyche looked out the window for a moment, then spoke again.

"…I still think all of this is probability, Hora. That Su-a wasn't
released before, and that her name got written today. All of it is a
vacancy and a number and a procedure lining up."

"…I know. You've said that from the start."

"…But now, even as I say it, I can't feel like that's the whole of it,
the way I used to."

I looked at her, surprised by that.

"…What does that mean?"

"…I can't explain it. It's just that before, saying probability was the
end of it. Lately, even after I say probability, something is left
over."

*I want to render this exactly. Tyche didn't convert. I don't want to
use this sentence as evidence of a conversion either. I only set down
the fact that she said this, that day. Tyche is still a person who
believes in probability. Only, something more has come to stand beside
that belief. What that is, even Tyche herself hasn't found a name for
yet.*

---

I told my aunt this story too. She listened for a long while, then
asked.

"…Did you ever meet her?"

"…Yeah. A long time ago."

"…What kind of person was she?"

It took me some time to choose an answer to that.

"…Ordinary. Just, exactly a person like me. Except that she had a
number attached to her."

My aunt nodded.

"…Then you did well."

"…Did well how? Her relocation status never got lifted."

"…But you still wrote her name."

I thought about that for a long while. My aunt wasn't promising
anything large. She was simply acknowledging something small.

"…Aunt, is this all over now, do you think?"

My aunt set down her rice bowl.

"…What's supposed to be over?"

"…I don't know. I just feel like something should be over."

"…Isn't it less that it's over, and more that you've come this far?"

"…What's the difference?"

"…If it's over, there's no what comes next. If you've come this far,
there's still a what comes next."

I thought about that distinction for a long while. I was about to close
this record. But by my aunt's account, closing a record and a story
ending were different things.

"…Then how far have I come?"

"…I don't know that either, Hora. That's probably not something even
you know."

---

That evening I walked along the water channel.

There was no particular reason. I just wanted to walk. The sun was
setting, and the sound of the water was the same as always. Listening
to it, I thought of the beginning of this record. The very first time
I'd seen this water channel, I hadn't even known why it was strange. Now
I know where this water comes from. I also know that this city has
still never once brought it before an agenda.

Walking, I counted back through this whole record. Forty-eight
chapters. Of what truly happened within them, there wasn't much. I read
a document. I wrote a name in the signature box. Today I wrote another
name beside a number. That was all of it. Everything else was someone's
words, someone's explanation, someone's silence. Writing this record, I
came to know again that those silences, in truth, held more than the
words did.

Walking along the channel, I came close to the edge of the city. From
here on, the water's origin could only be guessed at. No one had ever
brought this stretch before an agenda. I no longer bother pressing the
question, myself, either. Some things simply flow on, unrevealed. That,
too, is something I learned in this city.

Someone in worn clothes was sitting by the water.

He said nothing at all.

---

I walked past him and kept going. I didn't look back.

*I've written these two sentences more carefully than anything else in
this record. I don't know who that person is. I don't know his name
either. His face wasn't angry, and it wasn't sad. He was simply sitting
by the water. Across this whole book I've passed someone like him a
few times. Once on a road, once by the water, and once more, not long
ago. Each time I tried not to put any special meaning to it. I mean to
do the same, this time too.*

*Only, closing this record, I want to set down this one fact: that he
was there. Nothing more than that. I write nothing further.*

---

On the way back along the channel, I ran into Tyche. She said she was
on her way home from work. We walked a little way in silence.

"…Did you write it?" Tyche asked.

"…Yeah."

"…Beside the number?"

"…Yeah. I didn't erase the number."

Tyche nodded. Then, after a long while, she spoke.

"…Nothing will change for her. She's still relocation-target. Still
tagged the same."

"…I know."

"…And you still wrote it?"

"…Yeah."

Tyche didn't ask any further. We walked together as far as the fork in
the road, and there we each went our own way. Tyche raised a hand once
as she went.

*Writing this part, I want to leave it exactly on record that Tyche
never, in the end, agreed with me. She wasn't on my side. But she
walked with me as far as the fork. I learned, in this city, that those
two things can exist together.*

---

Back home, I opened the drawer.

My mother's twelve pages. One name from eleven years before. Temel's
name. Two hundred-some days of notes. The paper marked 927. Three
official documents from other cities. On top of it all, I added one
more sheet, on which I'd written a short line about today. *Jeong
Su-a. Written into the document today.*

I looked into that drawer for a long while. The papers had grown
fairly thick by now. When my mother first left twelve pages in this
drawer, she couldn't have known it would come to this. Neither had I.

*I wanted to leave this drawer as the last image of this record. On top
of my mother's twelve pages, the names and sentences of the people I've
met piled up, one by one. I don't want to call this an inheritance. An
inheritance is something handed down by someone; this drawer is
something I filled, one sheet at a time, myself. Only, it's true that
my mother laid down that first page.*

---

I mean to close this record here.

When I began this book, I didn't think I would end up proving anything
large. Even now, I've proven nothing. Of the one thousand one hundred
and forty in the Seventh Classification, only two hundred and thirteen
were released. Nine hundred and twenty-seven remain exactly as they
were. Jeong Su-a is one of them. I only wrote that one name into a
document, and it didn't change her circumstances.

There was a time I wished this book would end triumphantly. Everything
overturned, the hidden person exposed, all the wronged released at
once. No such ending came. I don't regret that anymore, now. If such an
ending had come, I probably would have finished writing this whole book
without properly learning anything at all.

Tyche believes in chance, to the end. I believe there's something more,
to the end. Both of us read the same documents, and saw the same facts.
Our conclusions diverged. This book doesn't erase that divergence. I
won't write that Tyche was wrong. She won't write that I was wrong
either. I think that much is the whole of what this book can give the
two of us.

The Secretary is still the Secretary. Tomorrow, too, he will convene
and pass things on. Only, once, exactly once, he said "I." I won't
write that this one time saved him. But I will write that this one
time can't be made as if it never happened.

I don't know what city Temel is in now. He's probably, even now, still
writing his own name onto someone else's application. And probably, in
payment for it, being called in somewhere, over and over.

Jeong Su-a still lives, even now, in that house near the water channel.
Still carrying the relocation-target tag. I never went back to see her
again. I couldn't be sure I had the right to. Only, I know that her
name now sits, at least in one document, beside a number, written down.

My name is now, at least, in the documents of three other cities. I
didn't write it there. Who did, I don't know. Even now, closing this
record, I still don't know.

---

I wrote this record in four parts. It began with someone who was no
one in particular, moved through learning that nobody did it, passed
through the question of who knows, and arrived at a name. I didn't
live according to this order, decided beforehand. Looking back, having
lived it, it simply turned out to be this order. Maybe every story
sorts itself out that way. While you're living it, the order isn't
visible; only once it's all written does it come to have parts and
chapters.

I don't think what I did in this city was special. I only read to the
end, and looked at an empty space, and wrote a name. That's all. It's
no great resolve, and no heroic story. I was only a child who'd read
her mother's last line two years too late, and after that, never wanted
to do it again.

The Consensus hid nothing. Everything written in this record was
something anyone could have read. I only read, to the end, what others
passed over after only the summary. If something had been hidden, I
couldn't have written this book. Nothing was hidden. And yet it still
took me forty-eight chapters to finish writing it.

This book has no author. The way no document in this city has one. I
write my own name onto this record. That doesn't make it my book,
though — I know that now. I'm only the one who wrote it. What happened
within it, I didn't make happen.

Writing this last chapter, I felt, for a long time, the temptation to
declare something. That I believe now, or that I know now. I decided
against it. In the first sentence of this book, I wrote that there was
no reason at all for me to end up in that seat. Even reaching this last
chapter, I never fully found that reason. Only, I learned, writing this
book, that believing there's no reason at all, and believing you simply
haven't found the reason yet, are different things.

*I once heard an old story. A story in which no name ever appears.
Someone raised, by chance, to a high seat. A decree that couldn't be
undone. A step taken forward with no summons at all. I heard that
story, too, didn't end triumphantly. I won't retell that story here. I
only set down that it and this record are asking the same question.
Over the same document, one person says there was nothing there at
all, and another says all of this cannot possibly have been chance. I
have written this record, up to here, so that neither side wins
completely.*

---

I never once met him.

And I cannot bring myself to believe all of this was chance.

*(The End.)*
