# Chapter 27. Why Do You Write It

> _Previously_ — I met Temel at the north market. He was a man who
> moved from city to city doing work finding people, and in this city
> too he had written his own name on an application. "If there's a box
> to write in, don't you write in it?" That was so simple, I couldn't
> answer.

The next day, Temel was in the same spot. In front of the notice
board, in almost the same posture as the day before. The sheets were
one fewer than yesterday. One new sheet had been added too.

*I went straight to the market after school that day. I told my aunt I
needed to read more district materials. That wasn't entirely a lie. I
really was going to read something. It's only that it wasn't a
document — it was a person.*

A few other people besides Temel were standing at the board. They all
seemed to have come to check their own notices. Nobody paid Temel any
attention.

"…Back again."

"…Yes. There's still something I wanted to ask."

Temel took his hand off the board and looked at me. There was no
annoyance in his face. Just a look that was making time for me.

"…What do you want to know?"

"…What I asked yesterday. Why do you write it? You don't have to."

But first, curious about the missing sheet, I asked that instead.

"…Did you find someone?"

"…No. The time limit passed, so it came down. That's the rule here.
After ten days it has to come off the board."

"…What about that person, then?"

"…I keep looking. Other ways. The board's just one way among
several."

---

Temel seemed to think a moment, then gave the same short answer as
yesterday.

"…Because there's a box to write in."

"…That's all?"

"…Yes."

I felt that answer wasn't enough. I thought there had to be more.
Some conviction, some principle, some resolve formed out of
something he'd been through. But I saw nothing like that on Temel's
face. It really was a face for whom that was all there was.

"…But nobody writes it. Here."

"…That's exactly what makes it stranger, to me. That there's a box
and nobody uses it."

"…That's strange to you?"

"…Where I'm from, it was. If there was a box, you put something in
it. Right or wrong, good or bad. Leaving it blank meant the box lost
its meaning."

*I turned this over for a long time. I'm turning it over even now,
writing this record. Leaving a box blank makes the box lose its
meaning. I thought about the opposite too. Couldn't leaving a box
blank make that box grow heavier instead? That's how it was in this
city. If it had been filled, I never would have known how heavy that
empty signature box could be.*

"…What was the place you're from like?"

"…I've lived in a lot of places. Not just one."

"…Where do you live now, then?"

"…It's hard to say I live anywhere in particular. I keep moving."

"…Isn't it lonely?"

Temel didn't answer right away. He looked toward the market for a
moment and said,

"…Sometimes. But there are times I meet the person I was looking for,
and that's what gets me through."

"…Do you meet them often?"

"…No. Not often. But sometimes."

I could only guess at what was folded into that "sometimes." I didn't
ask further. I'd learned in this city that some questions are better
left unasked, out of respect.

---

"…So what was good about writing your name here?"

Asking this, I expected a good answer. Something along the lines of
having gained something this city couldn't give.

Temel smiled a little at the question. It was an expression I hadn't
seen from him before.

"…Nothing good, particularly."

"…Nothing?"

"…No. All I got was being called in, over and over. But that's
something you brace for going in."

"…Then why do you keep writing it?"

"…Well. Writing it is my work. That's what finding people is. You
have to file an application to find someone, and the application
needs a name."

"…Not that. You're not even from here. Why bother?"

Temel looked at me for a moment. His face searched for an answer.

"…It's just habit, I suppose. I do it wherever I go."

"…Was it always like that?"

"…No. At first I didn't write it either."

That surprised me a little.

"…When did you start, then?"

"…A long time ago. I fell apart once, when I was young."

"…Fell apart?"

"…I misjudged something badly, once. After that there was a time I
lived as if running from it. Then when I came back, I started writing
it."

Temel told this very briefly. His tone made me hesitant to ask more.
I didn't ask more.

"…What do you mean, came back?"

"…Just that — I mean I decided to start again. Whenever I start
something over, I write my name first. That's my way of doing it."

*I stopped more than once, writing this passage. Even now I still feel
the urge to press further into what happened to Temel. But I didn't
press him that day, and I can't write more than this in this record
either. Leaving what's unknown as unknown seems like the right way to
treat this person, at least.*

Temel looked back at the board, as if changing the subject.

"…So is everything in this city like that — this business with the
signature box, and everything else too?"

"…Like what?"

"…Where there's no one doing anything, but there's still a result."

I thought about that for a moment. The Consensus, the four hundred
people, passing it on and putting it in order and confirming. All of
it fit.

"…Yes. Exactly like that."

"…That's something. I've never seen a city like this."

"…Something, or strange?"

Temel smiled a little.

"…Both."

That smile was the only light moment in the whole conversation that
day. It was only seeing him smile that I truly felt how flatly he'd
been saying such heavy things until then.

*I think I was a little disappointed by this answer, at the time. I'd
expected some bigger reason. I'm ashamed of that disappointment now.
I myself read to the end simply out of habit, and yet I wanted a
bigger reason out of someone else.*

---

Just then, a person passed by beside us. He was a man in worn clothes,
walking slowly, headed straight for somewhere. He glanced once at the
notice board and passed on without a word. I looked at him for a
moment, but there was nothing remarkable about him, and I soon turned
back to Temel.

"…So how many applications have your name on them right now?"

"…In this city alone? Three."

"…Three means how many times you get called in?"

"…Two more scheduled by tomorrow."

"…Isn't it hard?"

Temel shrugged slightly.

"…It's hard. But hard and not doing it are two different problems."

That was a short answer, but I held onto it for a long time.

---

"…Could I write it too, then?"

The question came out of me before I knew it. I surprised even myself,
asking it.

Temel looked straight at me. It was the first time his face had turned
serious.

"…You could. If there's a box."

"…What happens if I write it, though?"

"…You'd end up like me, probably. Called in."

"…Not that. What else changes?"

This time Temel didn't answer right away. He looked at the board for a
moment and said,

"…I don't know that either. What I wrote is something that happened
to me. Not something that's going to happen to you, Hora."

It was the first time he'd said my name. I don't remember when he
learned it. He must have asked at the front desk. That was how this
city worked.

*That day, for the first time, I asked someone other than myself what
happens if you write it. And for the first time, I heard the answer,
"I don't know." I thought it was nothing much of an answer at the
time. I would come to learn later just how different a weight this
"I don't know" could carry. That's a story for later.*

The sun was starting to lean. It was the hour the market began
winding down. I brought up the signature box a little more before I
left.

"…Why do you think nobody in this city writes it? What do you
think?"

"…I don't really know for sure. But the other places I've seen had
similar reasons, I think."

"…What reasons?"

"…If you write it, it becomes yours. Whether it's good or bad. Leave
it blank, and it's just something that happened — it belongs to no
one."

"…Isn't that comfortable, though? If it belongs to no one, nobody
gets wronged."

"…That's right. That's why everyone does it that way. It isn't a bad
thing. I envy that, myself, sometimes."

"…You envy it?"

"…Yes. Every time, something becomes mine. It would be easier for me
too, if I didn't write it. But I've found I can't."

"…Why can't you?"

Temel was quiet a moment. Then he answered, briefly.

"…I've tried not writing it, once. That was the worst it's ever been."

---

"…So you never know, every single time?"

"…Right. Every time, I don't know. I don't know each time I write
it."

"…How do you write it if you don't know?"

Temel shrugged again. It felt slightly different this time.

"…Not knowing isn't a reason not to write it."

Hearing that, I couldn't say anything for a moment. The market noise
went on around us. Someone haggling, a cart being pulled. I stood in
that noise turning that one line over and over.

Not knowing isn't a reason not to write it.

*I copied that sentence onto paper that evening. I put it in the
drawer with my mother's papers. That drawer now held two names and one
sentence.*

*I imagine that drawer sometimes. Twelve pages my mother left behind,
and the short notes I'd laid on top of them, one at a time. My mother
could never have imagined other people's names piling up on top of her
own papers. And yet, strangely, I didn't mind that drawer growing
heavier little by little. If anything, it felt like that drawer was
becoming something that no longer belonged only to me.*

*I didn't open that drawer often. I only opened it when there was
something new to put in. That day was one of those days.*

"…Doesn't it get old, being checked on like that every time?"

"…It does. But I can't just not go because I'm tired of it. If I
don't go, the application itself falls behind."

"…What happens if it falls behind?"

"…I find the person I'm looking for that much later. That's the part
I hate most."

"…Will you be here tomorrow too?"

"…Probably. Until the application's been processed."

"…When does that finish?"

"…I don't know. This place has a lot of confirmation steps."

"…So you'll be here a while longer, then."

"…Seems that way. No hurry, though. The person I'm looking for might
be somewhere around here too."

Hearing that, I felt something strange. Temel was taking a loss and
not even calling it a loss. He simply accepted it as something that
took time. I couldn't tell, that day, whether that was resignation or
ease.

I said goodbye and left. Walking away, I looked back once. Temel was
looking at the board again.

On my way home I stopped by the front desk and asked what the matters
were that kept calling Temel in. The clerk looked at the screen and
said,

"…Confirmation, confirmation, confirmation. All three of them."

"…Confirming what?"

"…Whether the application's contents match, whether he's really who
he says he is, that sort of thing."

"…They confirm the same thing every time?"

"…Seems that way. I don't know why, myself."

"…Isn't that strange? Why confirm something that's already been
confirmed?"

"…I don't know. I've thought it was strange myself, but I've never
found a way to stop it. It's a procedure written into the regulations."

"…Why is it written that way in the regulations?"

"…That's beyond me too. I didn't set it."

Hearing that answer, I thought of the other answers I'd heard around
this city for months. It was passed on. It was put in order. It was
convened. The clerk's answer had the same grain to it. No one had set
it, and only the confirming went on.

Leaving that spot, I felt I'd come to know one thing for certain.
Temel was someone who kept writing it, knowing full well it cost him,
and the reason was simple. There was a box. And yet that very
simplicity was what held me the longest.

*Hearing this conversation, I could dimly guess at what would happen
the next day. Whether that guess was right, the next chapter will
show.*

At home over dinner, I told Tyche briefly what had happened that day.
Tyche didn't eat with us that evening, but when we ran into each other
in front of the district hall the next morning, she brought it up
again.

"…Does he really keep getting called in like that?"

"…Yes. Three matters have his name on them."

"…That tells you why nobody writes it, doesn't it?"

"…I guess so."

"…So why does he keep writing it, then? It costs him."

"…I don't know. He said it was just habit."

Tyche tilted her head.

"…Habit basically means there's no real reason, doesn't it? So he's
just taking a loss over and over, for no real reason."

I couldn't answer that. What Tyche said didn't seem wrong. But
something felt missing. I couldn't explain, then, what it was.

*I think I can say a little now. Tyche asked for a reason, and Temel
said there wasn't one. But having no reason and having nothing at all
aren't the same thing. Temel had no reason, but he had a way. The way
of writing it every time. I wonder now whether that way was standing
in for the reason. I hadn't worked it out this far, back then.*
