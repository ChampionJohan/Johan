# Chapter 37. What It Is to Write It

> _Previously_ — Hora read no documents for three days. Reading was the
> only thing she knew how to do, so stopping it was her fast.

On the third night I opened the drawer.

The same drawer that had held my mother's papers. Other things live there
now. A notice, a copy of a summary, and the second page of the work
guidelines I'd first been given. I took that page out and unfolded it.

There was a box at the bottom. A long, horizontal box. The signature box.

*I had seen this box more times than I could count. Every day for over
two hundred days. It was on every document, and empty on every document.
I thought that was the rule. I still haven't fully learned, in this
city, how to tell a rule from a habit.*

Below the box, in small print, it read: *(This notice does not require a
signature.)*

Does not require. I read that sentence again. The same sentence I'd read
two hundred days before read differently to me now.

*Does not require doesn't mean do not. I already knew this. I'd learned
it back in Part Three. But knowing it, and holding it in your hand and
looking at it quietly, are different things.*

---

For three days I read no documents.

At first I thought I was simply resting. From the second day it started
to feel strange. My hand kept reaching for the paper on the table. The
third time my hand reached for it, I realized what I'd been doing. I
wasn't holding myself back from reading — the habit of reading had
worked its way into my body and was moving on its own.

*I already called this a fast at the end of Part Three. I didn't know
exactly why, then. Writing it again now, I can say it a little more
precisely. Reading was how I proved myself to myself. When I stopped
reading, I no longer knew what I was proven by.*

On the afternoon of the third day, Tyche stopped by the house. She came
even though it wasn't work hours.

"…Are you all right?"

"…Yeah. Just not reading."

"…Why?"

I couldn't answer. Tyche didn't press further either. She sat for a
while and then left. At the door she turned back once, as if to say
something, and didn't. I only understood what that had been much later.

---

I thought of the first day I'd asked the Secretary. When I'd asked what
happens if you write your name, he'd looked away.

After that I asked several other people the same thing. I looked for a
rulebook. I looked for a prohibition. There wasn't one. There really
wasn't one. All there was, was the box.

*Nobody stopped me. I've written this sentence many times already, and I
will write it many more. It is the most repeated sentence I say in this
book. Nobody stopped me.*

*If someone had stopped me, it would have been easier, in a way. If
there's someone stopping you, you can be angry at that person. But there
was no one. The box was simply empty. Built, from the start, so that
anyone could fill it.*

My aunt opened my door without knocking. She's a person who doesn't
knock. She's been that way since I was small.

"…Still not asleep?"

"…No."

My aunt came in and sat beside me. She looked at the paper and said
nothing. After a long while she asked.

"…What's that?"

"…Work guidelines. The first ones I got."

"…Why are you looking at that again?"

I couldn't answer. My aunt didn't ask again either.

---

*My aunt didn't know, that night, what I was about to do. Neither did
I. Let me be honest as I write this chapter: I hadn't decided anything
yet. The decision comes much later. This chapter isn't the decision —
it's the place you have to pass through before it.*

*The place where you just look at the box.*

I rubbed the box with my fingertip. The paper was just paper. No
special texture. With no ink on it, it was smooth. The other boxes —
the date box, the agenda number box — had letters printed on them, so
their surface was slightly rough. Only the signature box, having
nothing on it, was smooth.

*I felt, for the first time that night, that the smoothness was
frightening. I hadn't known before then that nothing being there could
be frightening. If I'd believed emptiness was a wrong thing, I'd have
been afraid of it long before. As long as I believed the emptiness was
a rule, it only looked comfortable to me.*

---

Beside the box, there was no instruction to write a name. No title, no
affiliation. Just a box. It didn't even say whether it was a place for
a name, or a place for something else.

*I learned later. That box hadn't been designed, from the start, to
hold anything in particular. It was simply a box left standing by
custom. Long ago it had appeared in some form, and no one had ever
erased it, so it had kept being carried forward. There had simply been
no one to propose erasing it.*

*Not erasing something and keeping it on purpose are different things.
I confused the two for a long time. The people of this city confuse
them too. Not erasing something just means no one touched it. Keeping
it means someone did so with intent.*

That night I suddenly wanted to look up old forms. Not the one I'd
taken from the drawer — the older forms in the archive room. But the
archive room was closed at night, and I didn't bother going out to get
a key.

*This part might look trivial, but I want to set it down. I didn't
rush out that night to check. There was no urgency. I hope the reader
of this book won't picture me as someone being chased by something. I
wasn't being chased. I was simply looking, for a long time.*

*Instead I remembered what I'd seen in the archive room before. The day
I'd opened twenty pages from the front. Back then I was struck that the
signature box was empty; I hadn't paid attention to the shape of the
box itself. Recalling it again now, those boxes had all been in the
same place, the same size. I also remembered that on the older
documents the box was slightly wider. Which meant that even as the
city changed its forms several times, this box alone had never been
removed — only carried forward.*

*Not removed. I come back to that phrase again. It wasn't that no one
said to remove it — it could have been erased at any time, if anyone
had wanted to. The Consensus does actually remove things it judges
unnecessary. There are quite a few old procedures that have vanished,
which I only learned once I reached Part Four. But this box stayed.*

*Whether that's a meaningful staying, or just a staying because nobody
cared — I won't give the answer here. This whole book is my answer to
that question, and I'm saving the answer for the last chapter.*

---

*This box is on the side of the not-erased. And yet every time I looked
at it, it felt kept. Whether that's my own delusion, or not a delusion
at all, I still can't be sure even after finishing this book.*

---

I thought again of the person who'd written a name eleven years before.
That one line I'd found once. A name, a date, and the record cut off
after that.

*I never found out, in the end, why it cut off — whether the person had
died, or left, or simply stopped writing. I keep writing that the
Equity hides nothing, but on this one point I have to be honest. I
couldn't even tell whether I hadn't found it because it wasn't there,
or because it was there and I still couldn't find it. Full openness
doesn't always mean full discovery.*

*Temel is different. Temel is still alive, and is still being called
in. I've already written about that. If there's one thing the person
from eleven years ago and Temel have in common, it's this: once each
of them filled that box, their own name stopped being only theirs.
Wherever that name appeared on a document, it followed.*

I also thought again of the moment I first heard the word review. The
Secretary had explained it wasn't a punishment. Not a punishment, just
being called out again and again. When I heard that explanation, I'd
thought, in fact, that it was more frightening for exactly that reason
— though at the time I couldn't say why in words.

*I can say it now. Punishment has an end. There's a term, there's a
fine, and once you've served or paid it, it's over. Review has no end.
As long as even one case bearing that name remains, being called
remains too. In this city, deliberations keep coming into being. So a
person who has once written their name, in theory, never finishes.*

*Temel knew all this, and wrote it anyway. That was what frightened me
most, that night. Not that Temel hadn't known. That he'd known, and
written it anyway.*

I was afraid of that. I admitted, for the first time that night, that
I was afraid.

*But being afraid, and therefore not doing it, were different matters.
I hadn't yet bound the two together into one, that night. Later Tyche
would ask, and later I would walk without an answer. That night I
hadn't gotten anywhere near that. Admitting I was afraid was the whole
of what that night held.*

---

My aunt got up and turned the light down a little. Then she sat back
down.

"…Hora."

"…Yeah."

"…Are you going to write it?"

I folded the paper. I answered as I folded it.

"…I don't know yet."

My aunt looked at my face and asked one more time.

"…Are you scared?"

"…Yeah."

"…I'm scared too."

I looked at my aunt. She didn't ask back what she was scared of. Neither
did I explain further. We just traded one sentence each and stopped.

*My aunt never tried to stop me after that night. Nor did she push me
on. I think now that was the largest thing she could have given me. Not
making the decision for me.*

My aunt seemed to believe that answer. And in fact, that night, I truly
didn't know. It would make this book tidier to write that I did know,
but it isn't true.

*I promised at the start to write honestly. So I'll write honestly here
too. That night, I hadn't decided anything at all. I only looked at the
box, touched it with my fingertip, folded it, and put it back in the
drawer.*

*But after that night, I saw that box differently. Before, I had seen
something empty. Now I saw something that could be filled. The same
box — and yet the two are entirely different things.*

---

Before closing the drawer, I looked once more at the first page of the
notice. *You have been assigned.* I read that sentence again.

*You have been assigned. Still no one who had assigned it. Every
sentence in this city takes this shape. What happened is there. Who
made it happen is not.*

*That night, for the first time, I gave this grammar a name. Subjectless
grammar. The grammar the Secretary spoke in. This whole city was built
out of that grammar.*

*And the signature box was, within that grammar, the one place where a
subject could be put in. A place no one had stopped anyone from
filling. A place no one had ever filled.*

---

I didn't close the drawer. I sat beside it for a long while.

My aunt went back to her room. I heard the door close. After that the
house grew quiet. The nights of this city are quiet by nature. There's
seldom any reason to make a sound.

*I wrote before that I loved this city for its quietness. That's still
true now. Even that night, I didn't hate this city. I want to set this
down clearly. I wasn't looking at that box in anger, then. I looked at
it while still loving this city.*

*That loving something and leaving it as it is aren't the same thing —
that night, I began, faintly, to understand this. It would be a lie to
say I understood it exactly. It was faint.*

I opened the second page of the work guidelines again. I looked at the
signature box again.

*What I would write in that box hadn't been decided yet. More exactly,
even whether I would write anything hadn't been decided. I only looked
at it for a long time, the way a person memorizes a shape. So many
boxes across, so many down, the thickness of the border. I still can't
explain why I was trying to memorize such things.*

One thing had become clear. That box had been there from the start. It
had been there my first day at work, in the very first deliberation
document, at the bottom of every document I'd read over more than two
hundred days. No one had ever removed that box. No one had ever hidden
it either.

*If the Consensus had ever hidden anything, it would have hidden this
box. But the Consensus didn't hide it. Instead, every single time, at
the bottom of every document, it printed it honestly. Even empty, the
very fact that this box was there had never once been concealed.*

*I realized this that night. This city had hidden nothing from me. That
the box existed, that it went unfilled, that filling it wasn't
forbidden — all of it had been shown to me from the beginning. I was
the one who hadn't looked. For two hundred days I saw that box every
day, and never once asked what it meant.*

*Seeing the box and asking about the box are different things. That
night, for the first time, I asked.*

Near dawn, I glanced out the window for a moment. I could still hear
sound from the direction of the water channel. Even at this hour, the
water was flowing. Water that had never once come before an agenda,
still flowing on, unconcerned with agendas.

*Listening to that sound, I thought. There are things in this city that
go on without ever coming before an agenda. The water channel is one.
And maybe this box was one too. Whether anyone brought it up as an
agenda item or not, the box would go on being there. Whether I filled
it or not.*

*That thought put me a little at ease. Whatever I did, that box wasn't
going to disappear. There was no need to hurry. That was the one
conclusion I reached that night. That I didn't need to hurry.*

I folded the paper again and put it back in the drawer.
I hadn't decided. But the question remained. I slept holding that
question.
