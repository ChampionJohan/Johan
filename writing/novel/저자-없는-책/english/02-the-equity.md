# Chapter 2. The Equity

> _Previously_ — Hora, seated at the Fourth Deliberation District's assistant
> member desk by a vacancy and a number. Her first session ended in twenty
> minutes, and she stayed behind alone to read all forty pages to the end.
> The summary was accurate, and the signature box on the last page was
> empty. That day, she thought nothing of it.

People hearing this city's name for the first time usually laugh. The Equity. I think it's because the name is so bald about itself. Haven lets you rest, the Reckoning squares the accounts, the Harvest gathers you in, the Preserve keeps you safe — so the Equity, people assume, must mean it treats you fairly.

I've watched that reaction many times. And I've heard, many times, what those same people say about half a year later. After about half a year, everyone says the same thing.

"…It really does live up to the name."

*I'm not writing that sentence to be ironic. It's simply true. This city lives up to its name. I'm going to write a great many hard things about this city later in this book, and before I write them, I wanted this chapter written first. I didn't want the things I say later to turn this chapter into a lie.*

---

I'll start with the ration office.

Every winter, heating fuel runs short. What's short has to be divided, and of course someone ends up with less. In other cities, I've heard, this is when people line up. First come, first served, and whoever comes late gets nothing.

Here, no one lines up. You only apply. It doesn't matter when. You can apply in the morning or in the evening. The Consensus gathers the year's need, the year's supply, and the number of households, calculates the allotment, and posts the result on the door. Who applied at what hour never enters the calculation.

When I was twelve, I once thought this was strange. I asked my aunt, "…Shouldn't the person who applies first get more?"

My aunt looked at me for a while and said,

"…Applying first doesn't make you any less cold."

She was right. I still can't explain, even now, why that sentence felt so clean.

*Someone who's never stood in a line doesn't know how much a line can divide people. I first learned it listening to kids who'd come from outside talk about lines — that a whole day gets decided by who stands in front and who stands behind. Here, there is no such day.*

---

The word for pulling strings is almost never used here either.

As far as I know, no one in this city goes to find someone and ask, separately, to be looked after well. There's no one to go find. Every counter runs the same procedure, and the procedure runs the same way no matter who's staffing it. There's no part left to a clerk's discretion.

This isn't because the officials are unbending. I've watched a good many people working the counters, and they were all kind. But kindness never changed the outcome. What kindness couldn't get done stayed undone, and what a curt manner got done, got done.

*I only learned later how rare this is. In other cities, I've heard, the outcome changes depending on who you smile at. Here, smiling changes nothing. I've never once thought this made the place bleak. If anything it was the opposite.*

My aunt once said she'd worked briefly in another city. There, she said, remembering a superior's birthday was part of the job. Whether you remembered or not is what split people apart at promotion time. She laughed telling me that, but there was a slight bitterness at the end of the laugh. "…Coming here, I found out you didn't have to remember things like that. At first, not having to felt strange. Like I was forgetting something." Hearing that, I wanted to tell her she wasn't forgetting anything — there had never been anything there to remember — but I could tell she already knew that, so I said nothing.

I've never seen the loud win here either. In meeting rooms, at counters, at school, I've never once watched anyone raise their voice and get something out of it. Raising your voice just makes you a somewhat strange person. That's the whole of it.

There's something I saw when I was thirteen. Two houses on our lane got into a dispute over a fence line. One side was loud and one side was quiet. In another city, the loud side would have won — a neighbor who'd come from outside told me that afterward. Here, the surveying office came out, checked the old land register against the current fence, and a number gave the answer. The loud side's adult fumed for a good while after getting the result, but fuming didn't move the fence. Three days later that house had gone quiet too. It had no choice but to go quiet. There was no longer anyone left to fight — you can't fight a number the way you can fight the surveying office.

*I still remember those three days. Watching that fuming adult go quiet after three days, I thought this wasn't losing — it was simply ending. Losing means losing to someone, and there had been no someone there.*

I crossed paths with that adult a few more times afterward, and they never seemed especially uncomfortable seeing me. There was no trace of someone who'd lost a fence dispute. I found that strange. In another city, I imagine, seeing that fence every time would have stung. Here, looking at the fence, there was no one left to be stung by. You couldn't hate the surveying office. The surveying office had only measured.

---

Housing reassignment worked the same way. When I was fifteen, the place my aunt and I lived became too small, and I applied for reassignment. The application only asked for household size and current square footage. It didn't ask what my aunt used to do for work, or how long we'd lived in this city. Two months later a new place was assigned. It wasn't bigger, but it had one more room. Why that particular place wasn't written down. The Consensus must have matched that year's list of empty homes against the list of applicants, the counter clerk explained. That explanation was enough for me. I had no reason to press further.

---

I should say something about the old man next door. He was among the longest-lived people in this city. His legs were bad, and every year he applied for care assignment, and the application asked nothing but that his legs were bad. How many children he had, what work he used to do — none of that was asked.

I thought that was strange. It seemed to me that knowing such things would let people understand his particular hardship better. I asked the old man about it, and he answered,

"…Where's the sense in one hardship being harder than another? Bad legs are bad legs, same as anyone's."

That might sound curt, but I thought there was relief inside it. The old man didn't have to sell his story. He didn't have to plead to anyone. The single fact that his legs were bad was enough.

*When I heard that in some places you have to sell your story to get help, I couldn't believe it at first. What happens to someone with no story? Someone who isn't good at telling one? Here, the question itself was never needed.*

That old man received his care assignment that winter, and when spring came, he filed the same application again. The same form, every time. I once asked whether he didn't find it tedious. He answered, "…Tedious is better than having to explain yourself fresh every time." Hearing that, I thought for the first time about how fortunate it actually was, for a form to be tedious. Explaining yourself fresh every time meant having to persuade someone, every time.

Work assignments were the same. There's no such thing as an interview when this city assigns you a job. You submit an aptitude test and a preference, and the Consensus places you. At first I found this disappointing too — it meant there was no chance to show how good I was at something. But looked at the other way, it also meant no one was rejected for a first impression.

I filed my first preference form at fifteen. I wrote that I wanted to work in the records room. I didn't have to give a reason, but I gave one anyway. "I'm good at reading things all the way to the end." Looking at it now it's a slightly embarrassing sentence, but at the time it was everything I had. When the result came, I was told I'd been placed on the preliminary list for the deliberation district instead of the records room. Why it had changed wasn't written down. The Consensus must have matched the aptitude results against that year's projected vacancies, the officer explained. I was disappointed, but not wronged. There's a real difference between disappointed and wronged, and that was the first time I learned it.

---

People hear talk like this and always ask, "So nobody has any complaints?"

There are complaints. I have them too. There was a winter I felt the allotment was too small, and a time my aunt's application was processed late. But the problem was always who those complaints were supposed to be aimed at. There was no one to aim them at.

The allotment had been small because that year there truly hadn't been enough. My aunt's application had been late because that year applications had piled up. I was angry, but I couldn't find anyone worth being angry at, so in the end I just let it go.

*Reading this sentence back now, I'm still not sure myself whether this is a good thing or a bad thing to say. Whether not being able to get angry is the same as not needing to — I still don't know. But at the time, it truly was a relief. It wasn't that I'd been wronged. It was just that the year had been the way it was.*

---

There's something I learned at school that I still remember.

The teacher wrote this on the board: "The Equity takes no one's side." And underneath, one more line: "And it sees no one as a person."

At the time I thought that second sentence was frightening. The teacher noticed and explained.

"…Not seeing you as a person doesn't mean treating you carelessly. The Consensus doesn't see you as Hora. It sees an application number. But that isn't a bad thing. If it saw you as Hora, then someone who liked you could look after you more, and someone who didn't could look after you less. Seen as a number, that can't happen."

At the time I thought I understood what she meant. Thinking about it now, I only half understood.

At that same moment, the kid in the next seat raised a hand and asked, "Then what happens if the number is wrong?" The teacher paused a moment, then answered, "…The number has never once been wrong." I didn't realize, at the time, that this wasn't an answer to the question. Whether the number has never been wrong, and what happens when it is, are two different questions.

*Whether seeing a number and not seeing a person are truly the same thing is a question this book comes back to later. That day in the classroom, I didn't even have the question yet.*

---

I should write down the clinic too. This city has no such thing as an appointment. When you go in sick, it's not the order you arrived in that decides when you're seen — the Consensus judges how severe your symptoms are and sets the order from that. I went through this myself when my aunt was badly hurt. There were six people in the waiting room, and my aunt, who'd arrived after us, was called in third. Not one of the people ahead of her complained. I asked afterward, and I was told the screen had shown, along with the queue order, the reason it had been set that way. My aunt's bleeding needed to be stopped urgently; the people ahead of her weren't urgent. Seeing the reason, the anger didn't come, a man in the waiting room told me afterward.

"…When they show you the reason, what's left to argue?"

*I remembered those words for a long time. That showing the reason settles the anger. This is always how this city calms people down. Not persuasion — inspection. Instead of someone stepping forward to say, "I'm sorry, it came out that way because of my judgment," the grounds appeared on the screen. I've never once felt that was cold. There are times when grounds comfort a person more than an apology does — I learned that for the first time in that waiting room.*

---

There's one reason I'm writing this chapter.

A day is coming when I write about this city while I'm angry at it. I wanted to set this down so that day's version of me won't betray the me writing now. I like this city right now. I genuinely like it.

No one envies me here, and I envy no one. No one lines up here, no one asks for a favor, no one raises a voice. I think this is a good way to live. I still think so.

*Writing this sentence, I don't yet know how hard it will come to make things for me later. It would have been so much easier if I could have said this wasn't a good place. It was a good place. That was the problem.*

I've tried, a few times, to explain to people from outside why this city is good. It never went well. If I said, "No one here gets wronged," they'd always ask back, "So no one here does especially well, either?" That was true. No one did especially well. I never once thought of that as a loss — but every time I got that question, I did doubt, briefly, whether I might be missing something. Looking back now, I think that doubt was the tiniest seed of this book.

On my second day of work, on my way to the meeting room, I passed the ration office. A notice was posted. This winter's heating fuel allotment results. No names, only numbers. I found my number and checked the amount. It was a little higher than last year's.

Why it had gone up wasn't written down. I wasn't curious about it at the time. There was that same box at the very bottom of the notice too. A box marked "approval." It was empty. I didn't think anything of it that day either. It was only natural for a notice to have an approval box, and only natural for it to be empty. I didn't yet know there was a gap between those two kinds of natural.

I arrived at the meeting room and took my seat. The same seat as yesterday. The person next to me only nodded instead of greeting me. That was fine too. I was already growing a little more comfortable with the fact that not greeting someone here wasn't rude. Today's stack of material was thinner than yesterday's. I started with the summary, and I meant to read the full text behind it to the end as well. For now that was simply a habit of mine. It meant nothing more.
