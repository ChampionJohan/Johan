# Chapter 35. The One Who Walks Out

> _Previously_ — The days remaining fell to five. Pheugon stood before
> the calculation: stay still and the city dies; step forward and he
> becomes the only person who can save it. He wrote his reasons down
> on paper and still couldn't reach an answer by the end of the
> night.

Five became four.

I did nothing special that day. I just walked as usual. Past the
bakery, through the market, by the well. Trope was sitting by her
jar again that day. We didn't say much. But she was quieter than
usual.

"…Trope," I asked. "Can someone with low weight still get driven
out?"

She looked a little startled by the question.

"…Why ask that?"

"No reason," I deflected. "Just curious."

"…Low weight, and you don't get driven out," she said. "But—"

"But what?"

"…Never mind." She said, adjusting her grip on the jar, changing the
subject. "Finish your own arithmetic, Pheugon."

*I didn't ask, at the time, what came after that "but." I took her
changing the subject as simple kindness. I know now. What came after
that "but" was something she'd been about to tell me that day, and
then held back.*

As we parted, she said the line again.

"…I'll be here tomorrow too."

I wasn't even counting how many times I'd heard it by then. It had
become too ordinary a thing to count.

Four became three.

I went twice to the Adjudicator's door that day, and turned back
twice. It wasn't that I had nothing to say. It was that I had too
much to say, and didn't know where to start.

That evening at the well, Trope packed up earlier than usual.

"…You're leaving early today," I said.

"…Something I need to do," she said.

"What?"

"…Nothing," she smiled. The same smile as always. I noticed nothing
in it.

She said the line again as we parted.

"…I'll be here tomorrow too."

*I let that pass that day too. Looking back, she was probably saying
it with a slightly different weight each time. I couldn't hear that
difference in weight. I was good at hearing sounds. What lay behind
the sound, I still couldn't hear well.*

Three became two.

That evening, Trope wasn't at the well. I just thought she must have
something on. I told myself I'd ask her the next day and let it go.

*Looking back, that day was the fork in the road. I didn't go looking
for her that day. I was too deep in my own arithmetic. I knew she was
carrying something as heavy as I was, and still I hadn't checked that
weight every day.*

*I know now. Three days before, when she said "I'll be here tomorrow
too" for the last time — that had been the last day I ever heard her
say it. I didn't notice, at the time, that it had stopped. Something
that has been there vanishing is far quieter than something new
appearing.*

Two became one.

---

The morning the square's board changed to **1**, I felt a strange
stillness from the first light of dawn.

The whole city seemed to be holding its breath. People still walked
as usual, the bakery still opened its doors. But the footsteps were
a little slower. The voices were a little fewer.

I went to the well. Trope wasn't there.

At first I didn't think much of it. She was sometimes late. I sat on
the stone wall and waited. Half an hour. An hour. She didn't come.

People fetching water passed by, one after another. I asked them.

"…Have you seen Trope?"

"Not today, no," one woman said. "I don't think I saw her yesterday
either."

"…Yesterday either?"

"I think so. Haven't seen much of her lately."

Hearing that, something went cold in my chest. Only then did I
count, for the first time, that I hadn't actually seen her in two
days. I couldn't understand myself, why that count had come so late.

*I started counting then. Half an hour, an hour, an hour and a half.
Counting let me forget the anxiety in small doses. But past two
hours, even counting stopped working.*

I went to her room.

---

I knocked. No answer.

I pushed the door. It wasn't locked.

The room inside was empty. The blanket was folded — more neatly than
usual. No water jar. The small bundle that had hung on the wall was
gone too.

I looked around the room. A small room. Only then did it strike me
that I'd never been inside it before. Two months I'd known her, and I
hadn't even known where she slept.

A nail was driven into the wall. Something had once hung there.
Nothing now. Beside it, a small seashell sat on the windowsill. A
worthless thing, the kind you'd pick up scooping water. And yet she'd
left it behind.

*I wondered, in that moment, why she'd left that shell behind. I
thought I might get to ask her later. I didn't know, then, whether
that chance would ever really come.*

A sheet of paper lay on the desk.

I picked it up. My hand was shaking.

> Pheugon.
>
> I'm leaving the city today. Don't worry. I'm not running away.
>
> I've kept thinking about it. Why this city still doesn't look like
> it's turned. Why the Scales' number won't move. You said people
> have changed. I saw it too. But the number stays the same.
>
> I know my weight is low. But low and not-heavy are different
> things. I was born in this city. My mother's overflowed weight,
> this city's overflowed weight — I grew up inside it. As long as I'm
> here, this city gets counted with me included in its weight.
>
> If I leave, the city gets a little lighter. Maybe that's the last
> bit it needs.
>
> I don't resent the Scales. The Scales was right. I'm just doing
> what I can do.
>
> Trope.

I read the letter twice, three times.

*I felt anger in that moment. But it wasn't anger at the Reckoning.
For the first time, that anger was aimed at myself.*

*For three years I had only learned how to hate someone else. I
learned, that day, for the first time, how to be angry at myself. I
hadn't wanted to learn it.*

I looked again at one line of the letter. *If I leave, the city gets
a little lighter.* I knew that calculation was wrong. The Adjudicator
had said it plainly. Weight is recorded per person; once accumulated,
it doesn't decrease. Subtracting her share from the city's
accumulated weight wouldn't change the total at all. She believed
that if she left, the city would grow lighter. That belief itself was
a calculation the Scales had never once taught anyone.

*I had a strange thought, in that moment. She knew the Scales as well
as I did. And still she'd made that calculation. Knowing and
believing are different things, she'd told me herself, once. At the
time I'd only understood it with my head. Now I was feeling the
weight of that sentence through a letter in my hand.*

I folded the letter and pressed it against my chest. My hand was
still shaking.

I thought of her mother. All I had was what Trope had told me a few
times. The day her mother crossed the weight limit and was driven out
of the city. Trope had followed her all the way to the gate, she'd
said. Her mother never looked back.

*I'd wondered, hearing that story, why her mother never looked back.
I thought I understood now. If you look back, your steps stop. If you
stop, you can't walk again. Trope was probably walking, right now, the
same road her mother had walked. Trying not to look back.*

---

I walked out of the room holding the letter.

*I understood now, why she'd been quiet these past days. She'd been
doing arithmetic. Same as me. Except I'd been calculating how to save
this city, and she'd been calculating how to erase herself. We'd
spent the same days on two entirely different sums.*

*I'd spent three years calculating anger. She'd spent five years
calculating her own weight. I'd only managed to break free of my
calculation once someone else caught me at it. Hers was still
uncaught by anyone.*

I knocked on a neighbor's door. An old woman answered.

"…Have you seen Trope?"

"Early this morning," the woman said. "Saw her headed toward the west
gate with a bundle. Why, what's happened?"

I couldn't answer.

"…Ask the gatekeeper," the woman said. "He logs everyone who passes
through."

I ran.

Halfway there, I stopped in front of the Adjudicator's office. I
thought about knocking, then didn't. Instead I shouted through the
door crack.

"…Adjudicator! I'm going after Trope! Tomorrow—" I couldn't finish
the sentence. I didn't know myself, yet, what I'd do tomorrow.

Footsteps sounded inside. The Adjudicator opened the door and came
out.

"…What's happened."

"Trope went outside the city," I said. "She left a note."

The Adjudicator's face went taut for a moment. He said nothing, just
looked at me.

"…Tomorrow," he asked. "Will you be back in time?"

I couldn't answer that.

"…I don't know," I finally said. "But I have to go anyway."

The Adjudicator looked at me a long while. Then gave a short nod.

"…Go," he said. "I'll hold things here, somehow."

I felt gratitude and fear in equal measure at that. He hadn't told me
what exactly he meant to hold. I didn't ask.

I ran again.

Passing the herald office lodgings, I stopped for a moment. There was
a light in the window. He would still be in there, preparing
something. Precisely. Steady as ever.

*I hesitated a moment, in that instant, over whether to tell him.
That I might not be back tomorrow. That if I didn't come back, he
would be the one who read. In the end I didn't knock on his door.
Saying it out loud would have meant admitting, to myself, that I
might not come back. I didn't want to give that possibility a voice.*

I walked on. Toward the gate.

---

The gatekeeper checked the log.

"…Someone left before dawn. Name was—" He traced the record with his
finger. "Trope."

"…Which way did she go?"

"West," he said. "There's nothing out that way. Why'd she leave?"

"…Was she alone?" I asked.

"Alone," he said. "Not fast, not slow. Just walked at a normal pace.
Didn't seem like someone in a hurry, either."

Hearing that only frightened me more. If she'd been walking fast, that
would have been better. A normal pace meant she'd already made up her
mind, completely.

I didn't answer his question. I looked out through the gate.

The plain stretched out into the distance. Not a single person in
sight. The sun was already high overhead.

*In that moment I realized for the first time how vast the world
outside this city was. For two months I had lived entirely inside
these walls. I'd always thought of the space outside them only as the
direction I'd come from, the direction I would someday leave toward.
Now, in that direction, one person was walking alone.*

I braced a hand against the gate post and caught my breath. I could
hear my own heartbeat.

*I understood something strange, in that moment. I had been swept
into this city. I had never entered it on my own feet. And now I was
about to walk out of it on my own feet. For the first time. And not
to save this city — to bring back one of its children. I thought that
order was strange. A person had come before the city.*

*One day left. I counted, on my fingers, how many hours of that day
I'd already let slip by. Five. Six. I stopped counting partway.*

*For the first time, counting was no use at all.*

The gatekeeper caught my arm.

"…You heading out too?" he asked. "Tomorrow's the fortieth day. You
know that, right?"

"…I know," I said. "That's why I have to go."

He looked at my face a moment, then asked nothing more. Instead he
brought out a lantern and handed it to me.

"…Light this once the sun sets," he said. "You can't see a thing on
this road at night."

I took the lantern. My hand was still shaking.

"…Thank you," I said.

"Thanks nothing," he said. "Hurry back. I don't know if you're the
one who's supposed to be standing here tomorrow or not, but better
to have someone than not, I'd think."

I didn't fully understand what he meant by that. But it pushed me
forward all the same, strangely.

*I realized, in that moment, that I hadn't said anything to either
the Adjudicator or the substitute herald about who would stand in the
square tomorrow. Nothing had been decided. I knew that, and I was
still walking out through the gate. A hundred and twenty thousand
lives mattered less to me, in that moment, than one person named
Trope. I knew that was a strange order of priority. I didn't want to
set it right.*

I stepped through the gate.

The sun was sinking to the west. The same direction she had walked.
