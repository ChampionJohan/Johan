# Chapter 26. Article Four

> _Previously_ — Pheugon went to the Adjudicator to ask for the
> original clause text. The Adjudicator looked at him a long time,
> said "This will take a while," and drew an old scroll from a locked
> drawer. "No one's ever shown you this."

The Adjudicator unrolled the scroll across his desk.

It wasn't paper. The material was older than that — its edges gone
brown and brittle, its color faded along every fold. I'd never seen
anything like it. Every other document in the Reckoning came printed
by the Scales. This one had been written by hand.

He moved his fingertips carefully as he spread it flat. Not the hands
of a man handling something old — the hands of a man handling
something long put off.

"Older than the Scales," the Adjudicator said, as if reading my
thought. "All of Article 4 is. This clause existed before the Scales
took its current form."

"…Who wrote it?"

"Don't know," he said, short. "That's the first strange thing about
this clause."

"No one knows? Not even you?"

"Not even me." He looked at me. "No one who has ever sat in this seat
knew. Not the Adjudicator before me, not the one before him. I went
back through the records — this clause was here before the Scales
office even existed. Who wrote it, when, why — there's no record at
all."

"Have you tried to find out?"

"Once," he said, short again. "When I was young. Never found it."

He didn't explain further. I didn't ask further. I understood that
his silence was the whole of his answer to that question.

---

The scroll held several clauses. Article 4 governed city-wide cases —
the ones where accumulated weight crossed the limit and a demolition
sentence fell on an entire city, not a single person. Clauses one
through ten were familiar territory: conditions for sentencing,
grace periods, execution procedure, order of resettlement for the
displaced. Exactly as I'd been trained. Even the handwriting was
neat, its sentences not so different from what the Scales printed —
precise, dry, leaving no exception.

Reading through those clauses, I remembered training. The sentence
structures were identical to what my supervisor had made us memorize
— subject, condition, disposal. Sentences with no room for feeling to
get in. I'd read sentences like that for three years, and it was for
reading them well that I'd been sent this far.

Clause 11 stood apart.

The margin above it was unusually wide, as if whoever wrote the body
had stopped there, and someone else had filled in the blank space
below, much later.

The handwriting differed from the other clauses. Finer, more
careful. As if, after finishing the body, someone had sat down
separately, at another time, and added this one line on its own.

I read the line without making a sound.

> If, within forty days, the said city is confirmed to have turned,
> this sentence shall be rendered void. The confirmation shall be
> performed by the one who read this sentence aloud.

I read it twice.

What I'd known ended with the first sentence. What was attached to
the sentence document went exactly that far. The line after it —
"The confirmation shall be performed by the one who read this
sentence aloud" — that, I was seeing for the first time.

*Even reading it then, I hadn't yet let the meaning arrive. My eyes
read it; my mind hadn't caught up. There are moments like that. Later
you remember the moment exactly. In the moment itself, you feel
nothing.*

"This is the original," the Adjudicator said. "Only the first
sentence goes out on the sentence document. Legal requirement.
Procedure and effect are all people need to be told — no need to
publish how the confirmation works."

"Why not publish it?"

The Adjudicator thought a moment.

"I don't know that either. That's another of the clause's strange
points. It could be a document made to hide something. Or—" He cut
himself off.

"Or?"

"Or it was hidden because it shouldn't be known by just anyone."

---

I sat in the Adjudicator's office and listened to what he had to
tell me.

He'd held this seat thirty-one years. In that time there had been
three city-wide cases. The same three the archivist had mentioned.

"I was the Adjudicator all three times," he said. "All three times
this clause was attached, and all three times I took out this
document. Just as I'm doing for you now."

He laid his hand on the scroll. It looked as if the weight he'd
carried for so long had settled into his palm.

"All three times, I hoped," he said. "It's shameful, but it's true.
That this time might be different. That the sentence might actually
move. At thirty, I truly believed that."

"And now?"

He didn't answer.

"…Who to?"

"To the herald."

I looked at him.

"It happened the same way every time," the Adjudicator said. "When
the herald arrived, I showed him this document. Told him the
confirmation was his to perform. And—"

He stopped.

"And?"

"Every time, nothing happened."

---

"All three cities fell." The Adjudicator went on. "The first was when
I was thirty. That herald read the sentence and left. He must have
known the confirmation clause existed. He never came back."

"Why not?"

"Who knows. Maybe he thought his job was done. Maybe he didn't
understand what there was to confirm." The Adjudicator's fingertip
traced the scroll's edge. "Whether that city actually turned or not —
no one knows. There was no one to confirm it."

I turned the words over. It sounded strange to me, that no one knew
whether it had turned or not. I'd been taught the Scales knew
everything. Yet faced with the one question that mattered most, even
the Scales didn't know, and no person knew, and a whole city had
vanished with no one knowing at all.

"So that city," I asked. "Might really have turned?"

"Who knows," the Adjudicator said again. "That's the most frightening
part of this clause. Without someone to confirm it, having turned is
indistinguishable from not having turned. The Scales can't tell the
two apart."

"What about the second one?"

"The second herald wasn't from that city. Came from somewhere else.
Read it precisely, left precisely. He never came back either."

"Did you show him this document too?"

"I showed him," the Adjudicator said. "He heard the whole thing, then
asked me: 'So you're saying I have to come back later and say whether
this city has changed or not?' I told him yes. He laughed. 'How would
I know that.' He was right. He'd left without meeting even ten people
in this city."

I felt something tighten in my chest, listening.

*I thought, at the time, that I understood that feeling. Looking back,
I'd understood only half of it. In that second herald, I wasn't
seeing myself — I was seeing the man who would arrive in seven days.
He too would read that sentence without meeting even ten people in
this city.*

*Listening, I felt something go cold in some corner of my chest. I
didn't know what it was then. I know now. It was a premonition.*

"The third one," the Adjudicator said, looking at me. "The third was
from this city. Born and raised here. Someone who knew better than
anyone why this city had come to this."

The Adjudicator's voice changed, just slightly. Slower than before.

"That one left the city as a child. To train. And years later, was
assigned back to this very city as a herald. The city he was born in.
The city he knew better than anyone."

"…He must have known why it was falling apart, too."

"He knew. In detail," the Adjudicator said. "I don't know about his
family. But I knew he didn't remember this city fondly. His hands
shook, handing over the papers. Not the shaking of gladness."

I couldn't say anything.

"What about him?"

"He didn't read it."

I looked at the Adjudicator.

"He fled the night before the proclamation."

I repeated the sentence to myself several times. *He fled the night
before.* I knew that sentence wasn't about me. And still, I kept
finding my own name slotted into it.

"So that city."

"Fell. On the fortieth day, no one read. The Scales simply executed.
No confirmation, nothing. If there's no one to read, there's nothing
to confirm."

---

The room went quiet.

I looked at my hands. They weren't shaking. They should have been.

*I thought, in that moment, that the Adjudicator was testing me. I
know now he wasn't. He was just telling me facts. That it felt like a
test was my own problem.*

"…Why did he flee?" I asked. My voice sounded strange.

"Don't know. I never got to ask. I never saw him again after that."

The Adjudicator started rolling the scroll back up. I watched his
hands.

"Adjudicator." I called out.

"Yes."

"What is confirmation. That sentence only says the one who read
performs the confirmation — it doesn't say what confirmation actually
means."

The Adjudicator's hands stopped, mid-roll.

He looked at me for a very long time. I still find that look hard to
describe. It wasn't the look of a man withholding an answer. It was
the look of a man gauging whether I was ready to hear one.

"That," he said slowly, "we'll talk about tomorrow."

"…Why not now?"

"You've heard enough for one day."

I couldn't press further.

The Adjudicator put the scroll back in the drawer and locked it. The
sound rang louder than before.

"Pheugon."

"…Yes."

"I'm not going to ask why you're clinging to this clause so hard," he
said. "You don't look like you'd give me a real answer if I did."

I said nothing.

"Come back tomorrow," he said. "Same time."

I got up and walked toward the door. When my hand was on the handle,
his voice came again from behind me.

"Pheugon."

"…Yes."

"Seven days, you said. The substitute."

"…Yes."

"I hope tomorrow won't come too late for you."

I didn't fully understand the weight of that sentence then. Walking
out, I simply took it for concern. I know now. It wasn't concern. It
was a warning.

---

It was already evening by the time I got out to the hallway.

The square was visible through the window. People were drifting home
one and two at a time. A few children had gathered by the board that
announced the days remaining, pointing at the number, talking about
something. I couldn't hear what.

Walking down the stairs, I found myself turning the Adjudicator's
words over again. *The third one, someone who knew better than
anyone why the city had come to this.*

*I turned that sentence over the whole way down those stairs. I
didn't know, at the time, why I couldn't look away from it. I know
now. It was my own story. Not yet finished — but my story had already
gone ahead of me and arrived there first.*

---

Trope was still at the well. It was the hour when the last few people
fetching evening water were thinning out.

"You're late," she said. "Coming from the Adjudicator's office?"

I froze for a second.

"…How did you know?"

"That's exactly the path from there." Trope smiled. "Why, is it a
secret?"

"…No."

*Even then I stacked one more lie on top. I'd said no, but it was a
secret. I told her nothing that day. And the fact that I was grateful
she didn't press — looking back now, I see how convenient an excuse
that gratitude was.*

Trope slung the water jar over her shoulder and glanced at me.

"You've been thinking hard about something lately, Pheugon."

"…Does it show?"

"Yes." She said, walking. "I know that look. My mother used to wear
it a lot. The face people make when they're thinking about something
that doesn't show up on the Scales."

I had no answer for that.

She didn't press further and just walked on ahead. I watched her
back for a moment, then followed.

On the way home, I passed the herald office's wall. There was one lit
window on the other side of it. The Adjudicator's office. He seemed
to still be in that room.

*I watched that light and wondered what he was doing in there. Had he
taken the scroll out again? Or was he just sitting, looking out the
window? I didn't know then. I don't know exactly now either. I only
know that across the thirty-one years he'd spent in that room, he had
wanted, at least once, to see that sentence move. That wish had never
once come true, and still he had taken out that same scroll three
times in that same room, and put it away three times again.*

*I couldn't sleep that night, back at home. The third herald's story
kept circling in my head. Someone born and raised in that city. Someone
who knew better than anyone why it had come to this. That person
hadn't read it. He'd fled the night before the proclamation.*

*I envied him. In that moment I didn't even know why I envied him. To
put a name to that envy, I would first have had to admit what I
wanted. That night, I still wasn't ready for that.*
