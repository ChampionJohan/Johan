# Chapter 18. The Room of Records

> _Previously_ — Pheugon began reading notices aloud for the city, at people's request. Hiding his name, he was slowly woven into the city's fabric. He sat at the old man's table, and felt, for the first time, that lying was no longer easy. That night he heard, for the first time, of a place called the room of records.

The room of records was behind the square, down three flights of stairs.

The next morning I headed there without a word to Trope. I didn't want to hear her ask where I was going. If she asked, I'd have to make up one more lie.

The air changed as I went down the stairs. The sounds of the square above grew fainter, and a cool, dry smell took their place. The smell of paper. The kind of smell that comes from a great deal of very old paper gathered together.

The moment I caught that smell, I felt strangely at ease. Paper smell had nothing to do with the Scales — just the smell of old things.

At the bottom of the stairs was a door. Wood, its handle worn pale from long handling.

I pushed it open.

---

The room was bigger than I'd expected. Shelves reached the ceiling on every wall, packed with scrolls and boxes. Several long tables stood in the middle, papers stacked on those too.

An older woman sat at one end of a table. She wore glasses, and there were ink stains on her fingertips.

"New face," the woman said, looking at me over her glasses. Her eyes looked unusually large behind the lenses. "What are you looking for?"

I didn't know how to answer for a moment. *I want to know why the Scales judges this way* would have been too blunt.

"…I'm curious how the Scales decides sentences," I said. Not a complete lie.

The woman looked at me a moment longer. Then shrugged, as though it weren't a particularly surprising question.

"People like that come around now and then," the woman said, standing. "The precedent section. This way."

*I felt relief, in that moment. I wasn't the only person in this city curious about the Scales. That fact made me, strangely, a little less lonely. Looking back now, that was a misunderstanding. The others were only curious. I wanted to bring it down.*

---

The record-keeper led me to one shelf. Better organized than the rest.

"Precedent," the record-keeper said, pulling out a scroll. "The Scales doesn't just look at the current weight when it sets a sentence. It adjusts based on similar past rulings."

"…Based on them, how?"

"For instance." The record-keeper unrolled the scroll. "Say someone steals something. Whether that person later admits their wrong in their own words and makes amends, or denies it to the end — that doesn't register in this weight. Only the value of the stolen goods registers. But the Scales finds similar past cases, and it looks at how those cases turned out, and adjusts this sentence up or down a little accordingly."

I turned that over in my mind.

"So… who decides that? Whether a case is similar or not."

"The Scales," the record-keeper said. "The Scales does all of it. It compares sentences, calculates degrees of similarity. There's almost no room for a person to step in."

*I understood, for the first time in that moment, a little more about where the Scales' precision came from. The Scales wasn't just weighing this one case. It carried the entire past on its back. This city's past, and the past of other cities too. I couldn't imagine how vast a memory that would be.*

"Are there cases from other cities too?" I asked.

"Plenty," the record-keeper said, pointing toward the shelves. "The Scales trade records with each other. What ruling happened where, and what came of that ruling afterward. It all piles up here."

---

I spent that whole morning digging through the precedent records.

*At first I didn't even know what I was looking for. I just read the Scales' sentences, over and over. Precise, dry, exceptionless sentences. Individual cases, shop disputes, quarrels between neighbors. All roughly the same structure. Facts, grounds, disposition. Sentences with no room for feeling to enter. I'd read sentences like that for three years. But these ones here were written for a different purpose than the ones I'd read. What I'd read were sentences delivering a verdict aloud. These were sentences justifying the verdict to itself.*

Around noon, I asked the record-keeper again.

"Are there any precedents where the Scales was wrong?"

The record-keeper's hand stopped. She looked at me for a long moment.

"What do you mean by wrong?" she asked back.

"…Where the sentence came out mistaken, or the grounds were mistaken."

"No," the record-keeper said. Firm. "Twenty years I've worked here, and I've never once seen a record like that."

"How would you check? Whether it's mistaken or not."

"There's no need to check," the record-keeper said. "The Scales is a calculating machine. Has a calculator ever gotten two plus two wrong? The Scales is the same. Correct input, correct output."

*I found myself briefly at a loss in front of that answer. It had no gap in it. It was the first time I dimly sensed that what I was looking for wasn't a calculation error in the Scales. The calculation isn't wrong. Then the crack had to be somewhere else.*

"…Input," I said. "The input could be wrong, couldn't it."

The record-keeper looked at me again. Longer this time.

"That," she said slowly. "Is a different question."

She paused a moment, choosing her words. Her gaze looked me up and down over the rims of her glasses.

"Young man, what are you looking for here." Her question, more direct than before.

I stiffened.

"…Nothing," I said. "Just curious."

The record-keeper didn't seem to believe that answer. She didn't press further, either.

"Whatever you're looking for," the record-keeper said, turning back to her papers. "There's one thing twenty years here has taught me. The Scales isn't wrong. That much is certain. Whether it's frightening or not, that doesn't change."

I held onto those words for a long time.

---

I held onto that sentence.

"Who does the input?"

"Records do," the record-keeper said. "Testimony, documents, witness statements. That's what people do. The Scales just takes it in and calculates."

"So if someone puts a wrong record in."

The record-keeper was silent for a moment.

"In theory," she said, carefully. "It's possible. But I've never seen a record of that actually happening. What comes in here all goes through review."

"Who reviews it?"

"The Scales," the record-keeper said. "The Scales again."

*I felt something strange in that circle. A person makes the record, the Scales reviews the record, and the Scales sets the sentence based on that record. There was exactly one point in that circle where a person got to step in. The moment a record is first made. Only that moment is touched by a human hand.*

I couldn't pin down that point precisely that day. I only left the room with a sense that it existed somewhere.

*I decided one thing clearly, leaving that room. The Scales must waver somewhere. If I found where it wavered, I'd have in hand the words to bring this city down. I didn't know then how dangerous that resolve was.*

---

That evening, Trope asked.

"Didn't see you today."

"…I went somewhere."

"Where?"

*I stopped, briefly, in front of that question. A few days earlier, I would have made up a lie without a thought. That day was different. Piling one more lie on her suddenly felt heavy. I wasn't exactly sure why.*

"…Just, here and there," I managed to say.

Trope looked at me a moment. Her eyes seemed ready to press further, but in the end she didn't.

"All right," Trope said. "You don't have to say if you don't want to."

*She always said that. She'd said it since the first day we met. Every time, I'd felt relief. That day, it wasn't relief that rose in me — it was something else. I was stacking lies on this person, and she kept accepting them. That accepting made me heavier.*

---

I went to the room of records for three days straight.

The record-keeper grew less wary of me than before. She stopped asking my name. She just called me "the student, there." I found that comfortable.

"I'd like to see records from other cities," I said on the second day.

The record-keeper glanced at me, then pointed toward a shelf much older than the others, further back in the room.

"There," the record-keeper said. "Not well organized. They come from many different cities, and each city has its own way of keeping records."

"Why? Why would records from other cities be needed here?"

"The Scales learn from each other," the record-keeper said. "It's rare for something to happen for the first time here in the Reckoning. Most things have already happened somewhere, once. So it references the outcome from elsewhere and fine-tunes this sentence."

"…Are those records accurate?"

"The Scales compiled them, so." The record-keeper said. But this time, her voice had lost a little of its earlier force. "Though — I can't be certain of that myself. The originals aren't kept here. The original record is received by that other city's Scales, and what's here is what's been reorganized into our Scales' format."

Something snagged in me at that.

"Reorganized — does that mean the wording could differ from the original?"

The record-keeper took off her glasses, then put them back on.

"Only the format changes," the record-keeper said. "The meaning doesn't. That's the Scales' principle."

*Listening to that, I strangely wanted to believe it. That the meaning doesn't change. But the very fact that I wanted to believe it made me doubt it all the more. I couldn't explain why, at the time. I know now. I was too desperate to find a crack. Desperation often makes doubt look like the truth.*

---

*I couldn't sleep that night, holding onto that feeling. The Scales isn't wrong. The record-keeper had said so too, and I'd confirmed it myself over three years. Not one miscarriage among the hundred and twelve cases. But if the precedent the Scales relies on had already been altered, somehow, in the hands of whoever first recorded it — I couldn't think that sentence through to the end. The more I thought about it, the more my fingertips trembled.*

*I was already looking for something by then, without even knowing what exactly. Not a flaw in the Scales, but a flaw in the ground the Scales stood on. If that existed, it wouldn't be the Scales' fault — it would be the fault of whoever made that ground. I couldn't put into words, that night, why that distinction mattered so much to me. I know now. I wanted to bring the Scales down. I didn't want to bring down the fact that the Scales was right. I couldn't distinguish, then, that those two things weren't the same.*

I decided to go back to the room of records the next day. This time, I planned to start not with this city's own precedents, but with records that had flowed in from other cities.

---

On the third day, I sat in front of that old shelf all day.

Each box had a city's name written on it. Names I'd never heard before. On some boxes the lettering on the outside had faded so much the city's name couldn't even be read. I opened those boxes one by one. The texture of the paper, the handwriting, the way sentences were composed — all a little different from box to box. Some cities wrote much longer sentences; others were shorter and drier than ours.

*Rifling through those boxes, I was overtaken by a strange feeling. This room held, compressed, the lives of cities I'd never once visited. Who had stolen what, who had cheated whom, who had been pushed out. All different names, different faces, but the same structure, over and over. I felt a strange exhaustion in front of that repetition. This whole world was weighing people with the same weight, in the same way.*

The record-keeper passed by me around evening and said.

"You there — what are you looking at so hard?"

"…Nothing," I said. "Just browsing."

"Serious browsing," the record-keeper said. Some amusement in her voice. "Coming back tomorrow too?"

"…Yes."

"I'll mark the spot, then. No sense finding it all over again each time."

The record-keeper tucked a small slip of paper into the box I'd been looking at. I found that gesture, oddly, kind. *Looking back now, I know how strange that situation was. I was searching for a hole to bring this city down through, and this city's own people were helping me do it more easily. Without knowing any of it.*

---

*I had no idea, then, what that would lead to. I was just looking for one crack. I believed that if I found it, I could hate this city again. I didn't know then how thin that belief was. Finding a crack wasn't going to make the old man's table disappear, or Trope's retreating back. I was just enduring day after day, holding both of them at once.*

*And three days later, I truly found it. A person's name was attached to it. Mena.*

*I didn't know, when I first saw that name, that it was even a person's name. It was just one of many characters written on the outside of the box. I had no idea, that day, how far that name would carry me, or what I would lose by the end of it.*
