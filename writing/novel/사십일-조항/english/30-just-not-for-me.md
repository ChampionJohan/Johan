# Chapter 30. Just Not For Me

> _Previously_ — Small changes had begun appearing all over the city.
> The Scales' numbers hadn't moved. The Adjudicator said "the number
> and what it's worth aren't the same thing." Trope had gone quiet,
> and Pheugon had seen her standing a long time at a Scales terminal.
> He decided to ask her about it the next day.

The next day I found her at the well.

The usual hour. She sat on the low stone wall, her water jar beside
her. She hadn't started drawing water yet.

"…Trope."

She looked up. She started to smile at me, then the smile stopped
halfway.

"…What is it."

"…Is something going on lately?"

Instead of answering, she ran her fingertips along the rim of the
well.

"…Nothing."

I didn't believe that. Her face said she knew I didn't believe it,
either.

Evening light rippled on the water in the well. The footsteps of
people drawing water grew fainter and farther. Soon we were the only
two left.

"…Trope." I called again. "Remember what you said, the day you
noticed I'd been going in and out of the Adjudicator's office?"

"…What did I say?"

"'The face people make when they're thinking about something that
doesn't show up on the Scales.'"

She laughed a little. "You remember that."

"You have that face right now."

Her laughter faded.

After a long while, she spoke again.

"…I won't ask what you heard in the Adjudicator's office," she said.
"You'll say it when you want to say it. Instead, let me say something
too. Trading."

I looked at her.

"…What."

"There's something I've been thinking about too, lately," she said.
"I couldn't find where to put it into words."

---

I sat down beside her. We stayed that way a long while without
speaking.

"…You stood at the Scales terminal a long time yesterday," I finally
said. "I saw you."

Her hand stopped.

"…I was just standing there."

"…Without raising your hand."

She looked at me. It was a look that seemed to be measuring — how
much she could say, how much I could bear.

"…Pheugon." She asked. "You've heard about the clause. From the
Adjudicator."

I was startled. I'd never told her that.

"…How did you know?"

"In the square. I'm not the only one who's noticed the Adjudicator's
window staying lit late these days. And—" she paused. "Your face has
changed. Lately."

---

"I've known about that clause," she went on, "since I was little."

I looked at her.

"Everyone born in this city knows. By rumor. No one knows the exact
content, but everyone knows there's a thing called the Forty-Day
Clause, and that it's never once fired. Parents use it to scare
children, too. 'If you don't behave, a clause like that will never
move for you.'"

"…You grew up hearing that."

"Yes," she said. "I believed it. That if a city became good, it
could be forgiven. But Pheugon, I—"

She looked straight at me.

"I've never once thought there was no god."

I had no answer for that. It wasn't the direction I'd expected.

---

"A lot of people misunderstand," she went on. "When I say I don't
resent the Scales, they think I don't believe in something. That's
not it. I believe it exists. Forgiveness, and whatever hands it down
— I believe those exist."

"But."

"But." She looked down at the water jar. "I don't believe it reaches
me."

Evening light rippled on the water.

"…What do you mean by that."

"The city can be forgiven," she said. "I believe that. What you've
been counting lately, Pheugon — I know about that too. The bakery,
the weights, the cane. I've seen it too. This city really is
changing. I believe that's real."

"…But?"

"I'm not."

She said it very quietly. Not an angry voice. Not a sad voice,
either. Just a voice stating a fact.

"There's forgiveness, I suppose," she said. "Just not for me."

---

I heard that sentence and couldn't say anything for a long time.

*I wanted to argue with her then. But I couldn't find the argument.
There was nothing logically wrong with what she'd said. That was what
frightened me more. I didn't know how to defeat her words.*

"…Why," I finally managed. "Why aren't you, Trope?"

"Remember my mother?" she asked.

"…Yes."

"My mother crossed the weight limit and was driven out. The Scales
calculated it precisely. I don't doubt that. But there's something I
want to know."

She looked at me.

"Do you know what my weight is?"

I didn't know.

"I know," she said. "I check every day. Other people check to feel
reassured. I'm different. I check because I'm afraid the number
might suddenly spike. Because I'm my mother's daughter. I think the
limit she crossed might be in me too. Inherited. Everyone says the
Scales doesn't measure by family. I can't bring myself to believe
that."

"The Adjudicator says," I said, carefully, "that the Scales really
doesn't measure by family. The records are kept entirely separate."

"I know that too," she said. "I know it in my head. The Adjudicator
told me that himself. Several times. But knowing and believing are
different. I know it and still can't believe it."

I turned that sentence over for a long time. Knowing and believing
being different things. I knew the Scales was right, and knowing
that had never once lessened my anger. She knew the Scales didn't
measure by family, and knowing that had never once lessened her fear.
Knowing didn't take the place of believing.

---

She stood up.

"…I'll show you," she said.

I followed her to a Scales terminal near the well. A small terminal
standing in a corner of the square. She raised her hand. A number
came up on the screen.

I looked at the number. Low. Much lower than I'd expected — well
below the city's average.

"…It's low," I said.

"Yes." She lowered her hand. "It's low. Because I haven't done much
wrong. But every time I look at this number, I see another number
alongside it. One that isn't here."

"…What number."

"The number my mother crossed," she said, pointing at the screen
with a fingertip. "It's not written on the Scales. Not anywhere on my
screen. But I feel like it's layered behind this one. The number you
can't see is scarier. At least a visible number, you can check."

I heard that and looked again at the small screen. Only one number
really was showing there. The other number she spoke of was nowhere
to be found. And yet, in that moment, I had the strange sensation of
seeing it anyway.

We went back and sat at the well.

---

"I was born in this city," she went on. "I was born carrying this
city's sin along with it. My mother's overflowed weight, this city's
overflowed weight — I grew up inside that. It couldn't not have
gotten onto me."

"…But if the city turns—"

"Then the city lives," she said. "I'd live inside it too. But that
and me, personally, being forgiven are different things. The city
living and me being all right are different problems."

That distinction felt foreign to me. And, at the same time, strangely
exact.

"Five years ago," she went on, "the day my mother left, my
grandfather took me and said something. 'You're not guilty. You just
got left behind.' I know he meant it as comfort. But that was the
first time I had a strange thought."

"…What thought."

"Someone with no sin doesn't need forgiveness," she said. "Forgiveness
is for someone with sin. But I was told I had no sin, and at the same
time I was living inside the results of that sin. I felt like there
was no place for me between those two sentences. Not a sinner. Not
someone to be forgiven, either. Just someone left behind."

*I understood, in that moment, that she had carried that one fear
every day since her mother was driven out three years before. I had
carried one anger for three years. She had carried one fear for three
years. We each held something different, and were exactly as heavy.*

"What about you," she asked, suddenly. "Being angry versus being
afraid — which is harder?"

I couldn't answer right away.

"…I don't know," I finally said. "I was on the angry side. The whole
time. But anger at least has a direction. I know who I'm angry at.
Listening to you, I think fear might not have a direction at all."

"That's right," she said. "Anger points at something. Fear just
points at me."

---

"I know I'm strange," she said. "Everyone tells me 'you did nothing
wrong.' That's true. I know it too. I never cheated with weights.
Never stole anything. But that doesn't make me safe."

"…Why not."

"Because." She paused a moment. "I'm not afraid of something I did.
I'm afraid of who I am. I'm the daughter of someone who went over the
limit. That fact doesn't change no matter what I do."

I felt something shift, deep down, hearing that.

"Forgiveness," she went on. "For other people it seems to feel like a
rule. Do this and you're forgiven, do that and you're not. Not for
me. For me, forgiveness exists, and it leaves me out of it. Like
everyone's in the room and I'm standing outside the door."

That image stayed with me a long time.

"…Who's in the room," I asked.

"Everyone," she said. "The baker. The weights man. The Adjudicator.
You too. Everyone will eventually walk into that room. I get as far
as the door. Every time. But I can't grab the handle."

"Why can't you."

"Because I don't know if my hand has the standing to grab it."

I started to say something, then stopped. It sounded too much like
something I'd carried for three years myself. *I was a boy who took
care not to lose his standing.* She was standing outside a door
because she believed she had no standing. Same word, opposite
direction.

"I have something similar," I said. "I spent three years guarding my
standing. You spent three years believing you had none. But somehow
we both ended up standing in the same place. Outside the door."

She looked at me for a long time.

"…No one's ever put it that way to me before," she said quietly.

---

I couldn't say anything. I had no comfort to offer. Like the
Adjudicator had done for me, I didn't want to tell her it was all
right either. I understood now how empty that would be.

"…Trope," I finally managed. "I don't know either. What I should
say."

"It's all right," she said. And, for the first time, smiled a little.
"I don't need anyone to say anything. Just being listened to is
enough."

*Hearing that, I thought of the Adjudicator. He had told me the exact
same thing. Listening. That's all. That was the first time I truly
understood the weight of that word. Listening wasn't nothing.*

*I turned her words over for a long time that night. Forgiveness
exists, but not for her. I found that sentence strangely familiar. I
was angry knowing forgiveness existed. She was afraid knowing
forgiveness existed. Neither of us had ever doubted forgiveness. Both
of us believed it never reached our own place. I didn't want to hand
that place to anyone else. She believed she couldn't fit into it. The
directions were opposite. The place we were standing was the same.*

"…Trope." I called again. "What if — hypothetically — I could
trigger the clause. Would you be saved along with everyone else?"

She looked at me. A long time.

"The city," she said. "I live in it too. But Pheugon, that's the
question that frightens me most. The city living and me living aren't
the same sentence."

"…Why aren't they the same sentence."

"Because," she said. "The city exists as a number. What the Scales
measures. But I, Pheugon — I'm not a number. I'm a person. And a
person has things scarier than numbers."

I didn't fully understand the meaning of that, that night.

We didn't draw water that evening. We just sat there a long while,
until the sun had sunk completely below the horizon.

"…I'll be here tomorrow too," she said again as we parted. The same
words she'd said once before.

I didn't know the weight of those words then.
