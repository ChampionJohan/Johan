# Chapter 9. The First Day

> _Previously_ — The second sluice gate was shut. Not an accident — a
> scheduled closure. This world had not closed the gate to punish Pheugon;
> it had simply gone on running as usual, and he happened to be inside
> that usual. In the dark where no one answered, he held out the night by
> counting his water and his bread.

I don't know exactly when the light disappeared completely.

The faint light that had leaked through the crack above me vanished while I drifted in and out of sleep. I waited for it to come back. It didn't.

*That was the first time I knew a day had passed. Not something I saw. Something I felt. If the light went out once and didn't come back, that meant night had come. That was how I counted the first day.*

The dark split into complete dark and slightly-less-complete dark. I felt the difference between them with my skin, not my eyes. When there had been light, the air had felt somehow different too. Probably my imagination. But even an illusion was something to hold onto.

I kept opening and closing my eyes, knowing it made no difference whether they were open or shut. My body wanted some familiar motion to perform.

---

The water rose to my knees.

At first it had been my ankles. Waking from sleep, it was my knees. I didn't know when. I couldn't tell whether the water itself had risen, or whether I'd simply kept sliding toward the lower ground.

*Seeing that water, I felt real fear for the first time. What I'd felt before had come from not knowing which way was which. This was different. Water rising meant that as time passed, my position kept getting worse. I learned, then, that things can get worse even while you sit perfectly still.*

I didn't know why the water was rising. Some other gate might have opened somewhere else, sending the current this way. Or this stretch might simply run on a cycle like that. I had no way to find out. All I had was the fact — the water was rising.

*Watching it, I thought again of that low mechanical hum. Something, somewhere, running the whole of this waterway, going on perfectly fine right at this very moment. To that device, this was just another day's routine. Send water this way, close that gate. I was an accident caught by chance in one corner of that routine. The water rising wasn't meant to threaten me. It was only flowing toward where it had to flow.*

I got to my feet. My legs shook. I felt my way along the wall to somewhere a little higher — a small ledge at the edge of the station. The water there only reached my instep.

That ledge wasn't wide. Two steps and it ended. I crouched in that narrow space and looked up. The crack the light had leaked through was still dark.

On a chance, I tried to reach that crack. If I climbed the stone pillar, my hand might reach. I gripped the pillar and hauled myself up. My foot slipped. Twice, three times. The last time my knee scraped the stone and drew blood.

*I only stopped trying once I felt that blood with my hand. Only then did pain seem to properly return to me at all. Before that, my body hadn't had the room to feel its own hurt as hurt. Strangely, the blood from my knee calmed me, in that moment. It felt like one certain thing had appeared. At least the pain wasn't lying.*

---

I was hungry. I took out the bread.

*In that moment I began calculating for the first time. To be precise, I was picking up the calculation I'd started the day before, sitting in front of the gate. From here, it was real calculation. Not a rough count — a calculation to survive.*

Five loaves of bread. I'd already eaten one, so four remained. I tore off half of one and ate it, and tied the rest back up.

*I decided, then, to eat half a loaf a day. That would hold out eight days. The flask was half full. There was water all around, but I didn't know if it was safe to drink. I had to ration it. A few mouthfuls a day, only.*

*Doing this calculation, I felt, strangely, calmed. Numbers hadn't abandoned me. Everything else in that dark had abandoned me. Only numbers still answered, when I asked.*

Chewing the bread, I thought of home. Of the table, to be precise. The one with four seats where now only one gets used. I tried to remember what our last dinner there had been. I couldn't.

*A strange thing. I was someone who remembered everything countable. A hundred and twelve cases, a hundred and eleven erased words. And yet I couldn't remember what our last dinner had been. I learned that day, for the first time, how easily the things you don't count simply vanish. I'd spent far too long, living, hanging onto only what could be counted.*

---

I drew a line on the wall with my fingernail.

That was the only way I had to count days. Scratching a mark into the stone with a fingertip. One line. Meaning today was the first day.

*I wasn't even confident about exactly which day it was. Counting from the night I left home, this might already have been the second or third day. I decided to start counting again from the moment I got trapped in front of this gate. That was the number that mattered to me now.*

Beside that line I began another calculation.

Days left until proclamation day. By the supervisor's schedule, I should be somewhere in the middle of the road to the Reckoning by now. Two days' travel, a day's rest on arrival, then the proclamation. By the original plan, I should have been standing in that city's square in three days.

*Doing that calculation, I felt a strange emotion. Relief. I hoped those three days would pass — that the city would cross its proclamation day without me. What happened when a herald didn't show, I didn't know exactly. But I knew something would be delayed. That delay felt, to me, like success.*

*Reading this sentence now, I look strangely at who I was then. Even in that moment of terror, I hadn't stopped calculating. Trapped in the water, I was still checking whether time was flowing in the direction I wanted. Too precise a thing for someone that frightened to be doing.*

---

When would this gate open.

I felt again at the numbers carved into the metal plate. I could feel the depth of the grooves with my fingertips, but not what number they made. I stood a long while in front of that plate.

*I felt, for the first time, a desperate need to know something. Until then I hadn't been someone who fretted much over what I didn't know. Training had made me that way. Ask, if you don't know. If no one told you, let it go. But this number, I couldn't let go. This was a number my life depended on.*

I struck the plate. No response. Of course not. This wasn't a thing you could speak to.

*I thought of the Scales again, in that moment. The one they say exists in the Reckoning. It doesn't speak, but it at least shows a number. Put a hand up, and something answers. This gate didn't even offer that much. I realized, for the first time in my life, that I had never before stood this long in front of something that gave no answer at all. Someone raised in the world of the Scales takes it for granted that asking always gets some kind of response. I learned, in front of that door, just how thin that assumption had always been.*

Once, amid the water's noise, I thought I heard something like a human voice. I held my breath and listened. Nothing was there. Just water striking stone, sounding that way by coincidence.

*I had that same illusion several times after. Sometimes it seemed like someone calling my name in the water's noise; sometimes like someone murmuring something close to a sentence. I learned later that this wasn't rare at all. A person can bear a place with no sound at all better than a place with constant, irregular sound — because in the second kind of place, the mind starts trying to find words inside it. The ears can't stand being bored. I didn't know that then. I was just afraid.*

---

Having no way at all to gauge how much time had passed was the hardest thing to bear.

At the training hall, a bell rang. At home, the sun crossed the window. Here, there was nothing. I tried measuring time by how many times I opened and closed my eyes. I soon learned how inaccurate that was. Sometimes I opened my eyes after a single breath's worth of closing them; sometimes after sleeping a long while. And there was no way to tell the two apart.

*I experienced, then, for the first time, calculation itself breaking down. The flask and the bread could be counted in numbers. Time couldn't. I found that difference hard to bear. When something countable and something uncountable sit in the same place, the uncountable swallows everything else.*

So instead I decided to count paces. Walking back and forth across the station. Twelve steps, wall to wall. I decided that about a hundred round trips meant, roughly, some fixed amount of time had passed. There was no basis for that. I just needed something to count.

One round trip, two, three. I counted the numbers silently, not aloud. I had no strength left to make a sound yet, and no reason to. I was saving my voice, then. Without even knowing why.

*I still don't know what those numbers meant. But while I walked and counted like that, I could forget, for a little while, that the water was rising, that the gate wouldn't open. Counting was that, for me. Even when it gave no answer, it still let me endure.*

After a long while of walking back and forth, I stopped. Out of breath. Walking in water took far more strength than I'd thought. I sat back down on the ledge and caught my breath.

*In that moment I noticed something strange. I was counting my steps, and at the same time, counting something else. The days left until the Reckoning. The forty days that city's people didn't even know had started yet. I was turning those two numbers over side by side in my head. The speed at which my own life was running down, and the speed at which that city's remaining time was running down. Strangely, it comforted me that the two were shrinking in the same direction. Every hour I held out here meant that, over there, the possibility was shrinking too.*

*My hand shakes, writing this sentence. I truly thought that way, then. I set my own dying time and one city's dying chance on the same scale, and found comfort in it. How terrible a calculation that was, I would only learn much later.*

---

My body shook. From too long in the water.

I wrung out my wet clothes as best I could. It did nothing. Water was everywhere. There was nowhere to dry.

*That night — though I didn't even know which night it was — listening to my teeth chatter, I thought: I could die like this. Strangely, the thought didn't frighten me. I think I already knew, then, that there was something I feared more than death. That the Reckoning would survive. That frightened me more.*

I hugged my knees. The wound on my knee throbbed. My clothes were still wet, showing no sign of drying. I curled myself as small as I could. It was the only way I had left to keep any warmth.

I was hungry. I wanted more bread. I held back. I'd already eaten today's share. I kept that rule for myself, exactly. Half a loaf a day. Yesterday, and today too. I checked how much bread was left with my fingertips, again and again. What was left after taking one from four, then half again from that. The number was exact. That exactness was, in that moment, almost my only comfort.

*I felt a strange pride, then. That I'd kept the rule even while hungry. It sounds absurd, I know. But that was the one rule left, in that moment, that I could still keep. Everything else had slipped out of my hands — the height of the water, when the gate would open, where exactly I was. In that helplessness, there was exactly one thing I could still decide for myself. How many pieces of bread to eat. I held onto that one thing.*

*Rereading this sentence now, it makes my skin crawl. In that moment I was setting my own life and one city's fate on the same scale. And without any hesitation, I thought the city's side had to weigh more — no, had to be made to weigh more. What that meant, I didn't know, then.*

---

I thought of the supervisor.

By now the supervisor probably believed I was somewhere on the road to the Reckoning. Or perhaps he'd already begun to sense something was wrong. A herald doesn't stray far from the set schedule. Running a day late might happen. Going entirely out of contact doesn't.

*I pictured the supervisor's face, then. The cool room, the roster on the wall, the number 112 beside my name. I wondered if he might be looking at that number again right now. That thought hurt me, strangely, in that moment. I didn't think I'd betrayed the supervisor. I only felt sorry that if he was worried about me, that worry, right now, in this moment, could do nothing at all.*

I didn't hold onto that regret for long. Holding it felt like it would throw off my calculations. I went back to the line on the wall, to the numbers of water and bread. That was the safer place to be.

I traced the first line I'd scratched on the wall again with my fingertip. One. A beginning.
