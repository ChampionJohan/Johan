# Chapter 28. The Confession

> _Previously_ — The Adjudicator told Pheugon the clause's true
> trigger condition: only the one who read the sentence aloud can
> confirm it. In that moment, Pheugon understood the real reason he'd
> fled — not fear, but calculation. "So it wasn't fear that made you
> run." That night Pheugon sat unable to speak, and the next day he
> knocked on the Adjudicator's door again.

After I knocked, I realized I hadn't decided what to say.

The Adjudicator opened the door. Nothing had changed from the day
before. Same seat, same desk. And yet I crossed that threshold as an
entirely different person than the day before.

"Come in."

I went in. I sat without waiting to be told.

There was nothing on the desk again today. Where the scroll had lain
yesterday, only the wood grain showed now. That emptiness pressed on
me all the more. There was nowhere to hide.

Morning light came in through the window at a slant. I watched it
touch the edge of the desk and slowly creep along. While I failed to
start speaking, the light moved two finger-widths.

"…Adjudicator."

"Yes."

I opened my mouth and closed it again. Three times, maybe.

The Adjudicator didn't rush me. He didn't look at his papers either.
He simply watched me.

---

"There was someone," I finally said, "who came to our house from the
Reckoning. Three years ago."

The Adjudicator's expression didn't change at all. He seemed to have
already guessed.

"My father took him in. That was the kind of man he was. Quick to
take in strangers."

I stopped speaking there for a long while.

"…Go on," the Adjudicator said. It wasn't a push. It was more a way
of letting me know there was another door where I'd stopped.

"He used a room. A converted storeroom — my father hurried a bed in
and made it into a room. There was a light on in there every night.
He was writing something. Said it was a verification task, copying
over my father's ledger."

"…What kind of verification."

"I don't know," I said. That wasn't a lie. "I was twelve. I thought
it was grown-up business. I still don't know."

The Adjudicator didn't ask further.

I nearly said I could still close my eyes and see the light leaking
through that door crack, then stopped myself. If I said that too, I
was sure my voice would break. Instead I caught a breath and moved to
the next sentence.

---

"For a few months it was fine," I went on. "We ate dinner together.
He talked with me and my brother. When we asked about the Reckoning,
he'd answer smiling. He said the Scales measures everyone so
precisely, no one ever gets treated unfairly."

"You believed that."

"…Yes." I looked at him. "I thought accuracy was a good thing."

"And now."

I couldn't answer.

"Then," I went on, "something changed. I don't know exactly when.
There were more evenings my father said nothing for days at a time. I
heard my mother crying alone in the kitchen. Twice. The second time
was quieter. I stood outside the door, then just turned back."

"Didn't knock."

"…No."

"Why."

I gave that question real thought for the first time.

"…I thought if I got caught, it would be harder for my mother. But I
know now. That wasn't for my mother. It was for me. Pretending not to
hear was easier than hearing and not being able to do anything."

The Adjudicator said nothing. There was no pity in his eyes. But no
indifference either. He was just listening.

---

"That winter I saw my father put his hand on a Scales terminal.
Once." I said. "A number came up on the screen. I didn't see the
number. He looked at it a long while and said, 'It's fine.' I hadn't
even asked."

"And that man."

"He left that winter. I walked with him as far as the edge of town. I
don't know why. I think I wanted to ask something, but in the end I
asked nothing at all. I just said goodbye."

"Did you hate that man."

"Not then," I said. "I didn't know how to hate yet. The hating grew
later. Very slowly."

The Adjudicator moved for the first time. He drew his fingertip once
along the edge of the desk.

"His name," he asked. "Do you know that man's name."

I looked at him.

"…I know it."

"Not going to say it."

"…No."

He nodded. He didn't press further. I understood then that he had no
intention of forcing me any further than I was willing to go.

---

"Our family fell apart after that," I went on. "My brother left that
winter. Without a word to me. I still don't know where he is."

"Did you ever look."

"No," I said, looking down at my hands. "I wouldn't have known what
to ask him if I found him."

"Your mother."

"She went to live with relatives in another city the next year. She
asked me to come with her. I said I didn't want to quit training. That
was what I told myself then."

"The real reason."

I stayed silent a long time.

"…I don't know. Even now."

"Your father."

"He's still in that city. We don't live together. He lives alone, on
the other side of the city. We see each other occasionally. We don't
talk about it."

"Not once."

"Not once."

The Adjudicator closed his eyes briefly, then opened them. It wasn't
surprise. It was more like the motion of putting something away
inside himself.

"The last time we met," I went on. This was something I hadn't been
asked, but it came out on its own. "It was a few days before I
received the Reckoning case. As we were parting, my father said one
thing. 'What case are you reading these days.' I lied, said it was
something small. It wasn't a lie then, since I hadn't been given the
Reckoning case yet — but I was already, in that moment, practicing
how to hide something."

"What did your father say."

"'Take care of yourself.' That was all."

"That was the most he could do."

I nodded at that. Strangely, hearing that sentence from someone
else's mouth, it sounded different. It was the very sentence I had
told myself for three years, but coming from the Adjudicator it
sounded like neither an accusation nor an excuse. Just a fact.

---

The room went quiet. Through the window came the smell of evening
drawing close.

"The Scales must have calculated it," I said. This was something I
wanted to say. "How much everyone's weight went up — I don't know
exactly. I never asked. But I know the Scales calculated it
precisely. The Scales is never wrong."

"But."

"But nothing came back." My voice cracked. "My brother didn't come
back. My mother didn't come back. Our family never became four
again. A precise disposal came down, and it fixed nothing."

*That was the first time I ever said that sentence aloud. Three years
I'd carried it only inside myself. The moment it came out as sound, I
understood for the first time how heavy it was. Some sentences weigh
far more coming out of a mouth than they do written on paper.*

"So I gave that anger away," I went on. "Not just to that one man —
to the whole city. I put the whole city where that man had been. I
know it isn't fair. I didn't know that then."

"Aside from the fact that he was from this city," the Adjudicator
asked, "did you have any other grounds to hate the whole city."

I had no answer for that.

"None," I said, after a long while. "I'd never met a single person
from the Reckoning. Not until I came to this city."

"And still you hated it."

"…Yes."

"And after meeting them."

I thought of Trope. The baker. The merchant fixing his weights. The
people drawing water at the well.

"…I don't know," I said. "Faces appeared. Once a face appears, it
gets hard to hate. And that doesn't mean the hating stops. It just
gets harder."

The Adjudicator heard that and passed no verdict. I was grateful for
it. And at the same time, frightened. Without a verdict, I had to
keep walking through it alone.

---

"For three years," I opened my mouth again. "I did nothing wrong. No
lying, no anger, kept my weight as low as it could go. Everyone
thought it was diligence."

"You thought so too."

"…Yes. But I learned yesterday. It wasn't diligence. I was guarding
my standing. Someday there'd be a sentence I'd have to read toward the
Reckoning, and I didn't want to lose the standing to read it. I'd been
preparing that, three years, without even knowing it."

The Adjudicator looked at me a moment.

"That's the part hurting you most right now."

I nodded. My throat closed and no words came.

---

The Adjudicator was quiet a long time.

I thought he'd say something. Comfort, or reproach. He did neither.

"I heard your story," he said. That was all.

"…That's it?"

"That's it." He looked at me. "If I told you it's all right, that
would be a lie. I don't know why your family fell apart. Whether
what you felt was fair or not isn't mine to judge either. All I know
is that you said that story out loud today, for the first time in
three years."

I turned that over.

"That," he said, "is enough for today."

"…Adjudicator." I asked. "Why don't you comfort me?"

He thought a moment.

"Comfort is easy," he said. "Saying it's all right takes less than
five seconds. I hear your own father said that to you too. You just
told me yourself. What did that do for you?"

I couldn't answer.

"Nothing," the Adjudicator said. "That sentence was for your father's
own sake. It wasn't for you. I don't want to add one more of those
onto you."

"Then what are you doing for me."

"Listening," he said. "That's all. But I don't think that's nothing.
You went three years without ever getting all of that out to anyone.
Today you did. That's not because I did anything. You did it."

*I found that answer disappointing. At the time. I think I'd been
hoping for more. Forgiveness, or denial — some sentence that would
close this story out. The Adjudicator didn't give me that. I know now.
That was the most honest thing he could have given me.*

---

Walking out, I found it strange that I felt neither lighter nor
heavier. I felt something should have changed. I'd finally told the
whole story, after three years. And yet my brother still hadn't come
back, my mother still wasn't in that city, my father still lived
alone on the other side of it.

*Speaking hadn't fixed anything. I learned that that day. Just as the
Scales' disposal had fixed nothing, my confession fixed nothing
either. But one thing was different. The Scales' disposal had kept
me locked inside it, alone. Confession had, at least, brought one
other person into that room.*

I came out into the hallway; it was evening. The square was visible
through the window. People stood in line at the Scales terminal. An
ordinary sight. But that day the line looked a little shorter.

*I didn't know why it looked that way then. I thought it was just my
imagination. I know now. It wasn't imagination. From around then, very
slowly, this city really was changing.*

---

Trope was at the well. She saw me and, unlike usual, didn't start
talking. She just patted the spot beside her with her hand. An
invitation to sit.

I sat.

For a long while neither of us said anything. People fetching water
came and went, one and two at a time. An evening bell rang somewhere
far off.

"…You have the face of someone who set down something heavy today,"
Trope said first.

"…Does it show?"

"Yes." She glanced at me. "But it's not the face of someone who set
down all of it. It's the face of someone who set down half."

I laughed at that. A sad laugh.

"…That's exact."

"I know that face," she said, resting a foot on a stone. "I used to
wear it a lot myself."

I looked at her. For the first time, I seriously considered that she
too might be carrying something as heavy as mine.

"…Will you tell it someday, Trope? Until you've set it all down?"

She didn't answer for a long while.

"…I don't know," she finally said. "I've never thought of it as a
weight I could set down."

I didn't fully grasp what that meant, at the time.

*I know now. She was already handing me, that day, the first line of
her own story. I didn't catch it. I was too caught up in telling my
own. The price for that came later, in a different place.*

We didn't say anything more that evening. We filled the jars, walked
together, and parted. As we said goodbye, she added one more line.

"…I'll be here tomorrow too."

I didn't understand, that night, why that sentence stayed with me the
way it did.
