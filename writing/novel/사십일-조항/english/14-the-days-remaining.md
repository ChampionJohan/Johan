# Chapter 14. The Days Remaining

> _Previously_ — The child who pulled Pheugon from the well at the Reckoning was named Trope. Pheugon hid his real name and lied, calling himself "Had." Trope brought him dry clothes and bread and let him sleep in the shed. The city was quiet and clean, and people kept glancing up. Pheugon didn't ask why, and fell asleep without counting anything.

The first thought I had when I opened my eyes that morning was a number.

Habit. Three years of checking my weight first thing every morning — the habit had stayed in my body even in this city, where nothing marked my wrist. I raised my hand, then realized there was nowhere to raise it to, and lowered it again.

*I still can't quite explain the emptiness of that moment. Having no number should have been a relief. It wasn't. I didn't know how to live without a number.*

Morning light came through the gap in the shed door. I folded the blanket and went outside.

Outside, people were already moving. Someone carrying a water jar, someone with a broom. The same quiet I'd seen the day before held true in the morning too. Nobody was running.

*At that point I was still thinking of this city's soon-to-vanish fate and this morning scene as two separate things. I didn't yet know that they were the same morning of the same city — that there was no contradiction between them at all.*

---

Trope hadn't come yet. I hesitated in front of the shed a moment, then decided to walk, staying close by.

Around a corner I found the same kind of post I'd seen the day before. A post about twice a person's height. A plate fixed to the top, with a number floating on it.

I went closer.

**39.**

That was all. No other letters. No city name, no clause number, no explanation. Just a number.

I stopped where I stood.

*I knew that number. Knew it exactly. Forty days' grace. Sentence of demolition. The number I'd been counting since the day I received the document in the supervisor's office. And that number was already being counted here, in the middle of this city, without me.*

I stood in front of it a long time. It made no sense. A sentence only takes effect once someone reads it aloud. I hadn't read anything yet. Not a single letter.

And yet the number already stood at thirty-nine.

---

A woman passing by stopped beside me and looked up at the plate. Older woman, carrying a market basket. She looked at the number for a long while, then walked on with no expression at all.

I followed her and asked.

"…Excuse me."

She turned.

"What's that number?" I asked.

She looked at me strangely for a moment, then seemed to place me as an outsider. Her expression softened a little.

"The days remaining," she said. "If you have to ask, you really must be from out of town."

"…Days remaining."

"Until demolition," she said, curtly. "Our city. The notice already came. Just the one who reads it hasn't come yet."

I couldn't breathe.

"…The one who reads it?"

"The herald," she said. "The Scales finished its calculation. The sentence document's already out. But it only really starts once someone reads it aloud. That person hasn't come yet. So everyone's waiting. Nobody knows what happens if that number hits zero and no one's come."

"…Has this happened before?" I asked. "In another city."

The woman shrugged.

"I've heard of it," she said. "Some far-off city, back when my grandmother was young, is what they say. But I don't know anyone who's actually seen it. A whole city ending up like this is very rare. So nobody really knows what to do, either."

She looked back up at the plate.

"Our Adjudicator's keeping watch on that number. Whatever happens, he's the one who'd know best," she said. "So we're just waiting. Until he says something."

*I couldn't say anything, standing there. She probably had no idea why I was standing there like that. It was the first time I truly understood it. The Scales sends its notice to a city the moment its calculation is finished. It's true a person has to read it aloud for it to take effect, but the Scales has already fixed when that person will arrive. The fortieth day. What happens if no one comes that day — nobody knows. I didn't know either.*

---

After the woman left, I looked up at the plate again.

**39.**

*That was a notice. Not a verdict — a notice. I understood the difference for the first time that day. The Scales had its own way of telling a city this news without me. My running away didn't leave this city in the dark about anything. This city already knew. What was coming for it. How much time was left. Only the hand to actually open that door was missing.*

*Only I knew that hand was mine.*

I kept walking toward the square. The farther I walked, the more plates I saw. On building walls, at the well, at the market entrance. Different sizes, but all the same number. 39.

People walked past them with a glance and moved on. No crying, no shouting. What Trope had said yesterday came back to me. "Everyone's counting."

---

Nearer the market, the crowd thickened. A vegetable seller was setting out her stall, a woman selling cloth was arranging her wares. One person was haggling over a price with a merchant. The merchant wasn't giving ground.

"You want a discount because we've got thirty-nine days left?" the merchant said. "Goods sold today get today's price."

A few people nearby laughed at that. Real laughter. I found it strange. Under a number counting down to the city's end, people were still bargaining, still laughing.

*I read that, at the time, as calm. I read it differently now. It wasn't calm — it was endurance. Keep doing today's work today — that was the only method these people had left. If there's nowhere to flee, and no way to turn the number back, all that's left is to do today's work today.*

Somewhere, someone in an official's uniform rang a bell and called something out. I froze, thinking it was the sentence document.

"West well maintenance starting tomorrow. No water in the mornings," the official called. "Use the east well."

Just a routine notice. People nodded and dispersed. The city was counting the day of its own end while still maintaining its wells.

*I felt something in front of that scene I couldn't quite name. I expected to feel anger, and didn't. Not because this city was calm about it, but because the fact that it was still moving as a city at all was what shook me. I had already thought of this city as a place already finished. But this city didn't yet think of itself that way.*

---

Leaving the market on my way to the square, I saw an old man.

He was standing in front of a plate mounted on a wall, not moving. Unlike the others who glanced and walked on, he stood rooted to the spot. The hand gripping his cane was trembling.

I stopped and, passing beside him, looked at his face.

He was crying. No sound. Just tears. He didn't wipe them away.

*I've never forgotten that old man. The only tears I saw that day. Everyone else was calm; only he wasn't. I wondered, then, why this one man couldn't endure it the way the others did. I ask it differently now. Were the others really enduring it — or had they, like the old man, already spent all the strength they had for crying?*

I felt I should go over and say something to him. I didn't. I didn't know what to say. I didn't know if I had any right to say anything at all to this city.

*I just walked past. That was the biggest lie I told that day. Bigger than the name Had. I walked past that old man pretending to know nothing, pretending none of it concerned me.*

---

On one side of the square, some children were playing. A game where you threw stones into a chalked circle — listening closer, they were counting numbers as they played.

"Thirty-nine, thirty-eight, thirty-seven—"

Each throw lowered the count by one. I couldn't tell whether it was a rule of the game or just the children bringing the number they saw every day into their play.

*Something in me twisted, watching them. An emotion I couldn't name exactly. Not anger, not sadness. That these children had turned their city's ending into a counting game, and how natural that looked — that was what shook me.*

One child spotted me and asked.

"Mister, can you count too?"

"…I count."

"How many left, you think?"

I didn't answer. The child seemed satisfied without an answer and went back to the game.

---

I sat down in the shade at the edge of the square. One question wouldn't leave my head.

Why wasn't anyone fleeing?

In the city I came from, half the people would have already packed their bags. A whole city's demolition, with thirty-nine days' grace — anyone who could move would have moved in that time.

Not here. All that morning, walking, I hadn't seen a single person packing, not a single cart leaving.

I found it strange, almost frightening. Knowing a whole city would disappear, and still doing today's work, selling today's goods, drawing today's water. As if the number thirty-nine weren't real, or as if they believed it had nothing to do with them.

*It wasn't that. I only understood by that evening. It wasn't that they didn't believe the number. They believed it too well — so well that there was nothing left to do but believe, and so they chose to live today instead. I didn't know the difference then.*

Trope arrived beside me without my noticing.

"What are you looking at?"

I startled and shifted a little.

"…Why isn't anyone leaving?" I asked. "This city's disappearing, you said. Within thirty-nine days."

Trope glanced at the square's plate. Her expression didn't change.

"Wherever you go, there's a Scales," Trope said.

"…What difference does that make?"

"Leaving gets you nothing. Every other city has a Scales too, and its own weight there. Nothing you've built up here disappears. You carry all of it with you."

*I thought I understood that at the time. Actually I'd only understood half of it. What Trope meant wasn't only about the system. I wouldn't fully understand it until weeks later, when I heard her mother's story.*

"And," Trope added. "This is my city."

She said it without looking at me. She was looking at the square's plate.

**39.**

I looked at her profile for a long time. I didn't know what to say, so I said nothing.

---

We sat like that for a while. Trope spoke first.

"Had," Trope asked. "You don't say where you came from, you don't know where you're going. So what are you going to do with your thirty-nine days here?"

I had no answer to that question. Or rather — I had an answer, but couldn't give it. *I'm here to watch this city fall.* Just imagining saying it made my stomach turn.

"…I don't know," I said again.

"You keep saying that," Trope said. Not laughing, but not mocking either. Just stating a fact.

"…Sorry."

"Nothing to be sorry about." Trope stood. "Come with me, then. If you've got nothing to do, there's something you can help with."

"…What kind of thing?"

"Errands at the Scales. It's what I do."

My body went stiff at that. I hadn't expected the words "at the Scales" to carry so much weight.

"…What do you do there?"

Trope started walking and answered.

"You'll see when we get there."

---

Following Trope, I looked back once more at the plate in the square.

**39.**

*I haven't forgotten that number for a single day since. It was a different kind of number from the hundred and twelve cases I'd counted over three years. Those numbers had belonged to other people. This number was one I'd made. No — not made by me. Made by the Scales. And yet I felt, standing in front of it, as if I'd carved it in myself.*

*It wasn't guilt. Not yet, at that point. It was closer to a strange sense of ownership. This number is mine. I brought it to this city. I had fled, but this number hadn't followed me — it had already arrived here first, waiting for me.*

*I still couldn't fully feel the hundred and twenty thousand people inside that number as people. Trope was the one exception. Trope had a face, a name, a hand that had pulled me out of the water the day before. The rest — a hundred and twenty thousand — were still just a number to me.*

*I don't remember exactly when the rest began to gain faces. Only that it started that day, in that square, in the long moment I spent looking at Trope's profile.*

Trope was already walking well ahead. I quickened my step to catch up.

*I didn't know, then, what "errands at the Scales" would mean, or what I'd see standing in front of it. I just followed Trope. In a fled city, under a fled name, with a fled sentence in my bag.*
