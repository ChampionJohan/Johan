# Chapter 23. The Man Who Does Not Get Angry

> _Previously_ — The Adjudicator told Pheugon he was lying to himself. "That isn't the face of someone afraid." For the first time, Pheugon felt the things he'd been certain about himself begin to shift. He carried that unsettling with him out to the square the next day.

The next morning, I was cutting across the square toward the market.

An ordinary morning, no different from any other. People walked, carried water jars, opened their stalls. The plate announcing the days remaining hung as high as ever.

*My steps were slower than usual, that morning. The unsettling from the night before still hung in my legs. I learned, that day, for the first time, that there are days when even walking feels like an effort.*

Leaving the shed, I ran into Trope. She was on her way to fill her jar.

"You look a little better today," Trope said.

"…Do I?"

"Better than yesterday, at least." She shrugged. "I won't ask what happened. But looking better's a good thing, so."

I laughed a little at that. Only I knew that "looking better" wasn't quite accurate. I had only shaken through the night, and by morning grown a little used to the shaking.

*Trope's words felt strangely comforting to me that morning. Someone saying I looked better also meant someone had kept watching me. I found that gaze both a burden and, at the same time, a comfort.*

Trope went off her own way soon after. I kept walking, across the square, toward the market.

The square was slowly warming under the morning sun. I could feel the stone underfoot growing warm. The smell of baking bread drifted from somewhere, and people carrying yoke-poles of water passed by at a steady pace. An ordinary morning.

Then, on one side of the square, I saw people gathered. Not a few. More than twenty, it looked like. All fixed on one spot, their voices raised.

---

I went closer, curious what was happening.

A man stood at the center. Worn clothes, a small sack in his hand. People surrounded him, shouting.

"That man stole it!" a woman shouted. "I saw it with my own eyes!"

"I didn't steal anything," the man said. His voice trembled. "This is mine."

"Don't lie!" another man cut in. "That sack — I saw it at that stall earlier. Same exact shape."

The voices grew louder. Some people pointed fingers; some stepped closer, one foot at a time. The man clutched the sack tight against his chest and backed away. Dust rose faintly under his feet, and a few shoes scraped against the stone.

*I felt an unfamiliar emotion, watching that scene. I hadn't once seen people raise their voices like this since coming to this city. A quiet, precise, unhurried city. And now that quiet was completely broken.*

*I found myself thinking again, in that moment, about where this city's quiet came from. I'd assumed it was because of the Scales. Every action left a weight, so people were careful even about raising their voices, about getting angry. But what I was watching now was flipping that thought on its head. Even with the Scales, people could fall apart in an instant. Fear of weight was no match, in the end, for the urge to corner someone.*

---

"I didn't steal it," the man said again. Nearly in tears now. "This is my son's. I bought it for his birthday."

"Then why'd you try to run?" someone shouted. "If you didn't steal it, why were you trying to run?"

"Look at his eyes, that's the look of a thief!" another voice cut in. The man had no answer, just clutched the sack tighter.

"…I was afraid," the man said.

I froze at that answer. *I was afraid.* The exact same word I'd said in front of the Adjudicator, four days earlier.

*I felt a strange awareness in that moment. Was that man using that word as a shield, like me — or was he saying it because he really was afraid? I couldn't tell. Only, that word rang unusually loud that morning.*

The people seemed angrier at that answer. Someone shoved the man's shoulder. He staggered back.

---

I stood frozen there, not knowing what to do. No courage to step in, no heart to simply pass by. I just stood behind the crowd, watching.

*I realized, in that moment, that I was watching this scene the way three years of training had taught me. Not engaging emotion, only confirming facts. Whether this man had really stolen it, or was being unjustly cornered — I was trying to judge it the way the Scales would. But the Scales wasn't here, in this place. All that filled this space was people's voices alone. I felt, for the first time, how dangerous a vacuum that was. When people judge each other in a place with no Scales, there's no margin of error checked against anything at all.*

An old woman, at the edge of the crowd, waved her cane at the man.

"People like this are why our city's come to this!" the old woman shouted. "Days running out, and still stealing!"

A few people murmured in agreement. I found that strange. No one had yet actually proven this man had stolen anything. And yet people were already tying him to the whole city's sin.

Then, one person caught my eye.

A man standing at the edge of the crowd. Empty-handed. It was rare, in that square, to see someone standing with nothing in their hands. Most everyone carried a jar, a basket, a tool. He wasn't raising his voice like the others. Wasn't pointing fingers either. He simply stood there, quietly watching the man being cornered.

*I thought, the first time I saw him, that he was just another passive onlooker. This city had plenty of those. People who didn't involve themselves in others' business.*

But something was different.

---

The other onlookers had no anger, but they also had no expression — they looked uninterested. He was different. He was clearly watching the disturbance. Watching the man, the sack, the crowd closing in — watching all of it, one by one. But there was no anger on his face.

Not indifference. I sensed that difference dimly, in that moment. An indifferent person looks away. He didn't look away. He simply — wasn't angry.

*I looked at that face for a long time. Strangely, I couldn't take my eyes off it. Why, I couldn't explain then, and can't fully explain now. Only that it was the one face in that square of a different kind.*

His clothes weren't anything special. Ordinary city clothing, not much different from anyone else's. His age was hard to guess. At times he looked much older than me; other times, not old at all. I couldn't find a single distinguishing feature on that face. The only thing I could find was that expression — that stillness.

---

The disturbance didn't last long. An official heard the noise and came running.

"What's going on here!" the official shoved through the crowd.

Several people spoke at once. The official raised a hand for quiet.

"Hand over the sack," the official said to the man.

The man held out the sack with a trembling hand. The official opened it and checked inside. A small wooden toy was inside.

"…Where'd you get this?"

"I made it," the man said. "My son's birthday's the day after tomorrow."

The official looked a little more inside the sack, then turned to the stall owner standing nearby.

"Did this man take your goods?"

The stall owner glanced into the sack and shook his head.

"…No. All my goods are here. I must have been mistaken."

---

The crowd dispersed, one by one. No one apologized. The very people who had just been shouting walked off in their own directions, as if nothing had happened.

The man held the sack to his chest again and walked away with his head down. No one saw him off.

*I felt anger, watching that scene. Anger that people had cornered a man on no grounds at all, and anger that they'd scattered as if nothing had happened. I knew where that anger came from. I knew, better than most, how a baseless judgment can break a person. I had stood, for three years, on the side that delivered such judgments.*

*But that anger felt strangely familiar. I'd felt something like it somewhere before. In front of the Adjudicator, in the moment I myself had shaken, there'd been something similar there too. I couldn't say exactly what it was, at the time. I know a little more now. I was someone who got angry in front of injustice. Very precisely, too. Only, that anger always tilted in one direction. It burned hot in front of an injustice done to someone else, and went quiet in front of an injustice I myself was committing.*

I looked around for that man again — the one who hadn't gotten angry.

He was already walking toward the wronged man. Said a few words. I couldn't hear what. The distance was too far.

The man briefly lifted his head. Then walked on again. The one who hadn't gotten angry stood there a moment longer, then walked off on his own way.

---

*That was all I saw of him, that day. I didn't know his name, and had no expectation of seeing him again. I just thought he was a strange man. The one person, in this whole city, who didn't get angry where anger belonged.*

*I had no idea, then, who that man was. I know now. What I saw that day was one of the most important scenes in this entire story. And I didn't notice it at all. I simply walked past him. Only his face, strangely, stayed lodged in my mind a long while after.*

---

That evening, the old man brought up the disturbance, as if he'd already heard about it.

"There was some trouble in the square, I hear," the old man said, leaning on his cane. "A toymaker nearly got mobbed."

"…Did you see it?"

"No, I heard about it," the old man said. "Doesn't happen much in this city. Something's off in the air lately."

"…Off, how?"

"Fewer days left," the old man said, glancing toward the plate with his chin. That day's number hung low. "When people get anxious, they want to grab hold of someone, anyone, and get angry at them. I was the same at that age."

I listened, and called back that face from the square. The one who hadn't gotten angry.

"…There was one person there who didn't get angry," I said. I didn't quite know myself why I brought it up. "Everyone was shouting, but not him."

The old man looked at me for a moment.

"What did he look like?"

I tried to picture that face. Strangely vivid. But trying to put it into words, there was nothing particular about it at all. Common clothes, common build, common gait.

"…Just ordinary," I said. "But his face stays with me, strangely."

The old man nodded. An empty sort of nod.

"There are people like that in this city, now and then," the old man said. "People who don't get angry. I've seen a few myself. Why, I don't know."

"Why's it odd that someone doesn't get angry?" Trope broke in suddenly, from where she sat eating. She was there at the table that evening too.

"…Not that it's odd," the old man said. "That it's rare. In this city, someone who doesn't get angry is either someone who's never done wrong — or someone else."

"Someone else?" I asked.

The old man shrugged.

"That, I don't know either," he said, laughing. "The older you get, the more there is you don't know than do."

I laughed off that answer. At the time, I didn't take it deeply to heart.

*I didn't press further, hearing that. I thought, then, that it was just one of this city's ordinary oddities. I didn't see that man again for a while, after that day. But strangely, that face alone didn't fade, even as days passed.*

---

I turned back toward the market. Work was still waiting for me that day.

*I kept thinking of that face all through the day's work. I couldn't explain why he hadn't gotten angry. It wasn't cowardice. A cowardly person avoids the place entirely. He hadn't avoided it. He'd watched it through to the end. Only, no anger.*

*I'd never once seen that combination before. A face watching without fear, and yet not angry. I hadn't known that was possible. I'd lived thinking that not getting angry meant looking away. If you don't look, you don't get angry. He hadn't looked away, and still, no anger had risen in him.*

*I sketched that face over again, several times, that evening, in my mind. It didn't last. The day's other events kept overlapping it. The Adjudicator's words, Trope's laugh, the two sentences I'd found in the room of records. That night, before falling asleep, I called that face to mind one last time, and then, in the end, let it go. I wouldn't call it back until much later, in an entirely different place.*

*And three days later, I'd hear an entirely different piece of news, in front of that very plate in the square.*
