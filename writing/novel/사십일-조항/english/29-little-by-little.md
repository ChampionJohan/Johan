# Chapter 29. Little by Little

> _Previously_ — Pheugon said his family's story aloud to the
> Adjudicator for the first time. The Adjudicator gave neither
> comfort nor judgment — he just listened. That evening at the well,
> Trope said, briefly, "I've never thought of it as a weight I could
> set down." Pheugon let the words pass.

I noticed it first outside the bakery.

The baker had always been strict about keeping accounts. Trope had
told me so before. But that morning, I watched her send an old man
off without paying at all.

"…Pay me next time," she said. "Just take it today."

The old man bowed his head over and over as he left. The baker
watched him go for a moment, then went back to kneading dough. As if
nothing had happened.

I stood there and watched it. Something felt strange, though I
couldn't place what. Two months earlier, I'd have walked right past
that scene. A kindness from someone in the Reckoning — I wouldn't
have felt a thing. That day, my feet stayed rooted to the spot.

I passed by again the next day, half wondering if it had been a
coincidence. But that day too, the baker said the same thing to
someone else. "Pay me next time." Hearing it a second time, I
understood this wasn't chance — it was a decision.

*Until then I'd only known the people of this city as numbers on the
Scales. The baker had been a single line to me — "strict about
accounts." That day I saw, for the first time, that there was another
sentence behind that line.*

---

A few days later I saw it in the market.

The merchant who'd cheated with his weights — a name I recognized
from the precedent files. He was crouched in front of his stall,
working at something. Coming closer, I saw he was repairing his
weights, filling back in the part he'd shaved off.

"…What are you doing?" I asked.

He glanced at me, then went back to the work.

"…Setting it right."

"Who told you to?"

"No one told me," he said. "It's just been weighing on me lately."

I had no answer for that.

*In that moment I remembered the name I'd seen in the precedent
database. A weight, a disposal. A person who'd survived only as
numbers. But what stood in front of me now wasn't a number. It was a
pair of hands. The hands that had shaved the weights down were now
filling them back in.*

---

The habit of counting came back.

I'd once been the boy who counted a hundred and twelve cases. Now I
was counting something else. What wasn't stolen. What anger wasn't
raised. Words that got spoken. A few of these caught my eye every
day.

The girl next door, who picked up an old man's dropped cane and
carried it back to him. She'd lost her mother. Trope told me. Since
her mother's name had vanished from the Scales, that child hadn't
spoken to anyone. That day she'd grabbed the cane and run to the old
man.

A young man who'd stopped to help someone who'd tripped and dropped
her water jar. He'd always kept his eyes on his own path before —
I'd heard that at the well.

Two neighbors who had lived turned backs on each other, meeting
briefly in an alley one day and exchanging a short greeting.

*Counting these, I thought of the habit from training. That habit of
counting a hundred and twelve cases was, here, counting a completely
different set of numbers. Numbers the Scales had never once counted.*

---

One day I went and stood in front of a Scales terminal.

I checked the baker's name. A number came up. I compared it to the
record from forty days ago. No change. I checked the merchant too.
The same.

I stood there a long while, looking at it.

*I thought the Scales was missing something. It seemed strange that
a man now refilling weights with his own hands showed the exact same
number as when he'd been shaving them down. But thinking again, it
wasn't strange at all. That man hadn't undone the act of shaving the
weights. He hadn't erased the past — he had simply started living
differently, from now on. The Scales had no field to tell the two
apart.*

That evening I went and asked the Adjudicator.

"Why isn't the number moving? People are clearly changing."

The Adjudicator looked at me a moment before answering.

"Weight accumulates," he said. "What's already piled up doesn't go
down. Bread you stole stays on the Scales. Not stealing today doesn't
erase yesterday's theft."

"So no matter how much they change, it means nothing?"

"It doesn't mean nothing," he said. "It just doesn't register on the
number. The number and what it's worth aren't the same thing."

At the time, that distinction sounded strange to me.

"Then no matter how much this city changes," I asked, "if the number
doesn't drop below the limit within forty days, it just falls? Even
if people have genuinely changed?"

The Adjudicator looked at me a long time.

"That's exactly why the clause exists," he said. "There are things
numbers can never prove. The clause was made knowing that. Something
only a person can see — not a number."

"But that person is me."

"That's right," he said. "So I won't ask what you've been counting
lately. I'll just say one thing. The numbers you're counting might be
more accurate to you than the Scales' numbers."

I carried those words out of the room. Walking the hallway, I counted
things off on my fingers. The bakery, the weights, the cane, the
purse. Four. Counting to four, I felt strangely settled. Counting
still calmed me. Only now the things being counted had changed.

The pile of papers sat on the Adjudicator's desk that day too. I'd
never paid much attention to the way he sorted them, but that day I
noticed. A clerk had mentioned in passing that he used to push
papers aside as he went. Lately, though, the Adjudicator seemed to
linger over each page before turning to the next.

"Are you counting something too, Adjudicator?" I asked.

He stopped his hand.

"…Something like that," he said. "Forty years, I've read these
papers as numbers. These days I read the names. I can't explain the
difference myself yet."

I turned that over for a long time.

---

*I know now. That distinction was the one that decided this whole
city's fate. The Scales knew precisely how heavy the city was. But
which direction the city was walking, right now, appeared nowhere on
the Scales' screen. Direction wasn't a number. It couldn't become
one.*

Once I saw a strange scene in the square.

An older man stood in front of someone else, holding something out.
A small pouch. The one receiving it looked confused.

"…What's this."

"I owed you something," the old man said. "Seven years ago. You
probably don't remember. I do."

The recipient truly looked like he didn't remember. The old man
didn't explain, just pressed the pouch into his hand and turned away.

I asked someone passing by. "What was that about?"

"Don't know," the man shrugged. "There's been a few like that
lately. People suddenly going around paying off old debts."

*The Scales had already recorded that seven-year-old debt. Converted
into weight. That weight wouldn't vanish just because the man handed
over a pouch. I knew that, and still I couldn't stop thinking about
that scene for a long time. What the Scales couldn't erase, that man
was trying to erase anyway, knowing it wouldn't erase.*

---

Around that time I got to know one person. I won't write her name
here. She carried water for a living, and years earlier, the weight
piled up from theft had put her just one step short of the limit. I
saw her number once, by chance, at a Scales terminal. The room she
had left before crossing the limit was small enough to count on one
hand.

She hadn't stolen once, since. I learned that from Trope. Three
years, Trope said. And still the number sat exactly where it had
three years before. One wrong step away from crossing the limit.

"…So is she," I asked, "still in danger, even after three years of
not stealing?"

"Yes," Trope said. "That's how the Scales sees it. Three years of not
stealing isn't a number."

I couldn't say anything for a long while after that answer. That
three years of patience couldn't move one single number — that was
the first time it truly frightened me. What frightened me wasn't the
Scales' coldness. It was that the coldness was accurate.

More days passed. I kept counting. Some days five things, some days
two. Some days none at all. On those days I felt uneasy, wondering if
the city was slipping back.

*Around then I noticed the strange place I was standing. I had fled
this city hoping it would fall. And now I was falling asleep every
night counting the number of good things I'd seen that day. When
that shift happened, I can't name an exact date. I just opened my
eyes one day and found it had already happened.*

That fact unsettled me. Lying awake at night I'd ask myself: do you
want this city to live. The answer was different every time. Some
nights, yes. Some nights, no. The most frightening nights were the
ones when I couldn't answer at all.

*For three years I had cultivated exactly one feeling: that the
Reckoning deserved punishment. That feeling was simple, and because
it was simple, it was strong. But in the two months I'd spent in this
city, another feeling had begun growing alongside it. I carried both
feelings at once. Both were real. If one of them had been a lie, it
would have been easier.*

Once I learned the baker's name, hating this city as a single mass
grew harder and harder. But liking these people one by one didn't
mean I had forgiven the city they'd washed into being. I didn't know
how to hold the two apart.

The baker kept giving discounts on credit. Eventually she put up a
sign about it outright. The market merchant, once he'd fixed all his
own weights, started offering to check the neighboring stalls' as
well. The girl who'd picked up the cane now traded morning greetings
with that old man every day.

Small things. Small enough that the Scales would never once take
notice.

---

The number on the board announcing the days remaining kept ticking
down too. Thirty-nine, the first time I'd seen it. Now it was well
past twenty and falling. People still glanced at the number every
time they passed it. But their expressions had changed a little from
before. At first everyone had looked at that number with fear. Now,
sometimes — only sometimes — people looked at it with something more
like endurance.

*I had no way of explaining that difference to the Scales. Fear and
endurance use the same muscles in the face. The Scales doesn't
measure faces. At first I couldn't tell the two apart either. Only
seeing it day after day did it slowly become visible.*

I found myself slowing down every time I passed the bakery now. Same
at the market, same at the well. Two months ago I'd wanted to move
through these streets as fast as possible. Now my pace slowed on its
own.

*At the time I thought that change of pace was just habit. I know now.
It wasn't habit. It was attachment. I was growing fond of this city —
the city I had decided to hate.*

---

Trope had been quiet lately.

I noticed, but didn't press. Since my confession, I'd been busy
sorting through my own affairs. What she'd said at the well — "I've
never thought of it as a weight I could set down" — came back to me
more than once, but something else always interrupted before I could
ask her about it.

One evening I saw her standing at a Scales terminal. She hadn't
raised her hand — just stood staring at the screen. Different from
usual. Usually she raised her hand, checked the number, and turned
away at once.

That day she stood there a long time.

I started toward her to call out, then stopped. Her shoulders looked
stiff somehow. I felt something heavy coming off her back, but I
couldn't put it into words.

*I didn't call to her that day. Looking back, that was a mistake. At
the time, I believed it was kindness — leaving someone alone who
seemed to want to be alone. I'd made that same judgment three years
before, outside a kitchen door. I still hadn't learned, at that point,
that the judgment was wrong.*

I decided that I'd go find her the next day and ask.

That night, before I fell asleep, I thought back to the day she'd
first pulled me out of the water. "…You're not from here, are you?"
The face she'd made asking that. It had been almost two months since
then, and I realized how little I actually knew about her. Her
mother had been driven out. She didn't resent the Scales. That was
about all I had.

*I understood, that night. I had only just barely learned how to tell
my own story. I hadn't learned, at all, how to listen to someone
else's. I didn't know, then, that I would be learning it the very
next day.*

Through the window, the number on the board announcing the days
remaining glowed faintly in the dark. I watched it a long time before
closing my eyes.
