# Chapter 24. Another Herald

> _Previously_ — In the square, a man was cornered as a thief on no evidence at all. Everyone else was angry; one person alone was not. Pheugon didn't recognize him, and simply filed him away as a strange man.

Three days later, I was walking past the Scales office when I stopped.

Beside the main entrance, in the spot where notices always went up, a new sheet of paper hung. The paste hadn't fully dried, and the edges curled slightly. They fluttered whenever the wind blew. A few people had gathered in front of it, murmuring.

*I felt no particular unease, in that moment. Notices went up often in this city. Well maintenance, ration schedules, market rules. I was about to just walk on past.*

But my feet stopped. Among the murmuring voices, a familiar word reached me.

**HERALD.**

---

I moved in among the people.

The notice was short. The dry phrasing peculiar to a document produced by the Scales.

> Regarding the Reckoning's sentence of demolition. Due to the non-arrival of the previously assigned herald, a new herald has been assigned. The new herald has already departed for the Reckoning. Expected arrival date to be announced.

I read that twice, three times.

*My body went completely stiff, in that moment. The letters registered with my eyes, but it took time for the meaning to reach my mind. I'd spent these past days among this city's people — helping with errands at the Scales, reading notices aloud, sitting at the old man's table. All that while, somewhere out beyond this city, an entirely different course of events had already been underway.*

---

Someone standing nearby said, quietly.

"Finally coming, is it." An older man. "How long we've waited."

"Waited?" someone beside him asked.

"Of course," the man said. "Nothing's settled until the herald comes. Staying this way just gets more unsettling. Better it come fast and get the sentence read, whatever it is."

I felt something twist inside me, listening to that.

*Up to that point, I had gone on vaguely believing that my running had bought this city time. That if I didn't read, the Scales couldn't do anything. But this man was saying the exact opposite. My not reading, in itself, was heaping a different kind of unease onto this city. The unease of hanging, unresolved.*

---

The talk around me went on.

"Why'd it take so long to get assigned, though?" a woman asked. "Our days are down this much already."

"Guess it takes time to find someone, even for the Scales," someone else answered. "Not a job just anyone can do. Someone specially trained, they say."

"Trained, how? Isn't it just reading a paper?"

"You don't know," the first man cut in. "My nephew was training for that. You wouldn't believe how hard it is. Keeping your voice from shaking, never stopping. Years of practicing nothing but that."

"But what happened to the one who was supposed to come first?" the woman asked.

"Don't know," the man shrugged. "Lost, maybe, or couldn't make it. The Scales doesn't tell you that part."

"Poor thing. Must've had some reason."

I felt a chill run through me, listening. *Years of practicing nothing but that.* I was someone who'd gone through those years too. And yet here, in this city, people weren't talking about my years — they were talking about the years of a person whose face they didn't even know yet.

---

I stood in front of that notice for a long while.

*I felt something had gone wrong in my calculations, in that moment. I didn't yet know exactly what. I just realized, for the first time, that I'd been vaguely assuming the world would wait for me, while I hid here in this city. The world hadn't waited. The Scales had moved on without me. Found someone else, and already set them walking.*

I read the phrase "has already departed" on the notice again.

*Already.* That word weighed the most. I'd thought, all this while, that I was postponing something. Deferring a choice. But that word was saying the deferral was already over. Someone else was already walking. Toward this city.

---

I thought about going to find the record-keeper, then didn't. Instead I stood in front of the notice until the crowd dispersed.

*I imagined, standing there, that new herald. Someone around fourteen or fifteen, like me? Or someone older? I tried to picture the face and nothing came. I knew nothing about that person at all. And yet, strangely, I felt certain they'd be very much like me. Someone trained to read without feeling. Someone who never stops at a name. Someone who'd sat in the same room, at a different time, and heard the same words from the same supervisor.*

*I knew that person would read all the way down to that small line of print at the very bottom of the sentence — Article Four, Clause Eleven. Would that person, like me, stop at that line? Or just pass over it? I had no way of knowing the answer. I only knew, for certain, that they would read that sentence not knowing anything about this city at all.*

I went to step into the Scales building, then stopped.

I wanted to go find the Adjudicator. Wanted to ask him what this meant. But my feet wouldn't move.

*I felt a strange fear, in that moment. If I went to the Adjudicator, I felt he would finish the words he hadn't finished last time. "That, you'll come to know." I was afraid that "coming" had already arrived. I wasn't ready yet. I didn't even know what I was supposed to be ready for — I just felt unready.*

In the end, I didn't go into the Scales building. I turned and walked toward the square instead.

---

By the square, word of the notice had already spread. People stood in twos and threes, talking. Expressions varied. Some relieved, some anxious.

I stood in front of the plate announcing the days remaining. Looked at the number.

People kept passing by all around. Someone glanced up at the plate and moved on; someone didn't even look. How quickly this city's people had grown used to that number — that, oddly, struck me as strange that day.

That number looked different that day. I'd watched this number every day. But that day, for the first time, the fact that it had kept counting down without me felt frightening. I'd thought of this number as one I'd made. That by running, I was exercising some kind of power over this number. But now, this number was simply flowing along at its own pace. With nothing to do with me.

*I understood, in that moment, for the first time. My running hadn't stopped anything. I'd believed I was holding something back. Time for this city to turn on its own, the possibility of the clause being triggered, whatever it was. But the Scales, when I didn't come, simply sent someone else. Why had I not thought of that, for so long?*

---

I sat down at the edge of the square. The strength went out of my legs.

*For the first time, sitting there, I began to weigh what running away truly meant. I'd believed that running would stop something. Time, or the sentence, or the clause. But the reality in front of me was saying the opposite. I'd stopped nothing. I had only made one more person walk toward this city.*

*That person was, at this very moment, still walking toward this city. Not knowing anything, like me. No — unlike me. That person would surely come knowing exactly what they had to read. What only I hadn't known, what only I had missed by running — that person might already know it.*

I couldn't move for a long time, in front of that thought.

---

Toward sundown, I finally got myself up. On the way back to the shed, I stopped several times. My feet kept wanting to turn a different direction. Toward the Adjudicator's office, or toward the room of records. I corrected them, each time.

*That evening, I tried not to meet Trope. I didn't have the confidence to face her. I now knew there were two heralds hanging over this city. One already living among its people, blended in; the other, still faceless, walking toward it. And Trope knew about neither.*

But near the shed, in an alley, I ran into her anyway. She stood with an empty water jar.

"You haven't eaten," Trope said, setting the jar down. "What's with your face."

"…Nothing."

"How many times is that today?" Trope crossed her arms. "At least I know something happened today, from that look."

*I nearly told her everything, in that moment. That a new herald was coming. That I was the reason. The words rose all the way to my throat, and went back down again. I swallowed it that day too. But that swallowing was unusually heavy, that day. I knew, by then, that what I was swallowing wasn't just one name.*

"…Nothing happened," was all I said.

*I didn't bring up the notice to Trope that day. She didn't seem to know about it yet. She wasn't someone who kept a close eye on the Scales office's notices. That silence felt, for a moment, like a small reprieve. Until she heard that news, I felt I didn't have to fully face it myself yet.*

*Looking back now, that wasn't a reprieve — it was simply putting it off. She would hear that news soon enough. Not from me, but from someone else. And on that day, I would have to pretend, once again, not to know.*

Trope didn't press further. She picked up her jar and walked off toward her house. I watched her back for a long while, then returned to the shed.

Back at the shed, I wrapped myself in the blanket and sat for a long time.

*I turned one sentence over, the whole night. I had run away. But that running hadn't been enough. I didn't know yet, at the time, exactly what that "not enough" meant. I only felt, for the first time, that I was standing in some strange place — neither having left this city, nor properly belonging to it. I was one of the people who knew this city's sin most exactly. And yet I still hadn't properly looked that fact in the face.*

*I didn't sleep that night, for a long while. Through the window, far off, I could see the square's plate glowing faintly even in the dark. The number was still counting down. Without me. With nothing to do with me.*

*I didn't know then where this failure of arithmetic would take me. I only sat that night, for the first time, in front of the fact that everything I'd believed I was controlling had already slipped from my hands.*

*I had thought, when I ran, that it was one complete act. You run, and it's over. I believed nothing more needed to be done after that. That not coming to this city, not reading this sentence, would be enough on its own. But the world didn't take my running that way. The world didn't leave an empty space alone. Wherever a space opened, the world filled it with something else. I hadn't even known how quickly that filling would happen. A day, two days, was enough. While I hesitated, the world had already set the next person walking.*

*I felt helplessness, for the first time that night, in front of that fact — a different kind than anything I'd felt in front of the Scales, or in front of the Adjudicator.*

*I suddenly thought again, that night, of the face I'd seen a few days before in the square — the one who didn't get angry. Why that face came to mind, in that moment, I couldn't explain then, and still can't fully explain now. Only, I remember, that face had held a different kind of stillness than the helplessness I felt that night. I envied that stillness. And, at the same time, I couldn't understand it.*

That night, I took the sentence document back out of my bag, for the first time in a while. The oiled paper still bore the marks of that old wetting. Holding that paper, I imagined the day someone else would read this sentence in my place. That imagining came unusually vivid. A stranger's voice, reading this sentence in front of this city's people. In front of Trope. In front of the old man. I couldn't picture where I would be standing, in that scene.

*I folded the paper back up and put it in the bag. And that night, for the first time, I asked myself this question. Do I really want this city to fall? Or do I just want to not have to read this sentence? I didn't yet know, that night, how different those two questions were.*

*Looking back now, all of Part Two comes down, in the end, to one sentence. I was washed up in this city looking for a place to hide, and while hiding I became dyed into it, and dyed into it, I went looking for a way to bring the Scales down, and failed, and failing, I faced, for the first time, the price of having run.*

*I didn't know, that night, that the price wouldn't end there. The arithmetic would keep going wrong, and I would face that wrongness again, more than once.*

I counted again, that night, that I hadn't even reached the halfway point of the forty days. A little over twenty days left. I turned that number over in my mouth. Twenty days ago, that had seemed like infinite time. The days that remained felt far heavier than they had before.
