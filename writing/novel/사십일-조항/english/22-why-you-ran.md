# Chapter 22. Why You Ran

> _Previously_ — The Adjudicator summoned Pheugon for the first time. He already knew Pheugon was the herald who'd been assigned to this city and never arrived, but he didn't report him. "The herald ends up sitting in that chair, eventually." Pheugon was gripped by the thought that he might not be the only one who had fled.

For three days, I walked past the Adjudicator's office several times.

I didn't go in. I had no good reason to. Unless the Adjudicator called for me first, I'd have to make up my own reason to go to him.

*I couldn't explain to myself, those three days, why I kept walking that corridor. I already knew that in front of that one man, I didn't have to hide. And that knowledge kept pulling me back toward that door.*

On the fourth day, I finally knocked.

---

Over those three days, Trope noticed I was preoccupied with something.

"You've been strange lately," she said the second evening, carrying a water jar. "Talking less. Seem to keep drifting off somewhere."

"…That obvious?"

"You can see it," Trope said. "I've lived alone long enough — I can tell that kind of face on sight."

I had no answer to that.

"Well, you don't have to say if you don't want to," Trope said again. I'd lost count of how many times she'd said that by now.

*That phrase landed unusually heavily that day. In front of the Adjudicator I could set my name down; in front of Trope I was still wearing it. In those three days, moving between the two of them, I first truly felt how exhausting it is to carry two faces inside one person.*

On the third night, I finally decided. I would go back to the Adjudicator and hear whatever he hadn't finished saying last time. I didn't know myself what I would hear. Only that the fact that in that room alone I didn't have to hide kept pulling me back to that door.

---

"…Why are you back," the Adjudicator asked, lifting his eyes from his papers. Not a dismissive tone.

"…I had something to ask."

"Sit."

I sat. Less trembling than last time. Still, my palms sweated.

"What's your question."

I'd prepared a question in advance. But sitting there in front of him, an entirely different one came out.

"…Why did you run away," I asked him, instead.

The Adjudicator's eyebrows lifted slightly.

"That's what I want to ask you," he said.

I went stiff at that.

---

"…Are you asking me, right now?" I asked back.

"I'm asking," the Adjudicator said, crossing his arms. "Since you brought it up first, I'll ask you the same thing right back. Why did you run away."

The room went quiet.

*It wasn't that I hadn't expected that question. I'd known someday I would be asked it. But now that the moment had actually come, I realized I had no prepared answer left standing.*

"…I was afraid," I managed to say.

The Adjudicator looked at me.

For a long time.

The air in the room didn't move at all. Outside the window, laborers carrying something made a faint sound that faded away. The Adjudicator didn't even seem to blink. I wanted to look away from those eyes, but strangely, my feet stayed rooted to the spot.

---

The silence went on. In that stretch of time, I imagined several times what he might say. That he'd press harder. Or pretend to believe me. Neither happened.

*I have turned that silence over, countless times, in the days since. I learned, that day, for the first time, that there are different kinds of silence. Some silences are empty. Some are full. His was the latter.*

"You," he finally said, slowly. "That isn't the face of someone afraid."

I lost my breath at that. A cold feeling deep in my throat. Nothing physically wrong, and yet I'd forgotten, for a moment, how to breathe.

"…What do you mean by that."

"I've watched people a long time," the Adjudicator said. "A frightened person's eyes waver. The voice cracks, the hands don't know where to go. You didn't waver your eyes just now, saying that. Your hands stayed still too."

I looked down at my hands. He was right. They weren't shaking.

"…So what if they were," I said. My voice rose a little. "People are afraid in different ways."

"Could be," the Adjudicator conceded readily. "But I saw something in your eyes besides fear."

"…What."

The Adjudicator didn't answer. He just kept looking at me.

---

*I wavered, in that moment, for the first time. For three years, I'd had exactly one answer for myself, about why I'd run. Afraid. That answer wasn't one I'd made. It was an answer that had simply attached itself to my mouth, naturally, from the day after I ran. I'd used that answer, unquestioned, in front of Trope, and in front of myself. But the first time I spoke that answer aloud, in front of the Adjudicator, I felt for the first time how thin it sounded.*

"…I'm a fearful person," I went on. Defensively. "I was like that back at the training grounds too. Everyone said so."

"Said what."

"…That I never stop reading, no matter what. My supervisor said so."

The Adjudicator tilted his head slightly.

"Someone who never stops reading," he said. "Ran away out of fear?"

I couldn't answer.

*In that moment, for the first time, I saw the contradiction myself. For three years, I had never once stopped, reading. I wasn't someone who stopped from fear. And yet, faced with the single greatest proclamation of all, I hadn't even read it — I'd fled. How could those two things live inside the same person. I put that question to myself, for the first time, in that room.*

---

"…I don't know," I finally said. "Sometimes I don't know myself why I ran."

"A lie," the Adjudicator said quietly. Not an angry voice.

I looked at him.

"…What?"

"I said it's a lie," he said again. "You know. Yours isn't a face that doesn't know. It's only that you're not yet ready to say it aloud."

*I didn't know how to argue against that. I wanted to argue. But no words came to argue with. Because he was right. I already knew something, in that moment. I couldn't put it into an exact sentence, but I was certain that something sat there. I was straining every muscle in me not to open it.*

"…What do you think I know," I asked.

"I don't know that," he said. "Only you know that. I've only seen, on your face, the face of someone who already knows the answer."

The room went quiet again.

The Adjudicator nodded. He didn't press.

"It's all right," he said. "You don't have to know it right away. There were plenty of things I didn't know at first either."

"…Have you ever run away, Adjudicator?"

The Adjudicator laughed briefly. A bitter laugh.

"I've never run," he said. "Instead I've sat in this chair for more than thirty years, watching people who ran. I'm not even sure, myself, how different that is from running."

I had no answer to that.

"You," the Adjudicator asked again. "What did they teach you, in your training?"

"…How not to stop," I said. "How not to listen. How not to catch on a name."

"Why learn that?"

"…So I could read without feeling. So no name, no disposition, could shake me."

The Adjudicator nodded.

"But," he said. "Right now, in this room, you're shaking, in front of me. That reaction doesn't happen to someone untrained. You're someone very well trained. And yet a person like that — why did you fall apart, of all places, in front of this city?"

I found myself at a loss again, in front of that question.

"…I don't know," I said again. This time, truly not knowing.

"You might not know," the Adjudicator said. "I'm not demanding an answer from you today, either. I just don't want to watch you keep lying to yourself."

The Adjudicator glanced out the window for a moment. The plate in the square was visible. That day's number was one less than the day before.

"If there's one thing I've learned in this chair," he said, looking back at me. "It's that a person never knows why they ran, at the moment they run. They find out later, much later, in some entirely different place. And not even because they wanted to know — they come to know it whether they want to or not."

"…How do you know that, Adjudicator?"

"Because I've watched it three times," he said, curtly. "Three people fell apart in front of this very chair. All three only understood why, later. Only one of them had time enough to tell me."

My ears sharpened at that.

"…Who was it?"

The Adjudicator didn't answer. He only glanced toward the drawer for a moment, then looked back at me.

"That story," he said. "When it's time, I'll tell it. Not today."

---

"May I ask one more thing," I said.

"Ask."

"…What happens if I don't read it? This city."

The Adjudicator's expression stiffened slightly.

"You said last time that the Scales finds a way even without a person," I continued. "What does that mean?"

The Adjudicator was silent for a long while.

"Another herald comes," he finally said. "The Scales reassigns. That's the way."

"…When that person comes, do they read it in my place?"

"They do."

"Then what about me? Do I just— become as if I never happened?"

The Adjudicator looked at me for a long time.

"That," he said, "you'll come to know. That's as far as I can tell you today."

---

"…Thank you," was all I said, standing.

"Pheugon." The Adjudicator called my name.

I turned back, hand still on the door handle.

"That thing you said, that you ran because you were afraid," he said. "I don't believe it. But I know you want to believe it. How frightening that difference is — you'll come to know that too, someday."

I gave no answer and opened the door.

I left that room without being satisfied by that answer.

*That day, walking the corridor, I chewed on those words the whole way. "That isn't the face of someone afraid." That single sentence kept colliding inside me. I wanted to deny it. I tried to find grounds to deny it. I couldn't find any. I truly, in that moment, hadn't known myself what expression had been on my face.*

*Up to that point, for three years, I had believed I was a child who didn't get angry. I'd told myself the difference — not that anger didn't rise in me, but that I chose not to show it — and I'd believed I understood that difference. But in front of the Adjudicator, I saw the back side of that sentence for the first time. What if it wasn't that I didn't show anger, but that I was storing it somewhere. What if I hadn't run out of fear, but for some other reason.*

*I didn't manage, that day, to learn what that other reason was. I only felt, for the first time, that everything I'd believed I knew about myself wasn't certain at all. It was a strange sensation. Not the ground shaking — realizing the ground I'd been standing on had never been where I thought it was, to begin with.*

---

On the way home, I chose a long way around on purpose.

I didn't cross the square. I turned down several narrow alleys instead. I didn't want to run into anyone. Not Trope, not the old man, no one.

*Walking that road, I went back over my three years again. I was a child who never did wrong. Not that anger didn't rise, but a child who chose not to show anger. I'd been proud of that distinction. But one question from the Adjudicator had shaken the whole distinction. Not showing anger, and storing anger somewhere — those were different things. I might have mistaken the two for the same thing, all my life.*

*Walking, I remembered the day I first heard the name of the Reckoning. In the supervisor's office, receiving the document, laughing. I called that laugh relief. Silent, and very brief. That night too, I called that laugh back to mind again. And for the first time, I asked myself whether it had really been relief. If it was relief, relief from what. I left the alley without answering that question.*

By the time I reached the shed, night had already fallen deep. I sat wrapped in the blanket, leaning against the wall, and turned the Adjudicator's last words over again. *I just don't want to watch you keep lying to yourself.* I didn't know, at the time, why that sentence stayed with me so long. I know now. It was a kind of sentence I had never once heard before. Not scolding me, not comforting me — and seeing me, exactly, for what I was.

*I stayed inside that unsettling all night, before I fell asleep. I kept thinking of that third person. The one whose name the Adjudicator still hadn't given me. I tried to imagine where that person might have gone, what face they might be living with now. I couldn't quite picture it. I didn't yet know how much that person resembled me.*

*And the next day, still carrying that unsettling with me, I went out to the square — and saw, for the first time, a person who never got angry.*
