# Chapter 34. Five Days

> _Previously_ — The substitute herald arrived. Precise and trained,
> exactly as instructed, with no feeling in it. If he — who knew
> nothing of this city — read the sentence in Pheugon's place, the
> clause would never fire. For the first time, Pheugon felt the full
> weight of being the only person in the world who could save this
> city.

Four days ago it had been six.

This morning, the number on the square's board had changed to five.

I stood in front of it and looked at the number a long time. Five. I
counted it off on my fingers. Thumb, index, middle, ring, little.
When all five fingers folded down, that would be the day.

*I was still counting, even then. Counting wasn't so much a habit as
a method. I'd learned, three years ago, that turning something
frightening into a number made it a little more bearable. That was
why I'd counted a hundred and twelve cases too. Count people, and it's
heavy. Count numbers, and it's light.*

*I tried the same method that day. Five. Just a written number. Fi.
Ve. Two syllables. I even counted the syllables. It didn't help at
all.*

*This time was different. No matter how many times I folded it into
my fingers, the number five wouldn't get lighter.*

---

The habit of counting had started at age ten.

I only remembered that after washing up in this city. Our house had
eleven stairs. I used to count them, climbing up and down every
evening. I don't remember why I'd started. I just found myself doing
it, one day.

*I hadn't known, then, what I was afraid of. I know now. I was
counting how many people sat at our dinner table each night. Four. Then
one day three. Then one day one. I told myself I was counting stairs.
I was really counting that table.*

I never broke the habit, even in training. My supervisor called it
concentration. I know now it wasn't concentration. It was defense.
Numbers let me step slightly sideways out of the present. When the
present was too heavy, numbers became a handle.

*That morning, I reached for that handle again. Five. Five fingers.
This time the handle wouldn't catch. The number didn't let me step
sideways. Instead it set me squarely, facing forward, right in that
spot.*

---

I spent the whole day doing arithmetic.

The first calculation was easy. If I did nothing, that man would
stand in the square in five days. He would read the sentence document
from beginning to end. Precisely. Without wavering. And having read
all the way to Article 4, Clause 11, he would confirm nothing. He
didn't know this city. There was nothing for him to confirm.

The demolition would execute as scheduled.

A hundred and twenty thousand.

I remembered the moment I'd first heard that number, in the
supervisor's office. I'd thought then it was a number I'd never
counted. It was different now. Now I had faces held inside that
hundred and twenty thousand. The baker. The merchant fixing his
weights. The girl who'd picked up the cane. Trope.

*Once a face exists, a number stops being a number. I learned that
over forty days. I learned things I hadn't wanted to learn.*

The second calculation was more complicated. If I stepped forward.

I would stand in the square and read the sentence document. From
beginning to end. And having read the last line, I would have to say
this: "This city has turned." Then the clause would fire. The
demolition would be void. A hundred and twenty thousand would live.

But the one saying that sentence would be me.

I, who had spent three years living to hate this city, would have to
speak, with my own mouth, the final sentence that saved it.

*I ran that calculation over and over. The answer came out the same
every time. And still I couldn't believe the answer. That the answer
was correct, and that I could act on the answer, were different
matters.*

---

I went to see the Adjudicator that afternoon.

"…Adjudicator."

"Yes." He didn't look up from his papers.

"If I do nothing. If he reads it instead."

"Then it's demolished," he said. His voice was calm. "You already
know that."

"…So what am I supposed to do?"

The Adjudicator finally raised his head.

"I won't answer that question," he said. "I told you before. The
decision is yours. I'm not going to tell you to do it, or not to."

"…Why not." I asked. My voice cracked. "You've waited forty years for
this clause, Adjudicator. Surely you'd want to tell me to do it."

The Adjudicator closed his eyes briefly, then opened them.

"…That's true," he said quietly. "I've waited forty years. But that's
not a reason for me to tell you anything. The opposite, actually.
Because I've waited so long, I'm most afraid of my own wish getting
mixed into your decision."

I found that answer strangely honest.

"What you have to confirm," he said, "is whether this city has
turned. Not whether I want it to. If I persuade you into this, the
confirmation stops being a pure confirmation. It becomes a
confirmation with my wish mixed in."

"…What difference does that make? I'm the one making the decision
either way."

"It makes a difference," he said. "There's a difference between
saying what you yourself have seen, and saying something for
someone else's sake. The clause doesn't want the second. The clause
wants what you saw."

I turned that over for a long time.

---

That evening I stopped at the well. Trope was in her usual spot.

"…Why the face," she asked.

"…Doing arithmetic," I said.

"What kind?"

I wanted to tell her everything. The left column and the right
column. A hundred and twenty thousand against family. But the words
wouldn't come.

"…I think it would make it harder for you, if I told you," I finally
said.

She smiled a little at that. A sad smile.

"…I have arithmetic like that too," she said. "I haven't told you."

I looked at her.

"…What kind?"

"Later," she said, lifting her jar. "I don't have an answer yet
either."

*I let that pass, at the time. I was too deep inside my own
arithmetic. Looking back, she'd already handed me a hint that day. I
didn't take it.*

We didn't say much more and parted. As we did, she said her usual
line.

"…I'll be here tomorrow too."

Hearing it, I thought for the first time that it sounded like a
promise. I couldn't have said, then, why it sounded that way.

---

That night I started calculating again at home.

*I believed calculation would save me. It had, for three years. Weigh
the case, count the total, count the dates — do that, and somehow an
answer would come. That night, for the first time, I sat before a
problem that wouldn't resolve.*

I decided to write it down on paper. On the left, reasons to stay
still. On the right, reasons to step forward.

On the left, I wrote:

*Someone from this city wrecked our home. The Scales calculated it.
Precisely. And nothing came back. My brother didn't return. My mother
didn't return. My father still lives alone on the other side of the
city.*

*I survived three years on that one anger. If I step forward and save
this city, where do I put that anger down?*

On the right, I wrote:

*The baker. I still don't know her name. She held my hand with
flour-dusted fingers.*

*The merchant fixing his weights. No one told him to. He was setting
it right on his own.*

*Trope. Lost her mother. Doesn't resent the Scales. Believes she has
no standing to be forgiven, and still comes to the well every day.*

*A hundred and twenty thousand.*

I looked at that paper for a long time.

*The left column was one line. The right column had many lines. But
I knew that wasn't the answer. Having more lines didn't mean winning.
That one line on the left was a line I'd guarded three years to
protect. The lines on the right had formed in two months. Counted by
time, the left should win. But I knew that method of counting itself
was wrong.*

I tried calculating another way. In the hundred and twelve cases I'd
read, I'd never saved anyone. I'd only read. I'd only made audible a
verdict the Scales had already reached. The hundred and thirteenth
was different. The hundred and thirteenth, one sentence from my own
mouth could save a hundred and twenty thousand, or leave them to die.

*A hundred and twelve to one. I wrote that ratio on the paper, then
crossed it out. Numbers made no meaning here. Some arithmetic can't be
solved by ratio. That night I learned that a second time.*

I thought of my brother. The winter he left, I'd calculated how many
days it would be before he came back. Three days. A week. A month. I
counted that way and waited. He never came back. That calculation
still sat unanswered, three years on.

*That night I laid the two calculations side by side for the first
time. The calculation of when my brother would return, and the
calculation of when the Reckoning would fall. Both were numbers I
couldn't control. But one I was allowed to let go of, and the other I
wasn't. I wanted to know where that difference came from.*

---

That night I sat for a long while, studying my own hand.

*Through a hundred and twelve readings, this hand had never once
shaken. It shook for the first time in front of the Adjudicator. That
day I thought the shaking was the shaking of someone caught. Tonight
this hand was shaking for a different reason. I still hadn't found a
name for what kind of shaking it was.*

I thought again about the number five. Five days. A hundred and
twenty hours. Seven thousand two hundred minutes.

*I broke the time down finer, thinking it would feel a little less
frightening. It didn't. Seven thousand two hundred was a far bigger,
far more frightening number than five. That was the night I first
admitted that counting couldn't save me.*

I folded the paper and put it in the drawer. It wasn't a finished
calculation. It was just one I could no longer continue.

---

I lay down, and kept counting even lying there.

*That night I ran one more strange calculation. If I did nothing, I
would end up someone who had, at last, kept that one sentence I'd
guarded for three years. Just as the Reckoning had given me nothing
back, I too would give the Reckoning nothing back. That calculation
was clean. A debt not repaid. Just end it there.*

*But there was a strange hole in that calculation. Not repaying a
debt, and making the debtor's children repay it in your place, were
different things. One person from the Reckoning had wrecked our home.
I had something owed to me by that man. But if I didn't step forward,
the price would be paid not by that man, but by the baker, by the
girl who'd picked up the cane, by Trope. I only then understood I'd
never properly done this sum. I'd been pounding on the wrong debtor's
door.*

I sat up. The room was dark. A few stars showed through the window.

*I found myself wondering, in that moment, how the Scales would solve
this equation. How would it handle the fact that the one who sinned
and the one who pays are different people? But the answer was
obvious. The Scales doesn't ask that question at all. The Scales only
measures who crossed the limit. It doesn't measure who ends up buried
under that weight. That was never one of the Scales' fields.*

*If that was something a person had to do, then it occurred to me,
for the first time, that I might be the one who had to fill in that
field.*

---

I lay back down. The number five seemed to hang there on the ceiling.

*I counted it again. This time not the days — something else. The
people whose names I'd learned since coming to this city. The baker,
the weights merchant, the cane girl, the neighbor girl, Trope. Five.
Five days, five faces. That coincidence caught at me strangely. Each
face seemed to overlap, one for one, with each day remaining.*

*That night, before sleep, I spoke aloud to myself for the first
time. "What am I supposed to do." No one answered. But saying it
aloud, just that, made me feel like a slightly different person. I
remembered the three days I'd been trapped in the conduit, the first
night I'd spoken aloud there. I'd spoken to no one then too. And now,
the same.*

*Looking back, what I truly learned that night was this. Knowing I
was the only one who could save this city gave me no strength at
all. That fact only left me standing alone. Standing in a spot where
no one else in the world stood was not a proud spot. It was only a
frightening one.*

*I don't know what Jonah calculated, counting forty days before
Nineveh. But I know this now — some arithmetic doesn't end even after
the answer comes. That night, I fell asleep not even sure whether I'd
reached an answer at all.*

I must have opened and closed my fingers even in sleep. When I woke
the next morning, my right hand was still half curled. The fourth
finger had folded in on itself. As I straightened it, I thought:
yesterday's me and today's me were not the same person. The number was
still five. But the person holding that number had changed, a
little.

*The very thing the Scales can never read was, that night, happening
inside me. I hadn't reached an answer. But a direction was forming.
The Scales cannot tell the two apart. That night, for the first time,
I thought that I might come to know my own direction before the
Scales ever could.*
