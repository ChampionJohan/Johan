# Chapter 3. The Last Line

> _Previously_ — Pheugon retraces the rules of the world the Scales
> measures: that weight only ever adds, never subtracts; that one person in
> his own family carried an exceptional weight. Walking to the proclamation
> office, he stops for the first time before the city's totalizer board. He
> imagines the Reckoning must have one too, and opens the door.

There's an archive room on the third floor of the proclamation office. A herald can go there to check the full text of any case assigned to him. The ordinary individual cases I usually handled had no full text to speak of — a one-page summary was all there was.

A city case was different.

The clerk at the desk checked my papers and looked at me for a long moment.

"…The Reckoning case."

"…Yes."

"First time, with a city case."

"…Yes."

The clerk went into the back and came out with a thick bundle of paper. Much thicker than the one I'd brought in my bag.

"This is the full text. What you have is the summary. You'll read the summary on proclamation day, but you need to verify against the full text."

I took the bundle. It was heavier than I'd expected.

---

The reading room had no window. A desk, a chair, one light coming down from above. A soundless room. The whole archive was designed that way — a place where a person confirms, in silence, sentences the Scales has calculated.

I started from the first page.

The opening part was all things I already knew. City name, coordinates, jurisdiction, the date its Scales connection began. The Reckoning was an old city. Its Scales connection was old too. Over a hundred years.

The second part was the basis for calculating accumulated weight. This was where the unfamiliar content began. How the whole city's weight was calculated, how individual weights were summed, even the history of how the summing method itself had changed several times.

*Reading that part, I had a strange thought. That the people actually living in that city had no way of knowing the number produced by such a complicated calculation. They must have just looked at the board every morning. No one would have known what was being calculated behind it.*

The third part was the sentence itself. Demolition. Forty-day grace period. Method of execution. This part was nearly identical to the summary. Only the sentences were a little longer, a little more legal.

An appendix was attached at the back of the full text. A weight-calculation ledger. A table organized by household, with names all struck out, only numbers remaining. District 12, household 3, accumulated 47. That kind of thing.

I turned through that table for a long while. Pages passed and it didn't end. A hundred and twenty thousand lives strung out in numbers and figures. Beside some numbers the weight was very low. Beside others, very high. Between the low and the high, no explanation at all. They just sat side by side.

*Turning through that table, I thought of the man I'd seen in the market that morning — the one people had stepped back from because his number was high. I tried counting how many like him might be in this table, and stopped. I stopped because this time I couldn't count. I could count up to a hundred and twelve. This wasn't a number that could be counted.*

---

The door opened and the supervisor came in sometime in the middle of the afternoon.

"…Still reading."

"…Yes."

The supervisor glanced at the full text on the desk.

"Everyone's like that at first. A city case is a different volume."

I didn't answer, just kept reading. The supervisor stood there a moment, then asked.

"Anything catching on you."

*I could have asked, then. About Article 4, Clause 11. But in that moment I chose not to. I didn't know why, at the time. I know now. I was afraid that if I put the question to the supervisor, he would carry the answer away for himself instead of me. It had to be my question. Not yet knowing why, I still wanted to keep it.*

"…No." I said. "There's just a lot of it."

The supervisor nodded and left. The door closing sounded unusually loud in the quiet room.

---

I stopped partway through and found an item that wasn't in the summary. It was labeled "Precedent Reference."

A few case numbers were listed there. Numbers for other cities' cases. I didn't know what they meant. I thought about asking and decided not to. I didn't think it was information I needed right away.

*I learned later what that item was. The precedent database. The place the Scales draws on past verdicts to adjust a sentence. I passed those numbers by, that day. I had no idea I'd walk into that room myself, months later, and lay a finger on one of those numbers.*

I kept reading. The sentences were short and exact. The Scales's sentences always were. Almost no adjectives. When they appeared, they were only there to describe a figure.

Reading a long while, I noticed something strange. Not once, in this whole document, did the word "why" appear.

*The Scales doesn't use why. It uses what, how much, when. Never why. I thought, at the time, that was the Scales's honesty. That it doesn't invent reasons. I think differently now. The Scales doesn't leave out why because it's honest. The question of why is simply outside the Scales's range of calculation. It isn't hiding a reason. It has no idea what a reason is.*

---

The closer I got to the final pages, the quieter the room seemed to feel.

Execution clauses, appeal procedure — a clause that was, in practice, meaningless in a city case. There's no way for a hundred and twenty thousand people to file an appeal all at once. Then, transitional measures. Then, supplementary provisions.

At the end of the supplementary provisions, one paragraph was set in type one size smaller than the rest. I'd already seen it once at home. But in the full text it looked a little different. It wasn't only the smaller type — the letter spacing sat faintly out of line with the body text. As though the entire document had been completed, and this had been slipped in afterward, by someone.

**Article 4, Clause 11.**

> Should this city, within forty days, be confirmed to have turned, this sentence shall be void.

I looked for a footnote beside the line. Every other clause had one — what precedent it came from, when it was enacted, how many times it had been applied.

This clause had no footnote.

*Nothing at all. A blank. I stared at that blank for a long while. It was the first time I'd ever seen a blank space in a document the Scales had written. The Scales doesn't leave blanks. When it doesn't know, it writes "unknown." This clause didn't even say unknown. There was simply nothing.*

---

I went back to the desk and asked the clerk.

"…This clause has no footnote."

The clerk took the document and checked it.

"Ah, that one." the clerk said. "That's just how it is. This clause never gets a footnote."

"…Why not?"

"I don't know. I really don't. It's just always been that way."

I stood there a moment longer.

"…Does this clause come up often?"

"Only on whole-city cases. Never on individual ones. And whole-city cases themselves are so rare." The clerk shrugged. "This is only the second time I've seen one in the flesh, myself."

"…And the first?"

"About twenty years ago. When I was new here. The clause was exactly the same, back then. Nobody paid it any mind."

"…What happened, that case?"

The clerk's face went thoughtful for a moment.

"Demolished. That city. Ran out the forty days and was demolished. The clause never triggered."

"…Why not?"

"Who could say." the clerk said, straightening the papers. "Never confirmed, I'd guess. Whatever it took — confirmation, it never happened."

I held onto that answer. Never confirmed. What would have happened if it had been confirmed, nobody knew. It had never been tried.

"…Who was the herald, that time."

"Don't know that either. It's not in the record here. A herald's name doesn't stay on the document. It just says 'the herald.'"

*Hearing that, I felt a strange relief. I didn't know why, at the time. I know now. In that moment I first understood that no name would stay attached to the thing I was about to do. And that it didn't feel bad.*

*Nobody paid it any mind. I heard that and just nodded. I didn't understand, then, what that sentence was doing to me. That "a clause nobody bothers with" could be heard two ways at once — as a dead letter, or as a clause no one had ever tested — I wouldn't understand the difference until that very evening.*

---

I returned the full text and came down the stairs.

The stairs from the third floor to the first are long. A window at every landing, so light crossed my face once with every flight down.

*Coming down those stairs, I kept repeating one sentence to myself. "Should this city, within forty days, be confirmed to have turned." Confirmed. Confirmed by whom. I asked myself that question for the first time, right there.*

*And having asked it, I didn't try to find the answer. That was strange. I was usually the kind of person who couldn't let a curiosity go. But something held me back in front of that question. I know now. It wasn't that I wasn't curious. It was that I was afraid of knowing the answer. I was already, faintly, sensing that the answer might be about myself.*

There was a terminal of the Scales in the first-floor lobby. I just walked past it. The second time that day.

Outside, the sun was low. Afternoon. I'd been in that room all day.

Walking home, I lost my way twice on a route I'd walked a hundred times. A familiar street, and still my feet kept straying somewhere else. I learned that day, for the first time, that when your mind is somewhere else, your body goes there too.

An old man tending a stall at the mouth of an alley called out to me. Someone who usually just gave me a light nod in passing.

"…Herald, you're late tonight."

"…Yes."

"Caught a big one, they say."

I stopped walking. So the rumor was already going around.

"…Who told you."

"Rumor, is all. A whole city, wiped off the map." The old man shook his head. "Handing that to a boy. What a world."

I couldn't say anything. The old man didn't wait for an answer either — he went back to arranging his stall.

*Walking through that alley, I thought, for the first time, that no one else in this city knew the Reckoning's story as well as I did. I was the only one in this whole city who had read the full text. That table, with a hundred and twenty thousand lives written into it — no one else had seen it, but me.*

*That thought weighed on me strangely. It weighed on me, and it wasn't unwelcome. I should have found it more frightening, that the weight wasn't unwelcome. I didn't know that yet.*

*That night, back home, I opened the summary again. The same sentence I'd seen in the full text. But strangely, the summary felt heavier. In the full text, that clause had been one among hundreds. In the summary, there were only two paragraphs — the sentence, and the clause. With only two paragraphs, the weight split in half.*

*Demolition and void. Just those two, and nothing else. I didn't know, that night, that I would come to stand between them.*

I traced the last line of the summary again with my fingertip.

> This sentence shall be void.

I don't know how many times I read that sentence over. I didn't count.

*The second time that year I'd forgotten to count.*

---

That night I ate dinner with the paper set beside me. Eating alone, the sound of my own spoon rang unusually loud.

The table was built for four. Now only one seat gets used. The other three sat empty. I'd long grown used to seeing those empty seats. That night they felt strange to me again.

*Three empty seats — I counted them again that night, for no reason. Three. If the Reckoning was a hundred and twenty thousand, our house was three. I learned, that day, that the size of a number doesn't decide how heavy it feels. A hundred and twenty thousand and three both felt equally heavy, inside me.*

*No — that isn't quite right. To be honest about it — three was far heavier. A hundred and twenty thousand still had no face.*

I opened the paper again. I read Article 4, Clause 11 once more. This time without sound, just following the shape of the words with my mouth. A herald isn't allowed to read a sentence aloud before proclamation day. No rule forbade simply moving your mouth along with it. I used that gap.

*That night I mouthed that sentence some dozen times, without sound. No sound came out, but I already knew that was a kind of reading too. I hadn't broken the rule. But I knew, even then, that I'd broken its spirit.*

That night I stayed up unusually late. Through the window I watched the neighbors' lights go out one by one. This was a quiet city. No one made noise late at night. Everyone had learned to live quietly, to guard their own weight.

*I liked that quiet. I wondered if the Reckoning was a quiet city too, like this one. It wasn't. I already knew that. What kind of city the Reckoning was — I already knew.*

*Whether I had any right to say I knew that city was another matter entirely. I had never walked a single one of its streets. And yet I was certain I already knew what it was. That night I didn't ask myself where that certainty had come from.*

Before putting out the light, I folded the paper again. I pressed the crease once with my fingertip. By now the paper was worn from more folds and unfolds than I could count.

*That wear was, strangely, a comfort. It meant the paper had been worn soft by how long I'd stared at that sentence. Staring longer doesn't produce an answer. And still I kept staring.*
