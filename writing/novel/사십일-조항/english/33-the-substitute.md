# Chapter 33. The Substitute

> _Previously_ — Pheugon deliberately provoked the man who doesn't get
> angry. He never broke, and Pheugon broke instead. The Adjudicator
> said "there's a way to lose like that." Pheugon, for the first
> time, admitted the distance between his own endurance and the
> man's true absence of anger.

I heard the news in the Adjudicator's office.

"He's arrived," the Adjudicator said. He slid a paper across to me.

I looked at it. An arrival report. Name, herald office of origin,
date of arrival. I scanned the name. Unfamiliar.

"…Already?"

"He said a week," the Adjudicator said. "It's been exactly a week."

I found that precision strangely frightening.

---

"Where is he, now?"

"Herald office lodgings," the Adjudicator said. "He came to pay his
respects the moment he arrived. As procedure requires."

"You met him?"

"Yes."

"…What's he like?"

The Adjudicator thought a moment.

"He reminds me of you, three years ago," he said. "Precise. Steady.
Very well trained."

That sentence lodged in my chest.

"…Does he know about me? About the clause?"

"No," the Adjudicator said. "I didn't tell him. No reason to. He came
because he was assigned. A city-wide case. Reading the forty-day
grace sentence. That's all."

"…He doesn't even know about the clause?"

"The clause gets summarized onto the sentence document. He'll know
that much. But not the original text. I'm the only one who has it."

---

I wanted to meet him myself.

The next afternoon, I went to the herald office lodgings. I knocked
and the door opened right away. Like he'd been waiting.

"…Hello," I said.

He looked at me. He seemed a year or two older than me. His posture
was straight. He wore the herald office uniform, pressed sharp.

"…Who are you," he asked.

"…I'm Pheugon." I hesitated, then added, "I've been staying in this
city a while."

"Ah." He nodded. "Not from this city, either?"

"…No."

"Neither am I," he said. "Fellow sufferers, then."

I nearly laughed at that. I didn't.

---

He let me into the room. It was tidy. Almost no belongings. A few
sheets of paper lay squared off on the desk. I recognized one of them
as the Reckoning's sentence document.

"…Have you read it?" I asked.

"Of course," he said. "It's the document I'm going to proclaim."

"…How did it feel? Reading it."

He looked a little puzzled at the question.

"How did what feel?"

"…It's a hundred and twenty thousand people."

"That's right," he said. "The biggest case I've heard of. Among the
ones I've been assigned, anyway."

"…Aren't you scared?"

He thought about that for a moment.

"Fear and what I have to do are separate things," he said. "I read.
Precisely. That's my job."

*I felt like I'd heard that sentence somewhere before. It was almost
word for word what my supervisor had once told me. In that moment I
felt like I was standing face to face with myself, three years ago.*

---

"…How much do you know about this city?" I asked.

"As much as I need to," he said. "Population, accumulated weight, the
breakdown of violations. I've memorized all of it."

"…And the people? Have you met any?"

"Not many yet," he said. "I bought bread from the bakery, talked to
the lodging manager. That's about it."

"…Why not more?"

"No need to," he said. "I didn't come here to get close to the
people of this city. I came to read the sentence. On the fortieth
day."

I wanted to argue with that. I didn't know what to say.

*In that moment I wanted to tell him he was wrong. But he wasn't
wrong. That was exactly the way I'd been taught, three years before.
Don't meet people, so you don't stop reading. That was the core of
the training.*

---

"…Have you heard," I asked, carefully, "about the clause? The
Forty-Day Clause."

"I've heard of it," he said. "It's attached to the sentence document.
The clause that voids the sentence if the city is confirmed to have
turned within forty days."

"Do you know who does the confirming?"

"It says the one who read performs it," he said. Matter-of-fact. "If
I read it, I suppose it would be me."

I lost my breath for a moment.

"…How would you know if this city really turned?"

"I wouldn't," he said. Very honest. "I don't know this city. But
that's not my job. For the clause to fire, I'd need to confirm it,
and if I have no basis to confirm it, then I simply can't confirm it.
I just read, and judge things as I find them."

"…So it wouldn't fire."

"Probably not," he said. Not indifferent — just stating a fact. "If I
had some attachment to this city, I might see it differently. But I
have no reason to. This city, to me, is a number."

---

I stood there a long while after hearing that.

*In that moment I didn't think this man was bad. He'd done nothing
wrong. He was simply working precisely, exactly as he'd been trained.
The problem wasn't that. The problem was that the more precisely he
worked, the more certainly this city would die.*

"…If it's not too forward," he said, "may I ask why you're asking
about this."

I wasn't ready with an answer.

"…No reason," I said. "Just curious."

He seemed to find the answer strange, but didn't press.

"…How many days left, now?" he asked, straightening his papers.

I did the math. "…Six."

"Six," he repeated. Then briefly counted something off with a
finger. A finger counting dates. "Plenty of time to prepare, then."

"…Prepare?"

"Voice practice," he said. "A case this size means a long reading. I
need to decide in advance how many times I'll need water, mid-read."

I couldn't answer. He was already standing there, calculating how
many breaths he'd take in front of a hundred and twenty thousand
people.

He straightened his papers into a neat pile and saw me out. At the
door, he added one more line.

"…It was good to meet you, Pheugon."

I knew that greeting was sincere. That made it weigh on me more,
strangely.

---

As I left the lodgings, a familiar face was waiting at the bottom of
the stairs. The baker. She had something in her hand.

"…Herald, sir," she said. Her voice was careful. "Have this. You must
have come a long way."

What she held out was a loaf of bread, wrapped in cloth, still
steaming.

He took it. With both hands, at a precise angle.

"…Thank you," he said. "What's the cost?"

The baker's face stiffened slightly.

"…It's a gift."

"Ah." He paused a moment, then said, "Thank you. I'll pay next time."

The baker started to say something else, then just bowed her head and
turned away.

*I stood there and watched the scene for a long time. He hadn't been
rude. He'd been perfectly courteous. And yet something was missing. I
only understood, much later, what it was. He couldn't tell a gift
from a purchase. No — he could tell the difference. He just felt no
difference between them at all. To the Scales, both weighed the
same.*

*I had been like that too. Just like erasing what the merchant said,
that first proclamation. I hadn't known, then, what I was erasing.
What this man was failing to receive now — he probably didn't know
that either.*

---

I went to the room of records that afternoon.

I needed no excuse. The room was open to anyone. I stood hidden among
the shelves and watched him.

He was already there. Opening the precedent drawers one at a time,
copying something out. His fingertip moved along the pages at a
steady pace. It never stopped.

The scene felt familiar. Two months ago, I too had sat in that same
seat, opening those same drawers. Until I found Mena's record, I'd
sat there for days.

*I'd been searching for something, then. A crack that could bring the
Scales down. What was he searching for now. I was curious. But I
didn't go over and ask. Asking would mean explaining why I was even
here.*

He stayed in that seat over an hour. I watched him reach Mena's
entry. His fingertip stopped on that page. Just for an instant.

I held my breath in that moment. Afraid he'd notice — the misquoted
sentence, the wrong word, "never repented." I wanted him to catch on
it. No — I didn't want that. I didn't even know what I wanted.

He turned the page.

He didn't stop.

*In that moment I understood. He wasn't the kind of person who'd
catch on that sentence, the way I had. I only realized then that I'd
caught it because I'd lived two months in this city. No one catches
it from the words alone. You have to have lived it to catch it.*

I stood there until he'd finished tidying the drawers and left the
room. He never saw me.

---

That evening I found Trope at the well.

I sat beside her as she drew water and brought it up.

"…I met the substitute herald."

Trope's hand stopped.

"…What's he like?"

I thought for a moment how to explain it.

"…He's like me," I finally said. "Me, three years ago."

"What does that mean?"

"He doesn't know this city," I said. "Doesn't even try to. He just
sees numbers. Population, weight, violation breakdown. He's memorized
all of it. But he doesn't know the baker's name. Doesn't know yours,
either."

Trope heard that and looked down at the water jar.

"…If he reads it," she asked, "the clause won't fire. Right?"

I couldn't answer. There was no need to. She'd already known the
answer when she asked.

"…Six days left, they're saying." She said. I was surprised she
already knew that too. "The baker's wife told me today at noon."

*Word travels faster than I do. I hadn't known that.*

We didn't say anything for a long while. Evening light rippled on the
water in the well.

"…Pheugon," she finally said. "Not about him — can I ask about you?"

"…What."

"Are you going to read it?"

I couldn't answer right away.

"…I don't know," I said, after a long while. It wasn't a lie. I
truly didn't know.

Trope nodded. She didn't ask further. It wasn't like her to hold back.
Usually she'd have pressed once more. This time she didn't.

She filled her jar and stood. Before turning to go, she looked at me
once more. She started to say something, then didn't.

I watched her back a long time. That evening, strangely, the water
jar on her shoulder looked unusually heavy.

---

Walking home, I kept thinking of that room.

*The tidy room, the squared-off papers, the unwavering voice. That
was the shape I'd built myself over three years. I'd been proud of
that shape. Facing it now like a mirror, I felt for the first time
how dangerous a shape it was.*

*He doesn't hate this city. Doesn't love it either. He simply doesn't
know it. And that not-knowing was, with perfect precision, blocking
off the only chance this city had to live. I understood that day,
for the first time, that there was something more frightening than
hatred. It wasn't indifference. It was trained ignorance, precisely
made.*

*Six days.*

I counted the number off on my fingers. In six days two people would
stand in the square. Me, who hated this city. And him, who didn't
know it.

*I understood precisely, for the first time, that night. That I was
the only person in the world who could save this city. That fact
didn't excite me. It crushed me.*

Before sleep, I thought again of the papers laid out in his room. The
squared corners, the unwavering handwriting. I too had lived like
that for three years. Keeping the papers straight. Never letting
feeling mix into the paperwork. I'd been taught that was precision.

*That night, for the first time, I thought about how that precision
split into two. One was the Scales' precision. The precision of
measuring weight, the precision of measuring the past. The other was
a person's precision. When a person tries to be precise, all he can
do is imitate the Scales. For three years, I'd been doing that
imitation. He was doing it now too. The only difference was that I
now knew what that imitation erased.*

I lay awake a long time that night. Through the window I could see
the lights of the herald office lodgings. A light that seemed like it
would come on at the exact hour and go off at the exact hour.

*Watching that light, I thought of my own room, three years ago. I
used to light my lamp like that too. At the exact hour. And put it
out at the exact hour. At the time I thought that was diligence. I
know now. It was fear. If I left an hour unaccounted for, I was
afraid something would slip in through it.*

*That friend was probably living inside that same fear right now,
without knowing it. I knew it wasn't his fault. And still, standing
in front of the fact that his fear could end this whole city, there
was nothing I could do about it.*
