# Chapter 15. Before the Scales

> _Previously_ — The number "39" hung everywhere across the Reckoning. People lived beneath it, and still no one fled. Pheugon asked why, and slowly began to sense that the number had a hundred and twenty thousand faces. Trope took him along to help with errands at the Scales.

The Reckoning's Scales was nothing like the one I knew.

In the city I came from, there were six terminals. Three in the square, two at the market entrance, one in the herald office corridor. Small, quiet machines — you raised your hand, and a number appeared.

The Reckoning's Scales was the entire square.

---

Trope led me to the middle of the city, an open plaza with clear sky on every side. In the center stood a huge plate, facing the sky. As tall as three or four people stacked together. Beneath it, a long line stretched out.

"This is the main office," Trope said. "Everyone comes here to check. The small plates around the neighborhoods are just notices — the real checking happens here."

Everyone in line held their wrist out in front of them. Something was fastened to each wrist. The tag Trope had mentioned the day before.

Up close, it was a thin metal band. A small groove on the inside. When their turn came, people touched that groove to a stand beneath the plate. A moment later, a number appeared on the plate, above, in the position corresponding to that person.

*I found the method strangely elaborate. Where I came from, raising your hand made a number appear on a small screen only you could see. Quiet. Not here. The Reckoning put a person's weight up on a plate the whole square could see, right above that person's head.*

*That was this city's way. Weight wasn't a private matter here. It was public. I saw that difference for the first time that day, and it would be a long while before I understood what it meant.*

The line moved forward little by little. The turn came for the young man standing ahead of me. He touched his wrist to the stand.

A number appeared on the plate, at his spot. It must have risen quite a bit from the day before. His shoulders visibly stiffened.

A few people nearby glanced at the number. No one stared outright, but he clearly knew they'd all seen it. He bowed his head and hurried away.

*I saw this city's cruelty for the first time in that scene. It wasn't the Scales that was cruel. The Scales only produced a number. What was cruel was where that number hung. Where I came from, you saw your own number alone. Even if you'd done something shameful, that shame stayed inside you and ended there. Here, that shame hung before the eyes of the entire square.*

*That day, I thought this the more honest way. If you have nothing to hide, there's nothing to fear. How naive that thought was, I would only understand later.*

---

Trope didn't join the line — she turned off to the side. There was a small table there, and an old man sitting behind it. He seemed unwell; two canes stood propped beside him.

"You came," the man said, smiling at Trope.

"I'll do it for you again today," Trope said.

Trope carefully lifted the man's wrist and touched it to the stand. He didn't seem able to raise his hand that high on his own. A moment later, a number appeared on the plate. He looked up at it and nodded.

"Same as before," he said. "Good."

"It's not good, it's just what should happen," Trope said. "You're not doing anything these days anyway, Grandfather."

"Doing nothing is the good thing, then," he said, laughing.

Trope set down a water bottle beside him and asked briefly how he was doing. The man seemed to know her well. Like people who'd known each other a long time.

*In that short exchange, I began to see a little of who Trope was. A girl who rationed her words about other people's business unhesitatingly lifted the hand of an old man who couldn't move well. I hadn't yet managed to connect those two as the same person.*

Standing there awkwardly, the old man looked up at me and asked.

"Who's this?"

"Had," Trope answered for me. "He's agreed to help for a few days."

"Outsider," the man said, glancing at my wrist. "No tag."

"…Yes."

"Tags get fitted at birth," the man said. He explained without being asked — old people sometimes do that. "Can't take it off. There's no way to take it off. You wear it until you die. Proof you belong to this city."

"…And after you die?"

"Even I don't know that," he laughed. "Haven't died yet."

Trope laughed a little beside him. I found the laugh unfamiliar. It was the softest expression I'd seen on her face so far.

*I would remember that laugh for a long time. I'd see it a few more times after that, but each time it surprised me. Probably because I'd already noticed, that first time, how rare it was.*

---

That wasn't all the table work involved. Trope helped two more people there. One a woman who couldn't see well, another a young woman carrying a small child on her back. The woman with the child had no free hands, so Trope touched her wrist to the groove for her.

"You help like this every day?" I asked.

"Not every day," Trope said. "Whenever someone needs it."

"…Do you get paid?"

Trope looked at me as if I'd said something strange.

"No," Trope said. "Should I?"

I couldn't answer. Where I came from, errands got paid in coin as a matter of course. This city seemed to run on a different rule.

When the work was done, Trope touched her own wrist to the stand.

A number appeared on the plate.

**4.**

A low number. Very low. I felt a small jolt of surprise seeing it. I'd vaguely assumed that someone Trope's age would have accumulated more weight by now.

Trope looked at the number with no expression at all. As if she saw it every day — which, indeed, she did.

"…That's low," I said.

"It should be," Trope said, lowering her wrist. "Living low is the goal, isn't it."

*I took that as obvious at the time. In a city with a Scales, everyone lives that way — low, cautious. So did I. But something else was mixed into Trope's answer. I couldn't put my finger on it then. I learned later. For Trope, a low number wasn't a goal. It was proof. Proof that she was different from her mother.*

---

Trope looked at me this time.

"Had — aren't you going to check?"

I should have anticipated that question, but I hadn't.

"…I don't have a tag here," I said, raising my wrist. Bare skin, nothing on it.

"You can do it temporarily," Trope said, pointing toward the table. "Outsiders can check too. You just write down your name and where you're from."

I stiffened.

*I was afraid of two things at once, in that moment. One was having to write a name. If I wrote the name Had into this city's official record, there was even the smallest chance that record could later be cross-checked against my real name. The other was simpler. I didn't want to see my own weight. There had been a reason I'd suddenly stopped my three years of daily checking. I wasn't yet ready to face what I was doing, myself.*

"…It's fine," I said. "I won't."

"Why?"

"…No reason."

Trope looked at me for a moment. A look similar to the one from the day before, when I'd said I didn't know what a Scales terminal was. But this time, she held it a little longer.

"Strange," Trope said. "Everyone's curious."

"…I'm not curious."

"That's exactly what's strange." Trope didn't press further. She just left that remark and moved on to the next person.

*I've come back to that moment many times since. Trope didn't start suspecting me that day. I know now. That day, Trope had simply met, for the first time, someone who didn't want to see their own weight. The people of this city aren't afraid to see their own numbers. Low, and they're relieved; high, and they're careful. Nobody wants not to see it. I was, for the first time in that city, someone who didn't dare face their own number.*

Even after Trope moved to the next person, I stood by that table for a long while. The stand was right in front of me. Close enough to reach.

*In that moment I felt my hand trying, on its own, to rise. Three years of the same motion, every day. My body remembered the rule. I watched that hand and forced myself to hold it back. It felt like holding down someone else's hand.*

*What I was really afraid of, I didn't know exactly, then. I know now. I wasn't afraid the number would be high. I was afraid it would be low. That the number of someone who'd fled — someone who had decided to abandon an entire city — might still read low. That even the Scales wouldn't notice that.*

---

We left the square and walked. The sun had passed midday.

"You come here every day?" I asked.

"Almost," Trope said. "Grandfather and that woman who can't see well — there's no one else to help them."

"…Isn't it hard?"

Trope glanced back at me.

"What's hard about it?" she asked back. Not mocking — genuinely curious.

I couldn't answer.

"It's just something I do every day," Trope said, facing forward again. "You don't think about hard or not hard before you do it. You just do it. If I don't, nobody looks after their numbers."

"…Why does that matter? What changes if their number isn't seen?"

Trope stopped walking.

"If a number suddenly jumps," Trope said. "You know what that means? Someone did something to that person. Robbed them, or cheated them, or hurt them. If you don't watch a number every day, that kind of thing can happen and pass right by, unnoticed. That's what scares me. Something passing by, unnoticed."

I had no answer to that.

*It was the first time I considered that the Scales might not simply be a tool of punishment. For Trope, that number wasn't a judgment — it was a signal. A signal telling her that something bad had happened to someone. I'd never once held that view before. To me, a number had always been the size of a sin. To Trope, it was sometimes a trace of what someone else had done to a person.*

"Who's your grandfather?" I asked, trying to turn the subject a little.

"Neighbor," Trope said. "I've known him since I was small."

"…And your family?"

Trope's pace slowed for a moment.

"None," Trope said. "It's just me."

I couldn't ask further. I felt there was something inside that short answer, but I didn't know whether I had any right to ask what it was.

*I thought about that answer for a long time that night. How light the tone was in which "it's just me" came out. Someone who tells a heavy story in a light tone has already told that story hundreds of times before. I didn't know that then. I'd learn it a few days later.*

Trope walked me back to the shed. Before we parted, she suddenly asked.

"Had's not your real name, is it?"

I thought my heart would stop.

"…Why do you say that?"

"You're always half a beat late answering to it," Trope said. "Nobody's ever late answering to their own name."

I said nothing.

"It's fine," Trope said. "You don't have to say. I've got things I'm not saying either."

Then she turned and walked away. I watched her back for a long time.

*I should have understood, in that moment, that Trope had already seen right through me. But that day, I only felt relief. Relief at not being caught. That I'd already been caught, in all but name, I couldn't see that yet.*

Alone in front of the shed, I looked back once toward the square. In the distance, that huge plate was visible. Everyone's numbers hung there, facing the sky. The sun was setting above it.

*Looking at that plate, I thought: this city doesn't know my number. However long I might live here, whatever I might do, there's no spot for me on that plate. I thought that was freedom. I know now. It wasn't freedom — it was absence. I was the one person in this city who was inside it but didn't belong to it. How dangerous that position was, I didn't know then.*

*Having no number on the plate, and having no name on the plate, were different things. I was free of the number, but not of the name. Somewhere, on the herald roster, I was still written down under my exact name. Pheugon. Assigned to the Reckoning. That name was waiting for me, unknown to this city's Scales, somewhere outside this city.*

*I pushed that thought away as I closed the shed door. I believed, then, that this was possible. That in a few days, or a few weeks, that name and that place would have nothing more to do with this city. I believed I wasn't hiding — I was only waiting. Without being able to say exactly what I was waiting for.*

*I didn't check my weight in that square that day. I didn't check it for a long while after, either. Trope was the only one who found that strange, and that it happened to be her who noticed the strangeness — looking back — was no accident.*
