# Chapter 7. The Conduit

> _Previously_ — The night before leaving for the Reckoning, Pheugon packed
> in secret and left the house without telling anyone. He turned away from
> his father's door without a goodbye, and passed the city gate wearing a
> herald's blank, trained face. At the end of the old riverside road, he
> climbed down into an abandoned well. He heard the plank settle back over
> the opening above him, and stepped into the dark.

There were twenty-two iron rungs.

I counted them going down. There was nothing else to do but count. My foot touched water on the twenty-second rung.

Cold. Far colder than I'd expected.

*I've never forgotten that temperature. My body lodged its first protest right then. My mind had made its decision long before. My feet were only just then realizing what was happening.*

---

The floor wasn't level. The water came to my ankles, then to my knees, then back to my ankles. I felt my way along the wall as I walked.

The wall was stone. Smooth. Worked by hands, for a long time. Not a natural cave. Someone had made this. A very long time ago.

*Feeling that wall, I wondered, for the first time, who had dug it. Dug to carry water, presumably — but then why dig it wide enough for a person to walk through? Too precise a width to be coincidence. I never got an answer to that question, walking this road for a long while after.*

The tunnel didn't run straight. It curved gently, then bent sharply, and sometimes split into two. I always chose whichever branch had the stronger current. No real reason. I just thought the side the water ran harder must lead somewhere.

The ceiling height wasn't constant either. Some stretches I had to bend at the waist to pass through; others my hand couldn't reach even stretched all the way up. I wondered what the difference meant. Had the stretches meant to carry more water been dug wide, and the ones meant to carry less, narrow? Or had it simply followed the shape of the ground?

*I made those questions in the dark, alone, and folded them back up, alone. No one there to answer. That was the first time I learned, in my body, that making a question and getting an answer might have nothing to do with each other. Until then I'd lived believing that a question, once asked, brought an answer along with it. That had always been true at the training hall. Ask, and the supervisor answered. No one was here.*

---

The sound was strange.

Water sounded from everywhere. But I couldn't tell the direction. It seemed to come from behind, then from ahead. The sound bouncing off the walls mixed with the sound of the actual current.

*I understood, for the first time, that in this dark, my ears grew just as useless as my eyes. I thought of the room at the training hall, where sound came back to me. At least that had one wall. Here the walls were on every side, at every angle. I couldn't even count how many times a sound bounced before it returned.*

I never let my hand off the wall. The moment I did, I felt I'd lose my direction entirely.

The wall sometimes had grooves cut into it. Feeling along with my fingertips, I found lines of even depth. At first I took them for cracks in the rock. But the spacing was too regular. Someone had cut them on purpose.

*I only learned later that those grooves were markers. Left by people who traveled this conduit often, so as not to lose their way. I didn't know how to read them, then. They weren't letters. It was a language read only with the fingertips, and I had never learned that language. I only understood, much later, that the answer had already been carved into the path I was walking.*

I walked a long while. There was no way to measure time. No sun, so I couldn't tell day from night either. I just counted steps. A hundred, two hundred, and at some point I stopped counting that too.

*I don't remember exactly the moment I stopped counting. That's strange. I was someone who counted everything countable. A hundred and twelve sentences, a hundred and eleven erased words. But in that dark, counting lost its meaning. The numbers weren't taking me anywhere.*

---

At one point I thought I heard another sound.

Footsteps. Not mine.

I stopped. Held my breath. Nothing but the sound of water.

*I still can't be sure, even now, whether that was real. This conduit is said to connect several cities. It wouldn't have been strange for someone besides me to be in it. Someone wanting to leave a city too. The senior had said as much. If so, whoever's footsteps those were, they were here for the same reason I was. I didn't call out to that person. That person didn't call out to me. In the dark, people learn on their own, somehow, how to pretend not to notice each other.*

I felt that presence a few more times after that. The water rippling in a different rhythm for a moment, or something knocking, far off. I never once confirmed it. I never once walked toward the sound. I never crossed paths with anyone, the whole time. Maybe that too was one of this road's rules. A rule the water arranged by itself, so that no two people would ever meet.

*At the time I thought that was fortunate. I wouldn't have known what to say if I'd met someone. Now I think differently. If someone else had been in that dark besides me, that person probably had no way to speak to anyone either. We were both avoiding each other in the same water.*

The footsteps didn't come again. I walked on.

---

I was hungry.

I took out the dried bread and ate it. Tore it with wet hands, chewed in the dark. I couldn't taste anything at all. Just the sensation of it going down my throat.

*I had a strange thought, then. What were the people of the Reckoning eating right now. I knew the forty days hadn't even begun for them yet. They must be eating dinner right now, not knowing whether I would come, or wouldn't. I let that thought go before it went further. I didn't want to picture that faceless hundred and twenty thousand again.*

I finished the bread and opened my water flask. There was water enough all around me. I found that funny. I wasn't rationing the flask because I had no water to drink — I was rationing it because I had no way of knowing whether this water around me was safe to drink.

When the flask was down to half, I sealed it again. I had to save it. I didn't know how many days this would take.

*It was then I began, for the first time, to calculate. Half a flask left. Five loaves of bread remaining. How much to use per day, to hold out how many days. I realized, then, that this calculation calmed me. Numbers were the one thing that answered me, in that dark. Ask, and there was always, without fail, one answer.*

---

I only learned there was such a thing as a sense of direction after I lost it.

Above ground, you just know where you are — by the sun's position, the wind's direction, the shape of a familiar building. Down here there was none of that. Every wall looked the same. The water ran every direction at once. I couldn't even tell whether I was getting closer to the Reckoning or farther away.

*I thought, that day, that this was frightening. I think differently now. I hadn't lost my direction. I had never had one to begin with. I entered this dark with no plan for where to go once I'd avoided the Reckoning — only the thought that I would go somewhere that wasn't the Reckoning. For someone with no direction, losing a sense of direction is only pretending to lose something that was never there.*

Another fork came up. This time I picked left, with no certainty either way, flipping a coin in my head.

*I've gone back over that choice many times since. I wrote that there was no reason for picking left. Was that really true? Thinking about it now, I think I noticed, without quite realizing it, that the water sounded a little louder on the left. That a stronger current would lead somewhere bigger, I must have guessed, somewhere in myself. If so, it wasn't a choice with no reason at all. I was calculating even then. Only not clearly enough to call it calculation.*

An hour, maybe, or two. Time had already lost its meaning. I felt my legs give out. Walking through water was many times harder than walking on land. The water gripped my legs and wouldn't let go.

Once my foot slipped and I went in to the knee. My bag nearly got soaked. I just barely caught it and hauled it up.

*In that moment I made a sound for the first time. Not a scream. Just a sharp intake of breath. Hearing it bounce off every wall and come back, I felt, strangely, reassured. At least I'd confirmed this dark wasn't entirely empty. My own sound, at least, came back.*

---

The water kept getting deeper.

From ankle to shin, from shin toward knee. I lifted the bag above my head. The wax envelope couldn't get wet. Whatever was inside it, it still had to be mine.

*Writing this sentence, I ask myself. Why hold onto it so carefully. It would have been so much easier just to let it go in the water. I think I believed, then, that protecting that document meant protecting my own rightness. As long as I held it, I wasn't a fugitive — only "holding" it. I told myself that lie.*

The fingertips resting on the wall began to ache with cold. I didn't know yet that long enough in water does that to you. My body was cooling, and I only registered it as cold.

---

Ahead, the sound of the water changed.

Different from anything I'd heard so far. Louder, and more regular. Like something large blocking the water.

I slowed my steps.

Very faint light began to show. Not total darkness — something leaking in from somewhere up ahead. I walked toward it.

*Seeing that light, I felt relieved. Looking back now, I laugh at how simple that was. Light didn't mean safe. I didn't know that yet.*

The tunnel widened. The ceiling rose, and instead of walls, thick stone pillars stood at intervals. Something like a way station. The current split here and ran in several directions. Somewhere above, a small crack let in a thread of faint light.

I stood beneath that light for a moment. Looked up. The crack was no wider than a finger. And yet the light coming through that narrow crack felt brighter than any darkness I'd walked through so far. My eyes stung.

*I stood looking up at that shaft of light for a long time. Who might be passing above it, right now. Does whoever's standing on the ground just above that crack know there's a road like this beneath their feet. I let time pass on thoughts like that. It had been a while since I'd had anything to do besides walk.*

And at the end of that station, a large iron structure stood blocking the waterway.

*A sluice gate. I didn't know, then, that's what it was called. I just thought of it as a large door of some kind. It stood open. Water flowed freely beneath it. Higher up on the door was an iron wheel with a handle. I could only guess, then, what it opened and closed.*

I stood in front of it and caught my breath. A little farther and I thought I'd reach another city. Which city, I didn't know. I didn't want to know. Anywhere but the Reckoning would do.

*I felt something like relief, in that moment, for the first time. A mistaken relief. I had no idea how far I'd already come, what time it was, or by what schedule that gate operated. I was only reassured because I'd seen light. People are reassured that easily.*

Somewhere, a very low mechanical hum sounded. Distant, regular. I didn't know what it was. It was nearly lost in the sound of the water.

*I know now. That was some device, somewhere, running the whole of this conduit. At the time I just took it for part of the water's noise and let it pass. There are things in this world that keep running, always, without anyone paying them any mind. That day, I was standing right in front of one of them.*

I moved to where the water ran shallower and passed beneath the sluice gate.

Past the gate, the tunnel narrowed again. The station's light fell away behind me, and ahead was dark once more. I stepped into that dark again. This time it frightened me a little less. My body seemed to have grown a little used to the dark.

*That familiarity was an illusion — I would learn that only a few steps further. The dark isn't something you get used to. I only wanted to believe I had.*

A few steps on, I turned to look back. The light from where the gate stood was still visible. Very small now.

I turned my back on that light and walked forward again. My legs were heavy. I adjusted my bag on my shoulder, put a hand to the wall. At the next fork, I'd pick a side again, with no reason for it. I wasn't afraid of that. I still didn't know, then, exactly what I ought to have been afraid of.

*Writing this chapter, I keep coming back to the same sentence. I didn't know, then. I didn't know, then. When this book is finished, I want to count how many times I've written that sentence. I imagine it will be too many to count. Knowing something, and having known it for a long time, are different things. I already knew a great deal, in that dark. I just wasn't ready yet to call it knowing.*
