# Chapter 5. That City

> _Previously_ — Pheugon presses the supervisor about Article 4, Clause 11.
> The supervisor tells him it has never once triggered, in three city cases
> across thirty years, and tries to reassure him there's nothing to worry
> about. Pheugon hears it the opposite way — that as long as it's never
> confirmed, nothing will ever happen, no matter what.

I have never been to the Reckoning.

And yet I know the Reckoning. I know that sounds like a contradiction. To explain it, I have to go back three years.

*I've tried to write this several times and stopped. I still don't know if I can write all of it, even now. I can't write all of it. Even I haven't finished sorting through it. But I need to write this much, or no one will ever understand why I ran.*

---

Three years ago, a person stayed in our house for a time.

Someone from the Reckoning. I won't write the name here. Not yet. I didn't know exactly, then, what business had brought that person to our city, and I don't fully know even now. I heard it had something to do with some matter between the two cities. That's about all I remember.

My father took that person in. That was the kind of man he was. He was good about letting in strangers.

*I still don't want to call that my father's fault. It really was a good thing about him. I learned, then, that a good quality doesn't always lead to a good result.*

---

The room that person used had been a storeroom. My father hurriedly put a bed in and made it a room. I sometimes passed that door and saw light through the crack. That person wrote something late into the night. He said he was copying over my father's storeroom ledger. Verification work, he called it.

I wasn't curious, then, what kind of verification that was. I thought it was adult business. That was explanation enough for a twelve-year-old.

*I'm curious now. Curious far too late. I can still see, when I close my eyes, that light leaking through that crack in the door. What numbers passed back and forth under that light, I may never know, for the rest of my life.*

For a few months, that person ate dinner with our family. Talked with my brother and me. When we asked about the Reckoning, he'd answer with a smile.

"Nobody's ever wronged there." that person said. "The Scales measures everything exactly."

I believed that. I thought precise meant good.

*I'd come to think of that line again, three years later. I didn't know what it meant, then. I still don't fully know now. I only remember that his face, just after saying it, trembled for the briefest moment.*

---

What happened after that, I won't write out in detail here. Voices heard through the door, evenings when my father went silent for days, my mother crying alone in the kitchen — I have pieces like that. I can't string them together. I was twelve then, and grown-ups know how not to finish a sentence in front of a child.

The one thing I know for certain is this. After that, something was recorded on the Scales. Someone in our house had their weight rise. Exactly who, exactly how much, I don't know either. I never asked. I didn't have the courage to ask.

*I believe that weight was accurate. The Scales is always accurate. But being accurate, and my knowing the full account of that calculation, are two different things. I don't know the full account. I still don't know it now. I may not know it even by the time this book is finished.*

I wrote that I heard my mother crying alone in the kitchen exactly once. To be precise, it was twice. The second time was much quieter. It frightened me more than the first. Crying loudly at least gives whoever hears it the illusion they might be able to do something. Crying quietly doesn't even offer that illusion. I stood outside that door and did nothing at all.

*I didn't knock on that door. I still don't know if that was right. At the time it felt like I shouldn't. I thought it would only make things harder for my mother, if she knew I'd heard her crying. Thinking about it now, that may have been a judgment made for my own sake. Pretending not to hear was easier for me than hearing and still doing nothing.*

That winter, just once, I saw my father's hand at a terminal of the Scales. He put his hand up. A number appeared on the screen. He looked at it for a long time. I was standing beside him. I couldn't see the number from where I stood.

"…Father."

"…It's fine." my father said. Before I'd even asked anything.

*I've turned that "it's fine" over for three years now. That wasn't said to me. My father was saying it to himself. I noticed that, at the time, and never once told him that I had.*

---

It was around then that I started checking my own weight every day. In Chapter 2 I called that just a habit. Here I should write it precisely. It wasn't a habit. It was a beginning. From that winter on, I couldn't bear it if my number so much as moved. Keeping it low, lower still, felt like the one thing I could still do.

*I think I said something like this to myself at the time — that since one person in our house had already had their weight rise, the rest of us had to make sure we never let ours rise, no matter what. Whether that's sound reasoning, I know now it wasn't at all. The Scales doesn't calculate by household. My being low doesn't lower my father's number. But I was doing that arithmetic at the time. A child's arithmetic. I never managed to let it go, even after I grew up.*

I wrote, in Chapter 1, about the day Had asked me why I never stopped. I wrote that I couldn't answer. In truth, there was an answer. I couldn't afford to stop. The moment I stopped, I felt something would catch up to me. What that was, I didn't know precisely at the time either. I know now. It was that light, leaking through the crack of that door, that winter.

---

The day that person left, I walked out to the edge of town to see him off.

I still don't quite know why I did. I think I wanted to ask something. But standing in front of him, in the end, I couldn't ask anything at all.

"…Sir."

That person turned around.

"…Yes, Pheugon."

I stood there a long while before I spoke.

"…Take care."

That person looked at me for a moment. As if about to say something, then deciding against it.

"…All right." that person said. "You too. Be well."

He turned and walked away after that. I watched that back for a long time.

*I still remember that back today. I didn't hate that person, then. I didn't know how to hate yet. Hatred grew much later, very slowly. I couldn't even say where it took root.*

I didn't go into the room he'd left behind for a while. It was months before I even went in. The bed was still there, the room already reverting to a storeroom's look. A few sheets of paper had fallen in a corner. I picked them up. Dense with numbers. What numbers, I didn't know. I set the paper back down. Somehow it felt like something I shouldn't hold onto.

*Thinking about it now, I wish I'd kept that paper. But I was twelve then, not old enough to make that call. That paper is nowhere now. It was probably thrown out, sometime, when the room was cleared out again.*

---

I wrote about the table in Chapter 3. That the table which once sat four now sees only one seat used. That table had been there since before that person ever came. I remember, every time I look at it, the seat he sat in too. He always sat to my father's right. My brother sat across from him.

After my brother left, I wouldn't let anyone sit in that seat for a while. When my mother asked why, I couldn't answer.

*I think I believed, back then, that as long as that seat stayed empty, a place would still be kept for my brother to return to. A child's belief. My brother never came back, and in the end that seat just stayed empty.*

---

After that, our family came apart.

My brother left home that winter. I still don't know where he went. He left without a word to me.

*I remember the night he left. He turned back once, at the door. I couldn't tell if he was looking at me or just checking his direction. That was the first night I learned, on my own, how not to cry out loud. Not taught — learned. No one showed me how. I simply figured it out for myself that night.*

My mother went to another city the following year, where relatives lived. She asked me to come with her. I didn't go.

*By then I was already training to become a herald, and I told myself I didn't want to give that up. That was the explanation I gave myself. It probably wasn't the real reason. What the real reason was, I still don't have the confidence to say, even now.*

My father is still in this city. He doesn't live with me. He lives alone, on the other side of town. I see him now and then. Not often. Even when we meet, we don't talk about it.

*We both learned, somehow, how to avoid that subject. We learned it well.*

The last time we met was a few days before I was given the Reckoning case. My father said little, as usual. At parting, he said just one thing.

"…What case are you reading these days."

"…A small one." I lied. It wasn't a lie yet, then — I hadn't received the Reckoning case at that point — but in that moment, I think I was already practicing how to hide something.

My father nodded.

"…Take care of yourself."

I know that was the best my father could say to me. He wasn't a man who could say more. That isn't his fault.

*Parting from my father that day, I thought, for the first time, whether he too still wore that face — the one where something caves in — whenever he heard the name Reckoning. I didn't want to find out, so I never once said that name in front of him, that day.*

---

In Chapter 1 I wrote about the lie I told in the supervisor's room. When he asked, "Do you have some connection to the Reckoning," I answered, "No."

*That was a lie. Now that I'm here, I can see exactly how much of a lie it was. In that moment I played at being someone with no connection to the Reckoning at all. It was the opposite. I was one of the people most deeply entangled with that city. Without ever having set foot on a single one of its streets.*

*Why did the supervisor ask that question. Maybe he'd noticed some expression that crossed my face whenever the Reckoning came up. If there was such an expression, I never knew it myself. I always believed I hid my face perfectly. Looking back, that was probably only my own illusion.*

The Scales calculated that matter. Calculated it precisely, I'm sure. I don't doubt that.

Precise, and nothing came back. My brother didn't come back, my mother didn't come back, our house never became four again. I thought for a long time about how those two sentences could stand together, and in the end I stopped thinking about it.

*No — that isn't true. I didn't stop thinking about it. I went on thinking, in a different way. I decided to pour all that thought into one name — the Reckoning. I set that whole city where that one person had once sat. That wasn't fair. I know that now. I didn't, then.*

---

The day I received the Reckoning's sentence document, my hands didn't shake. I wrote, in Chapter 1, that this was strange.

*It isn't strange to me now. I felt, in that moment, for the first time, that the Scales was right. Just as the weight it had set on my house was accurate, so would this weight, set on the whole of the Reckoning, be accurate too. That city falling felt to me like justice. A city whose name I hadn't even said aloud for three years, finally getting what was owed it.*

*But I never once asked myself, then, whether I had any right to call that justice. Without asking, I found the clause. The clause that could void that justice.*

*The moment I read that clause, I thought exactly one thing. That it must never trigger. Whether that thought came from fear, or from something else entirely, I didn't ask myself, then.*

That night I stared out the window for a long time. Our neighborhood was dark. Somewhere far off, on the other side of the city, I imagined a light my father had lit alone. I looked that way for a long time.

*That night I thought of the word mercy for the first time. To be precise, I admitted for the first time that Article 4, Clause 11 meant mercy. Until then I'd only thought of it as a rule, a procedure, a possibility. The moment I called it mercy, something changed. Mercy had never come to our house. My father's weight was accurate, and because it was accurate, no one ever shaved a single point off it. I was taught that was as it should be. And yet for the Reckoning, for that city, the door of mercy had stood open from the very start.*

*I couldn't bear that unfairness. That night I didn't call it unfairness, and I didn't call it anger, either. I just quietly decided. That door had to stay shut. I would do whatever I could, so that no one ever opened it.*

*I didn't know, that night, that the decision would take the shape of running.*
