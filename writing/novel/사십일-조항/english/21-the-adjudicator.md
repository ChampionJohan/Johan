# Chapter 21. The Adjudicator

> _Previously_ — Pheugon found that Mena's misquotation had actually been used in one of this city's own rulings. But recalculated without that citation, the result was nearly identical. One of the Scales' grounds was wrong; its conclusion was right. He sat in the dark, having lost the ground on which to hate this city again.

About ten days into my visits to the room of records, the record-keeper told me something.

"The Adjudicator's asking for you."

I nearly dropped the paper in my hand.

"…Me?"

"Who else would it be — you're the only outsider who comes here every day these days," the record-keeper said. Her tone suggested it was nothing much, but to me it could be nothing but a great deal.

*Every kind of thought crashed over me at once, in that moment. Had he found out who I was. Or had he simply noticed an outsider rifling through precedents for too long. I had no way of knowing the answer. Only one thing was certain. I couldn't not go. Not going, in itself, would raise suspicion.*

"Today?" I asked.

"Now," the record-keeper said. "He said to send you straight over once he'd seen your face."

I stood up without even tidying the box. Climbing the stairs, I thought several times about taking a roundabout route to the Adjudicator's office. A pointless thought. This city had few streets, and whichever way I went, I'd end up at the same door.

*Climbing those stairs, I remembered something from my training days. The supervisor had once said that a herald's face is sent ahead to each city's administrator. I'd let that pass over me at the time. I thought it was a procedure that had nothing to do with me. That a picture of my face had already come to this city before I had — I hadn't even imagined that.*

---

The Adjudicator's office was on the third floor of the Scales building, at the end of the corridor. I stood in front of the door and steadied my breath.

I knocked, and an answer came right away.

"Come in."

The voice was low and calm. I opened the door.

The room wasn't large. A desk, two chairs, a wall filled with filing cabinets. Through the window, the square was visible below. The plate announcing the days remaining was clearly visible from there too.

On top of the filing cabinet sat an old model of a scale. Too worn to be decoration, and too precisely placed to be something left there by neglect. Not a speck of dust on it. The papers on the desk were stacked with their edges squared. The moment I stepped into that room, I felt that its owner was someone who let nothing sit carelessly.

A man sat behind the desk. He looked to be about fifty. Half his hair had gone gray, and his eyes were deep. No sign of hurry in him.

"Sit," he said.

I sat.

---

He said nothing for a moment, just looked at me. That gaze felt strangely familiar. Not a judging look. A confirming one.

"I hear you've been coming to the room of records often," he finally said.

"…Yes."

"Going through the precedents."

"…I had something I was curious about."

"What's that."

I hadn't prepared an answer.

"…How the Scales judges," I managed to say.

The Adjudicator tilted his head slightly. He didn't look like he believed it. Nor did he press.

"You're not from this city," he said. Not a question — a confirmation.

I stiffened.

"…No," I admitted. There was nothing left to hide about that. It had already come out in front of Trope.

"Where are you from."

*I hesitated again in front of that question. I couldn't say the real name of my city. But strangely, in that moment, a lie didn't come easily to my mouth. I tried to count how many times I'd already lied in front of this man. I couldn't count them. Already too many.*

"…Somewhere far away," I said.

The Adjudicator showed little reaction to that answer.

---

He opened a desk drawer and took out a sheet of paper. Its edges shone from handling. He didn't turn it toward me. He just set it in front of himself and looked down at it.

"There's a roster of the herald assigned to come to this city," he said. "Whole-city cases are special — we get advance notice too. Name and face. Expected arrival date."

I forgot to breathe.

"That herald," the Adjudicator continued, "never came on the scheduled day."

The room went quiet.

*I looked, in that moment, for somewhere to run. The door was behind me. The window was three stories up. I couldn't go anywhere. I could only sit, and wait for whatever he said next.*

The Adjudicator looked at me again. There was still no anger in his eyes, no sharpness of interrogation. Just something he'd been carrying for a long time.

"You," he said.

---

I said nothing. I could neither deny it nor confirm it. My body froze where it sat.

"Don't be alarmed," the Adjudicator said. His voice softened a little. "I didn't call you in to report you."

"…How did you know."

"The face," he said. "There was a sketch attached to the roster. Not an exact likeness, but the features matched, well enough. And when I heard about a person who wasn't from this city, going through the precedents day after day, I didn't think that was a coincidence."

*I felt a strange emotion, in that moment. Fear was the largest part of it, but beneath it was something else. Something close to relief. For weeks, I hadn't been able to show my true self to anyone. And this man already knew. Knew, and hadn't reported me. Why that fact should put me so strangely at ease — I couldn't explain it, then.*

"…Why aren't you reporting me," I finally asked.

The Adjudicator put the paper back in the drawer.

"What would reporting change," he said. "If I caught you and forced you to read — would that be a proclamation? That wouldn't be a proclamation. Just sound on paper."

I didn't fully understand what that meant, at the time.

*I didn't notice, then, the speed at which his hand closed that drawer. Not hurried, not forceful. A gesture as if whatever was inside that drawer had nothing to do with his own feelings. I only learned later that this wasn't restraint — it was habit. He had probably closed that drawer with that same motion, countless times, for years.*

---

"What's your name," the Adjudicator asked. "Your real one."

I hesitated for a long time.

"…Pheugon."

The Adjudicator mouthed the name once, silently, without sound. As if confirming a name he already knew.

"Pheugon," he said again, this time aloud. "Good. I'll call you that."

"…To Trope, I'm still Had."

"That's your business, not mine," the Adjudicator said. No reproach in the tone.

*I felt, in that moment, for the first time, that I didn't need to hide in front of this man. The first time in weeks. I only realized later, looking back, how tense my body had still stayed all the way until I left that room.*

---

"Why did you call me here," I asked. "Just to tell me you're not reporting me?"

The Adjudicator glanced out the window for a moment. The plate in the square was visible.

"No," he said. "I wanted to see what kind of person you were."

"…Why?"

"A whole-city case is rare," he said. "This is the fourth one since I've sat in this chair. And all four times, the herald ends up sitting in that chair over there. Eventually."

I didn't fully understand what he meant.

"Has this city had this happen before?" I asked. "A herald not coming."

The Adjudicator was quiet for a moment.

"It has," he said.

"…What happened?"

"That's enough for today," he said. I felt that this was a way of avoiding the answer. But that avoidance felt, oddly, more careful than irritating. As if he'd judged that I wasn't ready yet to hear it.

"Why did you go through the precedents," the Adjudicator asked, changing the subject. "You said you wanted to know how the Scales judges."

"…Yes."

"Did you find anything."

I hesitated a moment, unsure how much to say, then opened my mouth.

"…I found an error," I said. "But the conclusion didn't change."

The Adjudicator's expression turned interested, for the first time.

"Is that so," he said. "That's a good find."

"…A good find? Nothing changed."

"Nothing changing is the finding," the Adjudicator said. "Everyone assumes that if the Scales is wrong, something must come apart. Now you know it doesn't."

I didn't fully grasp the weight of that, at the time.

"That's enough for today," the Adjudicator said, tidying his papers. "I've learned your face now. That's enough."

I stood. I walked toward the door, then turned back.

"…Why do you leave someone who's run away alone?"

The Adjudicator looked at me. A long look.

"That," he said. "I'll tell you next time."

"…One more thing." I asked again, hand still on the door handle. "What happens now. Since I didn't come."

The Adjudicator's expression shifted, slightly, for the first time. Only for a moment.

"That's the Scales' business," he said. "The Scales doesn't stop just because one person didn't come. It finds a way."

"…A way?"

"That's enough for today," the Adjudicator said. I felt, from his tone, that there was no more room to press.

"Go on," he said, turning his eyes back to his papers. "You may keep going to the room of records. I won't stop you."

I opened the door and went out.

*I turned that phrase, "it finds a way," over and over in my head, walking down the corridor. I didn't know, then, exactly what it meant. I only left that building carrying a vague unease.*

---

Out in the corridor, I felt my legs shaking.

*Leaving that room that day, I realized for the first time that I wasn't entirely alone in this city. I didn't know, then, whether that was a comfort. Having one more person who knew me also meant one fewer place to hide.*

*I thought for a long time that night about the air of that room. The Adjudicator's gaze, that calm. He hadn't judged me. And yet strangely, that calm had felt heavier than any judgment. I didn't understand why, at the time. I know now. He hadn't judged me because he hadn't judged me — he had already, before me, watched three people like me. Those three memories had settled, unmoved, behind his calm.*

*And I would hear, not long after, about that third person.*

---

I didn't go straight to the shed. I walked across the square. My feet carrying me I didn't quite know where.

Trope was at the well. Drawing the evening water. She saw me and waved.

"Had," she called. I still answered to that name. She didn't know that, a few minutes before, in another room, I'd been called by another name.

"…Yes," I answered, walking over.

"Where'd you go? Haven't seen you since midday."

*I stopped, in front of that question. I had just given my real name to the Adjudicator's face. Not half an hour after saying that name, I had to answer again under the fake one.*

"…The Scales office," I said, half the truth. "I had business there."

"What kind of business at the Scales office?" Trope asked. An unsuspecting question.

"…Nothing much," I said again.

Trope looked at me a moment, then laughed.

"That again," she said. "Fine. I won't ask."

*In front of that laugh, I felt, for the first time, something I could barely bear. She was trusting me. By not asking. And the larger that trust grew, the heavier the weight of the lies I was carrying grew alongside it. That day, I had shed my name in front of the Adjudicator and had to put it back on in front of Trope. Moving between the two, for the first time, felt exhausting.*

---

That night, lying in the shed, I called the Adjudicator's gaze back to mind.

*He had recognized me. And hadn't caught me. He hadn't yet told me why. I wanted to know the reason. Was it simple pity? Or something else? He'd said, "the herald ends up sitting in that chair, eventually." I couldn't tell what that meant. Only, from his tone, I could feel that he'd watched several people like me, before me.*

*I thought, for the first time that night, that I might not be the only person who had ever fled in this city. That thought made me feel a little less alone. And, at the same time, more afraid. If I wasn't the only one who had fled, where were they now? I would come to know the answer to that question soon enough.*

*I admitted one more thing, for the first time, that night. Leaving the Adjudicator's room, I had felt, oddly, lighter rather than more afraid. Being found out had made me lighter. Hiding takes strength. I had been spending that strength for weeks, and now that it was no longer needed in front of one person, at least, I understood, only then, how exhausted I had been.*
