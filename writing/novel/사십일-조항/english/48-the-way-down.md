# Chapter 48. The Way Down

> _Previously_ — Pheugon asked him, "Have you been in a city like this before?" For the first time, he smiled and dodged the question. He left with the words, "I'll come again. When you need me." Trope said she'd come up every day. That night, for the first time, Pheugon felt certain that the day he'd go down the hill was coming.

I spent a few more days on that hill.

I didn't count exactly how many. Writing this book, I know how strange that is. I was a person who counted three days, counted forty days, counted one hundred twelve cases. And yet I didn't count those days. I never decided not to count them, either. At some point, without my noticing, the habit of marking off days on my fingers had simply stopped.

*I still can't pin down exactly when that started. Maybe it was from the day the vine grew. Or maybe from the day he smiled. Either way, from that day on, I stopped being someone who counted time.*

Trope came up every day. She'd leave the water jug and bread, sit a while, then go back down. Some days she talked a lot. Other days she just sat beside me in silence and left. The Adjudicator never came up, not once. Instead he sent short messages through Trope. "Take your time." I turned those words over many times. There was no urging in them at all.

One day Trope brought up the substitute herald. He'd already left for another city, she said. Before leaving, he'd left one line. "This case is going to sit strangely in the record." Trope said she didn't know what that meant. I didn't say that I did.

One day, no one came at all. I spent that whole day sitting beside the dry stem, thinking about those small white points that had once looked like they might become buds. Whatever they might have become, they had withered along with everything else, before becoming anything at all. I still hadn't decided, even that day, whether that was a sad thing or not.

---

I knew, the moment I opened my eyes that morning.

I couldn't have said exactly what I knew, then. My body simply moved first. I sat up. The dry stem was still there. Withered black, its roots still lodged in the rock crack.

I looked at that stem for a long while.

*I've rewritten this scene several times. In one draft I wrote that I cried in front of it. In another, that I laughed. Neither was true. The truth is, I did nothing at all. I just looked. For a long time.*

I reached out and gripped the base of the dry stem once. It made a breaking sound. That sound no longer bothered me.

"…Thank you." I said. The same words I'd said, that first time, under its shade. But the words that day and these words today were different words. I still can't explain the difference. I only know that it is a difference.

I stood.

---

My body swayed. I hadn't eaten properly in days. I stood a moment, steadying myself. The city lay below. The moment I'd first been washed up at the well forty days before, the first night on this hill waiting for the city to fall, and now — these three moments overlapped all at once.

*I decided nothing, in that moment. Writing this book, I've felt the temptation, many times, to write "I decided, then, to..." It would have made for a cleaner sentence. But it isn't true. I didn't decide anything. My body simply stood, and my legs simply began to walk. The decision followed later, little by little, along the way.*

I gathered my things. There wasn't much. A few clothes, an empty water jug, and —

I stopped there.

---

There was a folded paper tucked inside my coat. Old, its edges a little worn. I took it out and unfolded it.

Two sentences, written in my own hand.

*Original*: This one never explained himself.
*Precedent*: This one never repented.

*I haven't once thrown this paper away, since the day I copied it out in the room of records. Not on the day I believed it would be evidence enough to bring down the Scales. Not on the day I learned that evidence would overturn nothing at all. Why I never threw it away, I didn't fully know myself, even then.*

I looked at that paper for a long time.

*Never explained himself. Never repented.*

Mena had been someone who, in some city, had watched the difference between those two lines. While that difference quietly reshaped verdicts in other cities, one after another, Mena had probably never known it. Or she had known, and been unable to do anything about it. I still don't know which of those two is the sadder thing.

*I thought about leaving this paper up on the hill. It was evidence that had already become useless inside this city. The Scales' conclusion had been right. This paper had overturned nothing. And still, I folded it back up and put it inside my coat.*

*I couldn't explain, that day, why I did that. I still can't, fully. I only know that I had become someone who knew that this person's — Mena's — sentence had become something else entirely, passing through someone else's hands. And someone who knows a thing like that, I learned that day, cannot leave that knowledge lying anywhere behind.*

---

Here, for the first time, I have something to set straight about this book.

I am writing these words right now.

I have been writing this from the very first line of this book. And yet, through forty-seven chapters, I never once set that fact plainly down on the page. I only ever wrote "I know," "I didn't know then," "now I know a little more." I never once revealed who stood behind those sentences.

*I asked myself why I'm writing this so late. The answer is simple. I felt I could only admit I was writing this once I had come all the way down this last hill of the book. Right now, before I've even finished coming down this hill, I set it down here. Because I wanted to write it before it was too late.*

Why am I writing this.

*Mena was someone who kept records. Someone who belonged to no city, who moved between many, writing down what people said. One sentence of hers, passing through a few hands, was turned into something entirely different. She couldn't stop it. Either she had already left that city, or she had already let go of that sentence — either way, she wasn't in a position to protect her own words.*

*I was afraid of my own sentences ending up the same way. So I decided to set down these forty days with my own hand. In case someone, copying this story out later, turned "he was angry to the end" into "he repented in the end." Or the reverse — turned "he raged to the end" into "in the end he understood it all."*

*I am neither. I am still walking, somewhere between the two.*

*That's why I'm keeping this paper. What Mena couldn't protect, I want to protect in her place. And yet I know — keeping this one sheet of paper in my coat won't restore their proper meaning to the rest of Mena's sentences, scattered somewhere across other cities. That wasn't something I could do today. But there was one thing I could do today. At least this one page in my hand — I could keep it safe.*

*I don't know who will read this book. Maybe no one will. I write it anyway. I never wanted to answer someone the way the supervisor once answered me — "because that's the rule." I wanted to leave behind what happened, instead of the rule. At the size it actually happened, neither adding nor taking away. That was the one proclamation I found I could still make, after enduring these forty days.*

---

I started down the hill.

The field path was the same one I'd first seen that night, forty days before, going out to bring Trope back. It had been dark then; I'd never really seen the shape of the road. Now it was daylight, and the path lay open before me. A narrow dirt track, half-buried in grass. A path farmers must have walked, once, long ago.

I followed it down.

*Writing this sentence, I find it strange that I can say precisely where I'm walking now. For the past three years I had always known exactly where I was supposed to go. The proclamation office. The training grounds. The Reckoning. There was always a fixed destination. But on this path down the hill, there was no fixed place at all. I was simply walking downward, toward where the city was.*

My body still had no strength. Every few steps I stopped briefly to catch my breath. Each time, I looked back. The hilltop grew smaller, little by little. The dry stem couldn't be seen from here anymore.

The sun was hot. Dry dirt crumbled under my feet. Somewhere, insects kept up a low, steady chirring. I caught myself listening to each of those sounds, one by one. For forty days, and for a few more days on this hill, I had spent most of my time listening only to the sounds inside myself. The sound anger made. The sound a question made. On this road, for the first time in a long while, I was hearing the world outside.

---

Walking, I thought of many faces.

The four who had sat at our table, and then five. Had's face, turning back as he packed his things and walked out. "Why don't you stop?" The supervisor's face, asking, "Do you have some connection to the Reckoning?" Trope's face at the well, asking, "You're not from here, are you?" The Adjudicator's face, saying, "That isn't the face of someone afraid." The substitute herald's face, saying, "Congratulations. I think you did the right thing." And the face, a few mornings before, that had smiled and dodged my question.

*I started counting those faces one by one, and stopped myself. I had decided I was no longer someone who counts. But a habit worn into the body doesn't disappear easily. I caught myself trying to count again a few more times, and stopped each time. Stopping, too, I learned on that road, takes its own kind of training.*

I thought, too, of his dirt-covered shoes — the ones from the day he'd said he'd walked all the way up. He could have simply appeared, and yet every time, he'd come with dust on his shoes. I noticed dust gathering on my own shoes now too. It felt strange, as if it placed him and me, somehow, on the same road. It was only a brief feeling, but I wanted to hold on to it.

---

Halfway down, I stopped and turned to look back up at the hill.

The vine couldn't be seen from here either. Too small, or too far. I only knew that something remained up there. There are things you know are there even when you can't see them.

*In that moment I thought of the Forty-Day Clause. That one line, sitting inside the Scales' code, never once invoked. No one had ever thought to strike it out. Even though it looked useless, someone had left it there, inside the text, all along. I understood that impulse a little now. Some things need to be there, even unused.*

I thought of the Adjudicator. In forty years, he had never once seen this clause invoked. And yet, he'd told me, he had never once suggested striking it from the text. I understood those forty years a little now, too. Someone who waits for something doesn't give up the waiting itself, even if they never live to see it used.

*In that moment I thought of Trope's mother too. The handprint left on the well's stone rim. A trace that hadn't faded in five years. Walking that road, I confirmed once more that waiting settles into different shapes for different people. For the Adjudicator, one line in a clause. For Trope, a handprint left in stone. For me, one sheet of paper folded inside my coat.*

I started walking again.

---

The city drew closer, little by little. The outline of the wall sharpened. A few threads of smoke rose among the rooftops. Someone was cooking a meal today too.

Watching that smoke, I thought again of everyone inside those walls — Trope, the Adjudicator, the baker, a hundred twenty thousand people whose names I didn't know, living out their day. How many of them knew that a vine had grown on that hill, and withered? Probably none of them.

*Strangely, that didn't weigh on me. I'd already learned this, back in Chapter 46. Some things don't need to be known by anyone. That day, on that hill, I'd learned to simply sit without needing to prove anything. Today I was learning to simply walk, having risen from that spot, without needing to prove anything either.*

I thought of the Scales. It was probably still turning somewhere in that city, right now. Someone's theft today, someone's restraint today, calculated without error. The Scales would still not be wrong. And I was still walking a place the Scales could never measure.

I reached a point where the gate came into view. Still a long way off. People moving in and out looked like nothing more than small points from here. I stopped again. The dry well beside the gate came into sight. The spot where he'd said he'd walked here from. No one there now.

I looked at that well for a long while. Whether I would meet him there again someday, or in some other city, at some other well, I didn't know. *I'll come again. When you need me.* I still hadn't decided whether to take those words as a real promise, or just as comfort.

I thought, suddenly, of my name again, and Trope's. To flee. Turning. For three years I had fled. For forty days I had fled. And on this hill, I had fled a few days more. And yet now, on this road, I was doing something that was neither fleeing nor turning. I was simply walking. I still don't know, even now, which of the two it's closer to.

---

The path curved in among the fields. I stopped, briefly, at that bend.

*I thought for a very long time about how to end this book, here. I considered writing the scene of my arrival in the city. Someone recognizing me in front of the gate. Trope running out. The Adjudicator taking my hand. I wrote scenes like that several times. I deleted them all.*

*It isn't that I deleted them because I felt I had no right to write them. It isn't that I deleted them because I couldn't clearly remember whether they'd actually happened. I knew that this book had to end in a question, not an answer. I didn't want to invent the answer.*

*When I was eleven, I asked the proclamation supervisor, "But why?" He only answered, "Because that's the rule." I didn't like that answer. I didn't know then that it wasn't really an answer at all. I know now. Some questions have no rule, and no answer. There is only the person who keeps walking, carrying the question.*

*In the first chapter of this book I wrote that I'd wondered why the Scales never speaks its own verdict aloud. I wrote that it was only much later that I came to think about why a person had been placed there instead, and who had placed them there, and why. Even reaching this last chapter, I still haven't found that answer in full. But I know, now, what it feels like to stand in that place.*

---

Right now, I am standing at that bend.

Mena's paper is inside my coat. Behind me is the withered vine. Ahead of me is the city. I don't know whether I'm ready to go back into that city. I don't know what I'll say when I see Trope again. I don't have an answer, yet, to his question — Doest thou well to be angry.

*I am walking, carrying all of this, unknown. For three years I believed that doing something without knowing was the most frightening thing in the world. That's why I always calculated. Always counted. Always confirmed. Right now I am walking without calculating anything at all. And strangely, it isn't frightening.*

I started walking again.

The path kept leading downward. The city lay at its end. I hadn't yet seen the end of that path.

*Even after finishing this book, I am still on that path. Whether I reached the city or not, I've chosen not to write here. That isn't me hiding something. I learned, writing this book, that some stories must not end in arrival.*

The wind blew across the fields. The smell of dry earth came with it, and, very faintly, the smell of someone cooking, carried from the direction of the city. I walked on, breathing that smell in.

My stomach was hungry again. My throat was dry again. This time I didn't turn that hunger and thirst into hurrying. I'd walk a few steps, stop to rest, and walk again. Whenever the thought came that someone might be waiting for me, I found myself, instead, slowing my steps. I couldn't explain why, then. I understand a little now — I wanted to walk this road itself a little longer.

I walk.

*And I am still, even now, standing before that question.*

*Was I right to be angry.*

*(The End.)*
