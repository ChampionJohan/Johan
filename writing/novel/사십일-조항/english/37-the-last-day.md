# Chapter 37. The Last Day

> _Previously_ — Trope, believing the whole city would die for her weight alone, walked out through the gate alone. Pheugon went out to find her. On the way back, the two of them saw the Reckoning's lights together, for the first time, from a distance.

The fortieth day broke exactly like every other day.

I thought that was strange. The world should have looked different somehow. The sky should have hung low. The birds should have gone quiet. But the sun rose in the same place it had risen yesterday, and the line at the well was the same line as yesterday. The baker opened her shop at the same hour as yesterday.

*That morning I found it unbearably unfair. A city was ending, and the sky didn't know it. It wasn't only the sky. The Scales didn't know it either. Today it was weighing people the same way it had weighed them every day before.*

---

Trope hadn't said a word since we'd reached the gate.

The night before, walking her back the whole way, I hadn't known what to say. I knew why she'd tried to leave. She believed her own weight was pulling the city down. Her mother had been pushed out that way, so she believed it was only right that she go the same way.

I didn't tell her she was wrong. I knew it would do no good. Instead I took her hand and walked. It was a long way. The city's lights had started as points and slowly grown larger.

She didn't pull away.

When we passed back through the gate, Trope spoke for the first time.

"…Why did you come for me."

I hadn't prepared an answer.

"…I don't know."

"You came without knowing?"

"…It felt like I had to go get you."

She didn't laugh at that, and she didn't cry either. She just lowered her head a little. Then she walked on again without another word. I walked beside her, and that was the whole of what we did that night.

*I was afraid of that silence. I couldn't tell whether it was an angry silence or just a tired one. Now I know. It was both. And there was a third thing in it too. Shame. That night, for the first time, she had been caught wanting to live.*

---

In the morning she shared her food with me. We sat on the steps outside the room where she was staying and ate. Nobody spoke. It felt like the whole city was holding its breath.

"…It's today." Trope said.

"…Yes."

"I'm not scared."

I didn't believe her. Her hand, around the bowl, was shaking slightly.

"…Liar."

"…Yes. I'm lying."

She set the bowl down.

"It's not that I'm afraid of the city falling. I thought it falling was only right, so that part doesn't scare me. What scares me." She stopped, then went on. "What scares me is what happens if it doesn't fall."

I didn't fully understand what she meant, not then.

*Now I know. She wasn't afraid of judgment. She was afraid of mercy. Because if mercy came, there would be no way left to confirm whether she really deserved it.*

*Judgment has rules. Cross the weight and you're pushed out. That can be predicted, and so it can be borne. Mercy has no rules. Trope would never be able to know why mercy came to her. I thought, at the time, that she would have found it easier if she'd simply crossed the weight. I didn't yet know how wrong that thought was.*

We sat on those steps a long time after the bowls were empty. The sun climbed a little higher. From the direction of the square, footsteps began to gather.

"…I should go." I said.

Trope nodded. As she stood, she added, very quietly.

"…Whatever happens, thank you for coming to get me."

I had no answer for that.

---

On the way to the square, I walked this city slowly for the first time.

I'd lived here over a month, and I'd never once looked closely at these streets. Today I did. There was a sign on the bakery that hadn't been there yesterday — anyone who couldn't pay their tab would simply be given the bread. At the edge of the market, the merchant who'd once been caught cheating his scales was recalibrating one with his own hands. A child passing by picked up the walking stick an elderly neighbor had dropped and carried it to him.

Small things. Things too small for the Scales to register at all.

*I counted those small things. I couldn't help it. The habit that had counted one hundred twelve cases surfaced here too. Eight, nine, ten. Ten things in ten steps. I didn't dare work out what that came to across the whole city.*

*The Scales weighed none of the ten. What isn't stolen, what isn't struck, what isn't lied — none of it carries weight. To the Scales, those were exactly as if nothing had happened at all.*

*That was the first time I felt the Scales' arithmetic was missing something. I didn't know, then, how to put that feeling into words.*

---

The square was already full of people.

I had never before seen the people of the Reckoning gathered like this in one place. On ordinary days, even the lines were only a few deep at the terminals. Today the whole square was packed. People holding children. People leaning on canes. People saying nothing, just staring at the sky. Two old men stood holding each other's hands. One had his face pressed against the other's shoulder.

On the board raised in the center of the square, a number hung in the air.

**0.**

I looked at that number. I had been counting down from thirty-nine. The first number I'd seen, the day I was washed up in this city, was thirty-nine, and every day since I'd watched it drop by one. Today there was nowhere left for it to fall.

*I did a strange arithmetic standing in front of that number. If I'd seen it once a day for thirty-nine days, I had seen it thirty-nine times. Thirty-eight of those times I'd watched it the way you watch a stranger's city. Only the last time had I watched it as my own.*

*That one time undid all the other thirty-eight.*

Familiar faces showed among the crowd. The baker. The market vendor who always drew the longest line at the terminal. The girl next door who used to fetch water with Trope. I didn't know their names. Three months ago that wouldn't have mattered. Now it did.

The baker recognized me and came over. There was still flour on her hands.

"…So you're the one. The boy from the well."

"…Yes."

"You're the one reading it today?"

I couldn't answer. She didn't wait for one — she took my hand. It was a big, rough hand.

"I don't know where to put my thanks." she said. "For coming. For being here."

I couldn't close my hand around hers. She had no idea what I was about to do. She only knew I was the one person who could save this city — not that I had spent all this time wishing it would die.

*I remember that hand for a long time. Holding it, I realized for the first time that I was a person with two faces. While this city saw me as a savior, I still thought of myself as a judge.*

---

The Adjudicator was standing on the platform.

Beside him stood an unfamiliar face — a year or two older than me, with a straight, trained posture, dressed in the herald's uniform. The substitute assigned after I'd run.

I had never seen him before. But I could read him at a glance. That stance, that gaze. I'd seen it every day at the training grounds. A face that had learned not to get angry. A face that had learned not to stop.

*A few years earlier, that would have been me standing there — that face, that posture. Looking at him, I knew for the first time how far I had come from that place.*

He looked at me. His expression didn't move.

"…You're Pheugon."

"…Yes."

"I was assigned two months ago. You never came."

There was no resentment in it. It was a voice simply stating a fact. That made it harder.

"…I'm sorry."

"No apology is needed." he said. "I only have to do my job. We don't write feelings into the record."

I felt like I'd heard that somewhere before. It was nearly word for word what the supervisor used to tell me.

"…How much have you read."

"This one case." he said. "I was told it's the largest there's ever been."

*In that moment I found him frightening. Not because he was a bad person. Because he was me. Me, forty days ago. A weightless face, a voice that never stopped, someone ready to read this city.*

---

The Adjudicator drew me aside.

"You took your time getting here."

"…Yes."

"And you found Trope."

"…Yes."

The Adjudicator looked out over the square for a moment. His eyes moved between the people, the number, one another, and the platform. Every face there knew what it was waiting for.

"You know something that boy doesn't." The Adjudicator moved his chin slightly toward the substitute herald. "That the clause exists. What it requires. He's only a reader. He does what he was trained to do."

"…So."

"If you don't step forward, he'll read it. And if he reads it, he becomes the confirmer." The Adjudicator looked at me. "He doesn't know this city. He arrived a month ago. How would he know whether this city has turned or not. Weight can't show him that."

I didn't answer.

"The decision is yours." The Adjudicator said. "I'm not telling you to do it, and I'm not telling you not to. For forty years I've never once invoked this clause. I may find out why today."

His voice was even. His hands weren't. Behind his back, they kept crumpling the edge of the papers.

"One thing I'll say." He lowered his voice. "If you read it, you have to read all of it. Not a summary — the full text. From the first line to the last, Article 4 Clause 11 and all, without missing a line. Leave one line out and the clause itself doesn't activate. That's how it's written."

"…Why."

"I don't know." The Adjudicator said. "Forty years, and I never worked it out. Whoever wrote that clause must have had a reason for setting it up that way. Maybe—" He stopped, then went on. "Maybe it means you can only recognize a city's turning once you know its sin in full. That's only my guess."

I felt the weight of the sentence document in my hand. It was only a few sheets of paper, but my wrist ached.

*I heard something in the Adjudicator's voice that day. I didn't know what it was, then. Now I know. It was envy. He had never once had the chance to stand in this place. Forty years living in this city, knowing only that the clause existed, with no one to use it. I had that chance. He hadn't.*

---

I looked at Trope.

She stood at the edge of the square. Alone, among the crowd, her two hands clasped in front of her. When our eyes met, she gave the smallest nod. That was all.

I didn't know what she was waiting for. Whether she was waiting for the city to fall, or waiting for it to live. Her face looked ready for either.

Someone on the platform rang a bell. The square went quiet. A child began to cry and was quickly hushed by its mother's hand. After that, there was truly no sound at all.

The Adjudicator looked at me. Then at the substitute herald. Then at me again.

"Who reads it."

I looked at the substitute herald. He already had the sentence document in his hands. His hands weren't shaking. I used to look exactly like that. His fingers, holding the paper, were spaced at precisely even intervals. That's what training teaches you.

*I want to say, even now, that up to that moment I still hadn't been sure what I would do. But that would be a lie. I already knew. It was only my feet that hadn't believed it yet.*

I took one step forward.

"…I'll read it."

The square looked at me. It felt like tens of thousands of eyes turning toward me all at once. Trope looked at me. Her clasped hands stiffened slightly.

The substitute herald looked at me, then held out the papers without a word. His face showed no resentment, no relief. As he handed the papers over, he added one thing.

"…Read it well."

It was something trainees said to each other. Before an exam, before a first proclamation — something they said to one another. I'd heard those words dozens of times in three years. I never expected to hear them like this.

"…Yes."

I took the papers. My fingertips felt a familiar weight. The weight of one hundred twelve readings, and now, for the hundred thirteenth time, a different weight. I could feel the difference in my hands.

Trope was still watching me. I sent her no signal. There was no signal to send. Even I didn't fully know, in that moment, what I was about to do.

*I still remember, in my fingertips, the moment I took that paper. People sometimes ask me what I decided in that moment. I can't answer. There was nothing that deserved to be called a decision. My feet simply moved forward, and my hand took the paper. The decision came after, while I was reading.*

The Adjudicator nodded, very quietly.

"Then." he said. "Let's begin."

Every eye in the square turned toward me. The zero on the board glowed behind my back. A wind passed once, and the edge of the paper stirred slightly. I unfolded it.

*I remember that wind. It was very mild. A wind with nothing to do with the smell of water I'd breathed in the conduit forty days before. And yet, strangely, in that instant I thought of that darkness — the three days I'd been shut in, the night I first spoke aloud. Only then did I understand that that night led straight to this square.*
