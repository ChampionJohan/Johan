# Chapter 31. The One Who Knows First

> _Previously_ — Trope said what she truly believed for the first
> time. That the city could be forgiven, but she might not be.
> "There's forgiveness, I suppose. Just not for me." Pheugon found no
> argument against it. The two of them sat at the well until the sun
> went down.

I saw the man again in a market alley.

I'd seen him a few times before — in the square, when someone was
being unfairly ganged up on, while everyone else was getting angry,
he was the one who didn't. I'd taken him for simply an odd man. This
time was different.

At the mouth of the market, a woman was shouting. She'd kicked over a
stall. The stall owner backed away, both hands raised.

"…I didn't steal it! Why would I steal it!"

"Who else would it be!" the woman shouted. "You were right next to my
spot yesterday!"

People gathered. Some took the woman's side, some defended the stall
owner. Voices rose.

I stopped as I was passing through. Two months ago I'd have walked
right past a scene like this. Now I knew some of the faces in this
alley a little. The stall owner had been trading here a long time; the
shouting woman was a stranger to me.

The man was standing in the middle of it.

---

I'd sensed something strange about him from the first time I saw him.
I couldn't have said exactly why. This time, up close, I understood.

His clothes weren't so different from anyone else's in this city.
Nothing about him stood out. And yet the spot where he stood was
strangely quiet. Right in the middle of the commotion, the noise
seemed to drop a full tone around him.

He was looking at the woman. But something about the look was
different. Everyone else looked at the stall owner with suspicion. He
was looking at the woman.

"…Is something the matter," he asked her.

His voice wasn't loud. And yet it carried clearly through the
commotion.

The woman turned toward him.

"…Who are you."

"You're angry about the missing goods," he said. "But that's not all
of it, I don't think."

The woman's face flickered.

"…What are you talking about."

"You lost something else yesterday too," he said quietly. "Not
goods."

---

A chill went down my spine.

The woman stood there, unable to speak. A few seconds later, tears
welled in her eyes. The face that had been shouting a moment ago
turned into a completely different face.

I hadn't heard anything at that spot. I had no idea what he knew, to
say something like that. But the woman understood. She sank down
right where she stood and began quietly crying.

The stall owner stood there, still not understanding what had
happened. The crowd drifted off, one and two at a time. What could
have become a fight ended, just like that.

*In that moment, I thought of the Scales. The Scales doesn't know
what that woman lost. It only measures what converts to weight. It
could weigh the price of stolen goods, but whatever she'd lost the
day before wasn't among the Scales' categories. And yet he knew.*

---

He walked over to the stall owner afterward.

The man was still standing there, looking wronged. The other man
stopped in front of him for a moment.

"…You're startled," he said.

"…I really didn't steal it," the stall owner said. His voice shook.

"I know," he said. "That you didn't steal it."

"…How do you know?"

"That sack under your stall." He pointed under the stall. "You bought
it from someone else this morning. Paid for it in full."

The stall owner's eyes went wide. I had no idea what that sack was.
Neither did the stall owner ask how he'd known. He just nodded. His
face looked relieved.

*I felt two things at once, in that moment. One was astonishment. He
was as precise as the Scales. Precise, but used entirely differently.
The Scales would have used that precision to expose a crime. He used
it to prove there wasn't one.*

*The other was fear. If he could know that precisely, there must be
something inside me he'd already read too. It was the first time I
wanted to shrink away from someone.*

---

I followed him.

From a distance. He left the market and turned into a back alley. His
pace was unhurried. No trace of being in a rush at all.

I stopped him.

"…Excuse me."

He turned. His face was hard to place an age on. Young in one light,
somewhere ancient in another.

"…Yes."

"What did you say to that woman just now? How did you know?"

He looked at me a moment, then smiled.

"I didn't know," he said. "I guessed."

"You guess that precisely?"

"I don't know what else to call it," he said. "I just tend to watch
people for a long time."

I found the answer strange. It didn't sound like an answer at all.

We walked side by side after that. The alley was narrow, in shade.
Laundry hung overhead. As we walked, he occasionally met the eyes of
someone passing — not quite a greeting, not quite not one. Just a
look that said he recognized them.

I found that strange too. I'd lived in this city almost two months
and still didn't recognize most people passing me on the street. He
claimed not to be from here, and yet he seemed to recognize nearly
everyone.

---

"…You could've been angry at her," I said. "Shouting, kicking over
the stall. That was rude."

"It was," he said.

"So why didn't you get angry?"

He thought about that for a moment.

"No reason to," he said.

"You just got treated rudely."

"That woman was rude, that's true," he said. "But I also know why
she did it. Once you know why, it's hard to stay angry."

I wanted to push back at that. I couldn't find the argument.

*That was the first time I truly thought this man was strange. The
first time I'd seen him, in the square, I'd just thought he was odd
for not getting angry. This time was different. He wasn't holding his
anger back. He wasn't angry at all. Those were two entirely different
things.*

"There's someone I know," I said, "who did something bad to my
family. I found myself getting angrier at him the more I got to know
him. The more I understood why he did it."

"That can happen," he said. He didn't deny it.

"But you're the opposite. The more you understand, the less angry you
get. Why is that different from me?"

He stopped walking and looked straight at me.

"What you know is why that person did it," he said. "I know one more
thing than that."

"…What."

"Where that person is right now," he said. "Knowing why someone did
something, and knowing which way they're walking, right now, this
instant — those are different things. Understanding the reason can
make you angrier. But knowing the direction turns the anger into
something else."

I half understood.

"…What does it turn into?"

"That's something you'll have to find out yourself, I think," he
smiled. "I'm not the one who gives answers."

---

"…If I wanted to know that woman's weight," I asked, "I could just
ask the Scales, couldn't I? Why watch people that long?"

"The Scales tells you what that woman did," he said. "It doesn't tell
you what she's going through right now."

"What's the difference?"

"What was done is the past," he said. "What's being gone through is
the present. The Scales is a machine that measures the past. Not a
machine that measures the present."

I turned that over. It resembled what the Adjudicator had told me.
That the Scales can't measure direction. But this man seemed to know
something even more precise than direction.

"…Are you," I asked, carefully, "from this city, by any chance?"

"No," he said.

"Then where are you from?"

He smiled instead of answering. A smile that deflected the question.

---

"…Someone I know," I said, suddenly. "Her weight is low, and she's
still constantly afraid. She believes she inherited something. Can
you guess her weight too?"

He looked at me. Something in that look made me freeze up for a
moment. As if he already knew her.

"That person doesn't believe she inherited something," he said
quietly. "She believes that's all she'll ever be."

I lost my breath. I had never told him Trope's name.

"…How did you—"

"I told you, I guess," he said, smiling again. But the smile was a
little different this time.

"…Have you ever met her?" I asked.

"Hard to say," he said. Not an answer.

"If you'd met her, you'd know. How convinced she is. Words won't
change that. I've tried."

"It's not going to be changed by words," he said. "I know that too."

"Then what does change it?"

He didn't answer. Instead he looked at me for a long time. There was
something in that look I couldn't read yet.

"Tell her this," he said. "That she's not the only one who can see
what the Scales can't."

"That's it?"

"That's it," he said. "For now."

That last word bothered me strangely. *For now.* It sounded as if
there were more, later. I didn't ask when that later was.

---

"…What's your name?" I asked, right before we parted.

He answered without stopping.

"Just a man passing through."

"Everyone has a name."

"Not everyone," he said. "Some people just haven't started using
theirs yet."

I didn't understand that answer either. He walked off toward the end
of the alley and disappeared.

---

I went to find Trope that evening and told her what happened.

"He said I'm not the only one who can see what the Scales can't?"
Trope repeated. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know," I said. "But he knew exactly what you'd told me,
without ever having met you. And I never said your name to him."

Trope's face turned complicated.

"…That's frightening," she said.

"It scared me too, at first."

"And now?"

I thought a moment.

"…I don't know," I said. "It's frightening, and at the same time
there's something else there too. That he knew me that precisely and
still didn't get angry. I keep coming back to that."

Trope listened and said nothing. She just looked off into the
distance.

"…I'd like to meet him sometime too," she finally said. "But it
scares me."

"Me too."

We didn't talk about it any more that night. As we parted, she looked
at my face a little longer than usual. Like there was something more
she wanted to ask, and was holding back.

---

That night before sleep, I made a strange decision.

*I wanted to confirm whether he really was a man who never got angry.
Words hadn't been able to confirm it. He'd already had two chances to
get angry and hadn't. I wanted to test him a third time — not by
chance, this time, but in a place I made myself.*

*Even then I dimly knew it wasn't a good idea. But I'd lived three
years as someone who held his anger back. What it felt like to hold
anger back, rather than simply not have it — I knew that better than
anyone. I wanted to know whether he was truly a man with no anger, or
just a man holding his back, like me.*

*That night I thought about him for a long time. He knew people
before the Scales did, more precisely than the Scales did. And that
knowledge went in a strange direction. The more precisely the Scales
knew, the heavier its sentence grew. The more precisely he knew, the
lighter he treated people. I couldn't understand that opposite
direction.*

*I had lived believing precision moved opposite to forgiveness. The
Scales was my proof. Know more precisely, and the sin becomes
clearer; the clearer the sin, the heavier the sentence. But this man,
knowing more precisely, saw not the sin but the person. I didn't know
where I could learn that difference.*

I mulled over whether I should tell the Adjudicator about this. Then
decided against it. Even asking, I doubted he'd know the answer. This
man seemed to belong to no one, not even inside this city.

*From that day on, I put him at the top of a list of strange people.
There were no other names on that list. I found myself waiting for
the day I'd meet him a second time. Why I was waiting, I didn't know
then.*

Right before sleep, I thought once more of that woman's face. Shouting,
then sinking down and crying. That was the first time I saw what face
is left when anger collapses. I never did hear what she had lost. But
the face stayed with me a long time.

*I tried to hold that face up against my own. If my own anger
collapsed, what would be underneath. That night I wasn't ready to
answer that question at all.*
