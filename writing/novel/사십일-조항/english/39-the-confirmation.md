# Chapter 39. The Confirmation

> _Previously_ — Pheugon read the sentence document from the first line to the last, all the way to Article 4, Clause 11, aloud. The moment he read it, he became the confirmer. The clause required one sentence, spoken by him. The square watched him and waited.

What the clause required was not a long explanation.

It was one sentence. Six words. "This city has turned." That was all. No lengthy testimony required, no grounds to line up. The whole thing hung on that one sentence.

*Even now, I find that simplicity strange. The Scales spends dozens of figures just to assess a single item. Neglect, cruelty, theft, lies — each one carried out to the decimal point. And yet saving an entire city took only six words. Weight required so much language. Turning required so little.*

*I felt that asymmetry was unfair. I still feel it, if I'm honest. How can the system for weighing sin be so meticulous, while the system for weighing forgiveness is allowed to be so bare? But thinking it over again, it wasn't bareness at all. Weight accumulates, so it needs many items. Turning is a direction, so it only needs confirming once. I only came to guess at that difference standing in that square.*

---

I had known those six words. Since forty days before.

They weren't printed on the paper. The clause set down no form for the confirmation. The Adjudicator had told me as much, once. "There's no fixed sentence. But by custom, this is what people say. 'This city has turned.'" In forty years no one had ever used that custom. It had only ever been passed down by word of mouth.

I had turned that sentence over on my tongue many times. In the conduit, on the way to the city, in the dark, in places where no one could hear. Back then I thought it was the one thing I would never say. I had no intention of admitting this city had turned. The moment I admitted this city had turned, the very reason I'd run would fall apart.

*I had memorized that sentence out of hatred for it. I didn't know then that hatred is what makes you memorize something best.*

In the conduit, on the third day, I had spoken it aloud for the first time. Alone in the dark, water up to my knees. "This city has turned." My voice, that time, had been a mockery. I said it to prove how absurd it was. That city, turned? I had laughed, then.

*What lay between that laugh and the silence in the square, I don't know exactly. Only that forty days had passed. In those forty days I had come to know the faces of the people in that city, one by one. And I learned, for the first time, that mockery gets harder once you know a face.*

---

The square was still silent.

I looked at Trope. She hadn't moved. Neither had the Adjudicator. The substitute herald still stood there, holding the papers. Everyone was waiting for me, and yet no one urged me on.

*I still can't describe that silence precisely, even now. It didn't feel like pressure. If it had been pressure, it would have been easier. You can resist pressure. That silence wasn't pressure. It was a blank space. A space that would receive whatever I filled it with.*

I thought of this city. Its people, living by checking their own weight before the Scales, living carefully so as never to spill over. And I thought of the person from this city who had torn our home apart. Both thoughts were in me at once, neither making room for the other.

I thought of the baker's hand. The hand, still floured, that had held mine. "For coming. For being here." The face that had said those words had no idea what I was about to do.

I thought of one person from the Reckoning. I can no longer remember that person's face clearly. But I still see clearly the back of him, walking out our door. For three years I had believed that back was this city's face.

*Between those two faces, the whole city lay. I didn't know which to choose. I still don't.*

I looked at the Adjudicator. There was no urging in his face. He was a man who had waited forty years for this place. And yet now that the moment had arrived, he did nothing on my behalf. He didn't even encourage me. He only watched. I learned later that this was his kindness. This sentence was not one anyone else could choose for me.

I looked at the substitute herald. There was no expectation in his face at all. He wasn't from this city, and he understood this sentence far less than I did. And yet he stood there without stirring. His was the face of someone watching another person's forty days as though they were his own.

The wind passed over the square once more. Someone's hem stirred. A child, unable to hold it in, let out a small cough. I learned, then, how loud that sound could feel.

---

I looked down at the paper again. The last line of the clause. "Confirmation is established by the herald's declaration."

That sentence gave me no instructions at all. When to say it, on what grounds to judge, whether I needed certainty or could do without it — none of it was written down. Whoever had written this clause had left the whole judgment to me and vanished.

*In that moment, for the first time, I wondered who had written this clause. Was it the same people who built the Scales? Or was it someone else entirely? The Adjudicator didn't know, and neither did I. I still don't. But I felt certain that whoever it was had foreseen this exact moment. That the person who knew a city's sins best, the person with every reason to hate it most, would end up standing in this place.*

*I thought that design was cruel. And at the same time I thought there could have been no other way. If this judgment had been given to someone who loved this city, it would have been doubted from the start. If it had been given to someone who didn't know this city at all, it would have meant nothing. Whoever wrote this clause seemed to have decided to trust exactly one kind of person. Someone who hated, and yet had kept watching to the very end.*

I looked out over the square. Of a hundred twenty thousand faces, I could recognize only a handful. The baker. The market merchant. The girl who used to fetch water with Trope. The Adjudicator. The substitute herald.

And Trope.

For a moment, very briefly, I thought of home. Of the last dinner our whole family had eaten together, before it came apart. I don't remember what we talked about that evening. I only remember that it was growing dark outside the window, and that someone had laughed. That laugh was never heard in our house again.

*I tried to tell myself there was no connection between that evening and this square. One was a private matter, and the other belonged to an entire city. But in that moment, inside me, the two sat at exactly the same weight. The Scales would never have calculated it that way.*

---

I had watched this city change, these forty days. It wasn't the same city I'd first arrived in. The one who used to steal, stopped stealing. The one who never spoke, began speaking to others. A child who'd lost her mother returned a stranger's dropped cane. A merchant who'd cheated his scales recalibrated one with his own hands.

None of those changes registered on the Scales. The weight hadn't gone down. The debt already accumulated remained exactly where it was. And yet I had seen it. I had seen what the Scales could not.

Trope's steps, on her way to check her own weight, had changed somewhere along the way. She used to leave with her shoulders sunk. Lately she left with her head still up. Not because the weight had fallen. Because the eyes that looked at the weight had changed.

The Adjudicator's hands, sorting through documents, had changed too. He used to push papers aside as he filed them. Lately he lingered over each sheet, as if reading the names written on it all over again.

The baker had begun giving away flour. The market merchant calibrated his own scales. None of these were items. They were things the Scales never wrote down. And yet I had seen them, every day, for forty days.

*I want to say that in that moment I finally understood why the clause left confirmation to a person. But to be honest, I knew it without understanding it. Understanding and knowing are different things. I couldn't have laid out, in logic, that this city had turned. I simply knew.*

Where that knowing came from, I still can't explain, even now. It can't be proven in numbers, the way the Scales proves things. It can't be backed by forty years of experience, the way the Adjudicator's could. I simply, standing there in the middle of that city, knew it.

---

I don't know how much time passed. To the people standing in the square it must have felt very long. It didn't, to me. To me, that time was the whole of the forty days passing at once.

I passed back through the darkness of the conduit. The feeling of water rising to my knees, the voice that had spoken aloud to no one. I had first told my own story in that darkness. Words that had begun where no one could hear were now about to end in front of a hundred twenty thousand people.

I passed back through the moment I'd opened my eyes at the well. "You're not from here, are you?" — Trope's face, asking. I had given her a false name, then. Now I stood in this city without a single lie left.

I thought of everything I'd kept hidden — that I was a herald, that I hated this city, the true reason I'd run. All of it had been piling up behind this one sentence. As if the entire forty days had been preparation for this single sentence.

*I still haven't decided whether that moment was the end of calculation, or something outside calculation altogether. I had lived my life as someone who calculates. I counted one hundred twelve cases, counted dates, counted reasons. And yet before this sentence, there was nothing left to count.*

My throat was dry. My fingertips, holding the paper, had gone slightly stiff. The square was still waiting for me. No one urging me on, and everyone waiting.

The stone beneath my feet was the same stone I had walked every day for forty days. But that day it felt unusually hard. The sun stood directly overhead. My shadow lay short, right beneath my feet.

My mouth opened.

I don't remember which came first — the voice, or the resolve. A single sentence simply went out into the square.

"This city has turned."

Only after I'd finished speaking did I realize I was the one who had said it.

I heard those six words strike the far wall of the square and return, very faintly. It was my own voice, and yet it didn't feel like mine. As though someone else, far away, were repeating it after me.

My voice wasn't loud. It was a trained voice. Quiet, but it carries far. It probably took less than a second for those six words to reach the far edge of the square. But it was enough — enough to overturn the city's whole forty days.

*I won't write more about that moment. People ask me what I was thinking then, why I said it that way. I don't answer. It isn't that I have no words. Some sentences become lies the moment you attach a reason to them. I don't want to attach a reason to that one. Not even now.*

---

For one instant, the square made no sound at all.

It took the whole square time to understand what I had just done. The Adjudicator's face moved first. He closed his eyes, and opened them again. It had probably been forty years since he'd last seen something like that.

Trope didn't move. Her lips had parted, just slightly.

The substitute herald slowly lowered the papers in his hand. For the first time, I saw something in his face give way. Not the trained face — just a person's face. He had never once been taught this sentence. Nowhere at the training grounds was there any lesson in reading it.

From the back of the square, sound began to spread like a low wave. Someone passed my words to the person beside them, and that person to the next. "It turned." Those two words took time to travel to the far edge of the square. It wasn't the speed of sound. It was the speed of belief spreading.

A low noise came from the direction of the Scales' terminal. People turned toward it. Something was appearing on the screen.

Trope stepped toward me. She started to say something, then stopped. Her lips closed again. I didn't ask what she'd meant to say. I had no confidence, then, to ask.

The baker pressed a hand to her mouth. The market merchant sat where he'd fallen, unmoving. The woman holding her child lifted the child's face up to look. "Did you hear that." she whispered to the child. The child nodded, not understanding a word of it.

*That day, in that square, I didn't understand what I had just done. I knew that a hundred twenty thousand lives had just hung on six words from my mouth, and yet I couldn't feel the weight of it. That feeling came later. Much later, and in a very different way.*

The Adjudicator walked toward me. His steps were slower than usual. He came to stand in front of me, and without a word, rested his hand on my shoulder. And then he said nothing at all.

I felt the weight of that hand. A weight the Scales could never measure.

He said nothing, and drew his hand away. I couldn't even guess what words he was holding back. I wasn't ready to hear the words of a man who had waited forty years.
