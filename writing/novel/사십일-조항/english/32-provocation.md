# Chapter 32. Provocation

> _Previously_ — Pheugon met the man again. He knew people more
> precisely than the Scales, before the Scales did, and still never
> got angry. He'd even pinpointed Trope's fear exactly, though
> Pheugon had never told him her name. That night Pheugon decided to
> test him directly.

I found him again two days later.

It wasn't hard. He walked near the market around the same time every
day. I waited for him at the mouth of an alley.

"…We meet again," he said, seeing me first. No sign of surprise.

"…I've been waiting."

"I know," he said.

That answer alone shook me a little. I tried to hold on to what I'd
rehearsed.

*Looking back now, I was already shaking before the test even began.
When the one who comes to test gets shaken before the testing starts,
that means the test itself was badly designed from the outset.*

*I'd rehearsed this conversation in my head, the night before, more
than once. What to open with, how to push, where he'd break. I was a
herald. I'd spent three years training to handle words precisely in
front of people. And I was about to use that training, for the first
time, for something other than good.*

I walked alongside him. He was in no hurry. That unhurried pace only
irritated me more.

The sky was overcast. It looked like it might rain, or might not. We
were alone in the alley. I thought that was for the best. I didn't
want anyone else to hear what I was about to say.

---

"…Sir." I said. "Last time, you said you knew what that woman had
lost."

"I did."

"I've lost something too. Guess it."

He looked at me a moment.

"…Shall I try," he said.

"My family," he said. "More precisely, the reason it fell apart. You
still only know half of that reason. You don't even know where the
other half is."

I lost my words for a moment. It was something I hadn't even told the
Adjudicator in full.

*I didn't want to admit I was surprised, in that moment. My rehearsed
script hadn't accounted for me being surprised.*

"…That's right," was all I managed.

---

"Then," I asked again, "do you know where that person is right now?"

"Who."

"The person who wrecked my family."

He fell silent at the question for a moment.

"Don't know," he said. "That, I don't know."

"You're not lying?"

"No."

I didn't like that answer. I wanted to believe he knew everything.
That was the only way my next question would work.

"…You're on the side of this city's people, aren't you," I said. A
deliberately sharpened remark. "Not getting angry, forgiving
everyone — that's all a fine story. But that fine story only works
for some people. Go say it to Trope. Do you think it'd comfort her?"

"Trope isn't looking for comfort," he said. No change in his voice.

"Then what is she looking for."

"Same as you," he said. "Someone to be angry in her place."

I lost my words for a second at that.

"…What do you mean by that."

"You've been hating the Reckoning on your family's behalf," he said.
"The anger your family couldn't raise themselves, you're raising it
for them. Trope is similar. Only the direction's reversed. You do it
by hating someone else. Trope does it by hating herself."

I wanted to argue with that, and no words came. He was too precise.
That precision, I learned that day, could render a person entirely
helpless.

---

"Then," I said. "If you're that precise. This city's people who
stole weights, cheated on rations — you'd know all of it. Name them,
one by one."

"Why."

"I'm curious," I said. My voice was already sharpening.

---

"Those are things the Scales already knows," he said. "No need for
me to say them again."

"Then tell me what the Scales doesn't know," I pushed. "This city's
people, all pretending to be good on the outside while still hoping,
inside, for the Reckoning to fall. There must be people like that.
Name them."

He looked at me a long moment at that.

"There is one," he said quietly.

My heart leapt.

"…Who."

"You."

---

I fell apart completely, in that moment.

*I hadn't prepared for that answer. I'd expected him to name someone
else. That he would point at me — I hadn't thought of that. Or maybe
I had thought of it. I just hadn't expected he'd say it so precisely,
so quietly.*

"…What would you know." My voice shook. "I want this city to live,
these days. I've been counting it, every day. How the city is
changing."

"You're counting," he said. "But counting and wanting are different.
You're still half and half. The part that wants it to live, and the
part that once wanted it to fall. Both are still in you."

"…That's not true."

"No?" He looked straight at me. "Then why did you just ask me for
someone else's name. You're still hunting for a guilty party inside
this city. So that there'd still be proof this city deserves to
fall."

I had no answer for that. It was exact. He'd said what even I hadn't
known about myself, before I did.

---

"…Doesn't that make you angry?" I asked. My voice cracked. "I was
just rude to you. I did it on purpose to test you. Doesn't that make
you angry?"

He looked at me quietly at that question.

"No," he said.

"Why not! I did it on purpose! I was trying to make you angry!"

"I know," he said. "I knew that too."

"And still?"

"And still," he said. No change in his voice.

In that moment I understood what I'd wanted. I'd wanted him to get
angry. I'd wanted him to break, the way I did. If he broke, I could
have felt at ease. If it were proven impossible not to get angry,
then what I'd endured for three years could be called an ordinary
weight, the same as everyone else's.

He didn't break.

---

I pushed harder.

"…You've never lost anything, have you," I said. I wasn't choosing my
words anymore. "That's why you can talk about forgiveness so calmly.
Someone who's actually lost something couldn't talk like that."

At that, he closed his eyes for just a moment, then opened them.

*I still remember that instant. It was a flicker. Brief, but I saw it
clearly. But it wasn't anger. I didn't know the difference then.*

"…I've lost something," he said quietly.

"What."

He didn't answer. I saw the silence as a chance to press harder.

"Not lying? Prove it. Say what you lost. If you can't say it, it was
just something you said for effect."

"There's a difference between what can be said and what should be
said," he said. "This isn't something to tell you right now."

"There you go dodging again."

"I'm not dodging."

"You are!" I shouted it. My voice echoed through the alley. A few
passersby turned to look.

---

"…Why don't you get angry?" I finally shouted.

The alley went quiet. A few people passing by turned to look. I had
no room to care.

"Why!" I said again. "I get angry. I know how hard it is, three
years, holding anger back. And you just don't, without even looking
like it costs you anything. Is that even possible? Can a person be
like that?"

He waited for my voice to settle.

"The reason I don't get angry," he said quietly, "isn't that I have
more patience than you do."

"Then what?"

"I know where you're standing, right now," he said. "And I know which
way you're walking, too. See those two things together, and there's
not much room left for anger."

"…What does that mean."

"You came here today to make me angry," he said. "That's hatred. But
underneath that hatred, what you really wanted was to see someone who
didn't collapse before you did. So you could believe you might not
have to collapse either."

---

I felt entirely stripped bare in front of that sentence.

*That was the first day I learned it. I hadn't gotten angry because
he didn't get angry. I'd gotten angry at what I saw in myself. He was
only standing there like a mirror.*

I stood there and couldn't say anything. My breath came short. My
eyes stung, and I couldn't tell if it was anger or something else.

"…What are you," I finally said. "What are you, really. Are you even
a person?"

At that question, his expression shifted, for the first time, into
something different. Something between sorrow and gladness.

"That question," he said, "isn't one to answer right now."

"Then when?"

"When you're less angry than you are now."

He turned and walked away. I watched his back for a long time. I
didn't follow. I had no strength left to follow. Raindrops began
falling one by one in the alley.

---

I stood alone in that alley for a long while.

*I'd gone to break him, and I came back broken. I was ashamed of
that. And at the same time there was something strangely light about
it. For three years I'd lived proving I could hold my anger back.
That day, for the first time, someone had seen through the fact that
I couldn't. And the world didn't end when I was caught.*

*I felt something like what I'd felt confessing to the Adjudicator. I
had expected something to fix itself then too, and been disappointed.
This time I expected nothing. And still, something, just barely,
sat a little differently.*

Walking home, I ran into Trope. She looked at my face and started to
ask something, then stopped.

"…Something happen?"

"…Yes," I said. "I lost completely today."

"To who?"

"Him. The one I told you about."

"…What did you do?"

I couldn't answer right away.

"…I tested him," I finally said. "Tried to make him angry. On
purpose."

Trope stopped walking.

"…And?"

"He didn't," I said. "The whole time. Even after everything I threw
at him."

"…Then you didn't lose. He didn't get angry — that's not you losing."

"I lost," I said. "I went to break him, and I got everything I'm
holding onto exposed instead. If that's not losing, what is."

Trope heard that and said nothing more. She just walked beside me,
step for step.

---

I saw the Adjudicator the next day.

He seemed to notice something the moment he saw my face.

"…Something happen?"

"…I met him," I said. "The man who doesn't get angry."

"Ah." The Adjudicator said it briefly. No surprise on his face.

"You know him too, Adjudicator?"

"I've seen him a few times," he said. "Thirty-one years in this
city, and you meet a handful of people you can't explain."

"What is he, exactly?"

The Adjudicator didn't answer. He just looked at me quietly instead.

"You wanted to make him angry."

I couldn't answer that.

"I don't even need to ask why you did it," he said. "It's written all
over your face."

"…What's written there."

"The face of someone who lost." He said it without smiling. It wasn't
a taunt either. "But not a bad kind of losing. There's a way to lose
like that."

I turned that over for a long time.

The Adjudicator went back to his papers. But he held the pen a long
while without writing anything. I could feel that he had something
more he wanted to say, and was holding it back. I didn't ask.

---

*I never tried to test him again after that day. There was no need
to. I already knew the answer. He wasn't a man holding his anger
back. He was a man who simply didn't get angry. I had to live it in
my own body to finally believe the difference.*

*But knowing that didn't make me more comfortable. If anything, it
made me more uncomfortable. For three years I'd believed that holding
anger back was a virtue. The moment I saw that a man who truly didn't
get angry actually existed, I understood that everything I'd done was
not virtue but simple endurance. The distance between endurance and
genuinely not getting angry was far greater than I'd ever imagined.*

*I didn't know then how to cross that distance. I still don't fully
know now. But knowing the distance existed at all was enough to make
me a different person than I'd been before that day.*

At the well, when I saw Trope again, she looked at my face for a long
while and then said briefly:

"…I'll be here tomorrow too."

I was hearing that for the third time now. I still didn't fully take
the weight of it in. I watched her back, water jar on her shoulder,
until the dark swallowed it whole.
