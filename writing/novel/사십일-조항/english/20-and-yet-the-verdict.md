# Chapter 20. And Yet the Verdict

> _Previously_ — Pheugon compared Mena's testimony against the Scales' precedent record. Mena had written "This one never explained himself"; the precedent read "This one never repented." He found a list of other rulings that had cited this wrong sentence as a reference factor, and copied down the most recent case number.

The next morning, I took that case number to the record-keeper.

"Can you find this number?" I asked.

The record-keeper looked at the number and raised an eyebrow slightly.

"This is a recent one," the record-keeper said. "Give me a moment."

The record-keeper went to another shelf and brought back a case file. A thick document. I took it and spread it on the table.

---

The case had happened here, in this city — the Reckoning.

*I went stiff in that moment. I'd vaguely assumed this would be some other city's affair. It wasn't. Right here, in this very city, to a person of this very city, that wrong quotation had actually been used.*

The first page held a summary of the case. The name was covered over. The record-keeper had explained to me once before that this city's precedent files kept the names of those involved masked over. Instead there was a case number, a date, and the charge.

A fraud case from the market. A merchant who had cheated buyers repeatedly on prices, taking advantage. Multiple victims. Weight had accumulated close to the limit.

I turned the page.

The second page held the grounds for the sentence's calculation. And in that list of grounds, the very reference case I'd been looking for. Quoted verbatim.

> …According to the referenced precedent, in similar cases where the accused "never repented to the end," the sentence tends to be increased. In this case as well, the accused refused to give a final statement, which is consistent with that tendency.

I read that sentence and my fingertips trembled. I gripped the document so hard the corner of the page crumpled.

*I was certain, in that moment. This was it. Because of this sentence, this merchant's sentence had grown heavier. The wrong quotation, "never repented to the end," had paired with this man's action of "refusing a final statement," and increased the sentence. I was watching that cause and effect with my own eyes.*

---

I kept reading through the document, right there.

Why this merchant had refused to give a final statement — the reason was noted briefly on the next page. A memo left by a legal officer.

> The accused acknowledges the act itself but refuses the statement procedure entirely. Reason unconfirmed.

I paused for breath on that sentence.

*Reason unconfirmed. I thought that phrase resembled, exactly, the situation Mena had testified to. The person Mena had met had "never explained himself, to the end" either. The reason was unknown. And here, that same reasonless silence had led straight into "never repented," and from there into a heavier sentence. I felt a chill at how naturally that chain had happened. No one would have felt anything strange about it. One sentence quietly turns into another sentence, that sentence quietly makes the punishment a little heavier, and no one ever notices.*

I decided, in that moment, that I had to bring this to the Adjudicator — or anywhere at all. If I laid this document next to Mena's original testimony, side by side, it seemed like it would prove this case's sentence had been wrong. I pictured that scene in my head several times over. Laying the two sentences out before the Adjudicator, saying, "This is wrong." I hadn't thought past that point, what would happen next. I only imagined that far, and leaned on the imagining.

*I felt happy, in that moment, for the first time in a while. Or something close to happiness, anyway. I'd spent my time in this city constantly feeling hunted. A name I had to hide, words I had to swallow, guilt that grew heavier, little by little, every day. But that moment was different. For the first time in a long while, I felt I was standing on the right side. A place where I knew more than the Scales. I didn't know, then, how briefly that place would be granted to me.*

I didn't go straight back to the shed. I read the document once more from the start, right there. This time, not to find fault, but to make the evidence more solid. I was certain this error must have affected the other cases on that list too. If one was wrong, the other rulings built on it would shake as well. I thought that shaking might reach all the way to this city's own sentence of demolition. That evening, I genuinely believed that.

*I stayed drunk on that thought for thirty minutes. I pictured not the city falling, but the city's sentence of demolition itself being voided. That the Scales' grounds were wrong, so the whole thing had to be recalculated — and then the forty days, the clause, none of it would matter anymore. I still had no idea how flimsy that picture was. I already believed I had won.*

---

I read the document again from the start. This time meaning to make sure I hadn't missed anything.

The grounds for the sentence weren't a single line. Several items were listed. I went through them one by one.

1. Damage amount — a clear figure.
2. Number of victims — seven.
3. Repeated occurrence — at least three times, same method.
4. Confession — the act acknowledged.
5. Reference precedent — the "never repented to the end" tendency.

I circled item five. This was the error I'd come for.

*I hadn't yet thought of one thing, at that moment. What items one through four, by themselves, were saying. My eyes were only on item five. That was what I'd come looking for. A person only sees clearly what he's looking for. The rest passes like background. I didn't yet know, that day, that this background wasn't background at all — it was everything.*

I kept reading. The next page showed how much weight the Scales had converted each of these five items into. I went through the numbers one by one.

Weight from the damage amount. Weight from the number of victims. Weight from repeated occurrence. And last, weight from the reference precedent.

I looked at that final number.

**It was tiny.**

---

I looked at the number again. Checked it, then checked again.

The weight added by the reference precedent item was an extremely small fraction of the total sentence. The first four items — damage amount, victims, repetition, and confession — had already brought the sentence, on their own, to almost the same point.

*I tried, in that moment, to read the fact differently. Small is still something, I told myself. An error is an error. I clung to that thought. But the more I clung to it, the larger the other number looked.*

I calculated it. What would happen if the reference precedent item were removed entirely, and the sentence recalculated from the remaining four items alone.

I knew the Scales' method of calculation. I'd learned it over three years, reading sentences like this. I copied the numbers onto paper and subtracted and added them, one at a time.

The result came out.

Almost the same.

*Only the tiniest difference remained. That difference wasn't large enough to decide whether this merchant would be pushed out of the city or not. With the reference precedent item or without it, this merchant would have received the same disposition.*

---

I sat in front of that number for a long time.

*I collapsed, in that moment. Silently, very quietly. I had come this far to prove the Scales was wrong. I believed I held that proof in my hands. But now, in front of my eyes, sat a different conclusion. The quotation was wrong. That's a fact. Mena had said "never explained himself"; the Scales had transcribed it as "never repented." That was an unmistakable error. And yet that error hadn't changed this case's outcome. Even without that error, on the strength of the other evidence alone, this merchant would have been pushed out just the same.*

*I saw, straight on, for the first time in that moment, what I had actually come here hoping for. I had wanted the Scales to be wrong. Only if the Scales were wrong could this city's fall remain a righteous thing. But the Scales hadn't been wrong. One quotation had gone bad, and that badness had changed nothing. I knew two things at once, in front of that fact. One: the crack I'd been looking for was real. The other: that crack couldn't bring anything down.*

I flipped back through the document from the beginning again. In case I'd miscalculated somewhere. However many times I checked, the same conclusion came out.

*The Scales was not wrong.*

*Not even this time.*

---

I looked at the disposition attached at the back of the document. Pushed out. A date was noted. I calculated the date. Not long ago. Very recently.

*I realized something strange, in that moment. This merchant had been pushed out while I was already in this city. I'd passed through the market every day and never once known when it happened. A person quietly disappears, and by the next day some other stall fills that empty spot. I would have walked right past that spot. Without knowing.*

I tried, for a moment, to bring back the faces I'd seen at the market. A gap between stalls — someone who'd been there a few days ago, now gone. I couldn't remember. I'd only just started learning this city's faces, not long enough yet to notice a face that had disappeared.

---

The record-keeper passed by and stopped when she saw my face.

"Are you sick?" the record-keeper asked.

"…No," I said.

"You don't look well."

"…I'm fine."

The record-keeper looked at me a moment longer, then, without saying more, went on.

*I don't know how long I sat there in that room that day. I kept running the same calculation over. Remove one item, add it back. The result was the same every time. I tried to find a way to make it come out differently. There wasn't one. The numbers were honest. The numbers didn't lie for my sake.*

---

I sat in that room late into the evening.

*I first, dimly, felt a sentence that would come to run through this whole book. What is precise and what is right are different things. But I'd been mistaken to think that sentence would help me. I'd believed that sentence took aim at the Scales. That the Scales might only be precise, and not necessarily right. But now, the document in my hands was saying the opposite. One of the Scales' grounds hadn't been precise. And yet the Scales' conclusion had been right. That those two things could run apart from each other this way — I hadn't known that until that moment.*

*I'd found the misquotation. It was real. I hadn't invented it. But that error wasn't a key that could bring this world down. It was just an error. An error that changed nothing. I found that more frightening. If the Scales had been wrong, I could have taken hold of that wrongness and done something with it. But the Scales was wrong and right at the same time. In front of that, there was nothing at all I could do.*

*I thought, for the first time, differently about what the name of the Scales meant. The Scales measures weight. If the weight is correct, the Scales is right. Deciding right and wrong wasn't the Scales' job. But I had gone on believing, all this while, that as long as the Scales was precise, that precision was rightness itself. Only holding this one document did I come to know, in my body, that the two were different.*

---

I put the document back together and returned it to the box. My hands felt heavy.

*I don't remember well the path I walked leaving that room that night. Whether I passed through the square, through the market, I'm not sure. But I remember one thing clearly. That was the first time I asked myself the real reason I'd started this investigation. I hadn't wanted to find the Scales' error. I had been looking for an excuse to hate this city again. And when that excuse collapsed this emptily, I lost the ground on which to build my hatred back up.*

---

I didn't go find Trope that evening. I went straight to the shed. Wrapped in the blanket, leaning against the wall, I called back to mind that morning's happiness. It seemed absurd. Just a few hours before, I'd believed I'd found a new reason this city would fall. Now that reason lay scattered into a few numbers in my own hands.

*I thought, for the first time, of my father's face. After the man from the Reckoning had left our house, the disposition the Scales had calculated and handed down. I had endured three years believing that disposition was precise. That old sentence — precise, and yet nothing came back — returned to me that night, wearing a different face. I now knew how precision and rightness differed. But that knowledge gave me no comfort at all. Quite the opposite. If the Scales was precise and right at the same time, then I had nowhere left to lay the blame on the Scales. Whatever I could blame now had to be something other than the Scales.*

*I couldn't name what that something was, that night. I only sat in the dark for a long time. A darkness of the same kind as the darkness of the conduit that had trapped me for three days. Back then, water had risen to my knees; this time, nothing rose at all — and yet I felt just as short of breath.*

*And it was out of that darkness, still not having found my way clear of it, that I would meet the Adjudicator for the first time.*

*Looking back now, everything I truly needed to learn in this book was already there, that night. I believed that if I proved the Scales wrong, something would come loose. It took a single day to learn that belief was wrong. But what I truly lost, in that one day, wasn't evidence. It was the ground on which to build my hatred. Only after losing that ground did I understand how heavily I had been leaning on it, all along.*
