# Chapter 8. The Gate Closes

> _Previously_ — Pheugon walked for hours through the dark conduit, with no
> way to gauge direction or time. Once he sensed someone's presence but
> could never confirm it. He passed the first sluice gate, standing open,
> and felt a brief relief. He didn't know yet that the relief was
> misplaced.

I met the second sluice gate a long while after that.

By then I was tired enough to have forgotten the difference between walking and standing still. My legs didn't feel like my own. I'd lost count of how many hours I'd dragged my feet through that water. I'd stopped counting long before.

*I still remember that exhaustion in my body. Not a muscle ache — an ache in the bone. A kind of tired that four hours of daily reading at the training hall had never taught me.*

At the training hall, the hard part was my throat. Here it was my legs, my back, my fingertips. I learned that day, for the first time, that a whole body can grow tired — not any one part of it, but the fact of having a body at all.

The current kept pushing at my calves. My body got no rest even standing still. I had to keep bracing against that current just to stay upright.

---

This gate was different from the first.

Shut. Completely.

An enormous steel plate blocked the tunnel. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall. Barely a gap. Water only slipped through a small crack at the bottom.

I stood in front of it and felt it with my hand. Cold, slick. The same iron wheel as the first gate's was mounted here too. I tried to turn it. It didn't move.

*I threw everything I had into turning it, that time. It didn't budge. Looking back, of course it didn't. That wheel wasn't built to be turned by hand. I didn't know that yet, and because I didn't know it, I kept trying. Trying something you know can't be done, and trying something without knowing it can't, are two different things. I was the latter.*

I pushed at the wheel with both hands, then with my shoulder. Again and again. The inside of my palms scraped raw. The wheel didn't so much as tremble. It sat there like part of the wall itself, as if it had never been a thing meant to turn at all.

Far off, I heard that same low mechanical hum again. This time it felt a little closer. Regular, and indifferent.

*Hearing that sound, a strange certainty settled in me. Whatever was making that sound had no idea I existed. I thought about calling out toward it, and decided not to. No one shouts at a machine. I already knew, by instinct, that it would be useless.*

---

I tried shouting.

"…Hello!"

My voice went out and came back. Bounced back and forth so many times that by the end it barely sounded like my voice at all. No one answered.

I called again. Louder this time. Until my throat felt like it was tearing.

Only my own voice came back.

*I thought of the training hall then, for the first time. The room where my own voice returned to me. The supervisor said it was so I could grow used to it. Used to it. That out there, no one answers. I had only ever taken that as training for reading. That it was also how to live — I learned that for the first time, in front of this gate.*

*It wasn't only true for reading. There are moments in living, too, when no one answers. The supervisor probably never meant to teach that far. But the training ended up carrying me here, all the same.*

I sank down where I stood.

I thought of turning back. Retracing my steps, I thought I could find my way to the first station. But in that moment I realized. I didn't remember how many forks I'd passed.

*That was the quietest moment in this whole chapter. No screaming, no tears. Just, quietly, the fact settling into place — that I didn't know the way back. Some fears make no sound. They just take their seat as fact.*

I retraced my steps a little. Twenty paces, and another fork appeared. I couldn't tell whether it was the same fork I'd passed before. Every fork looked exactly alike.

I came back to the gate.

The station — I'd decided to call that wide space that. From there, at least three paths branched off. I couldn't be certain of any of them. Retracing my steps might carry me deeper in, not out.

*In that moment I made a strange decision. To stop moving at all. Better to stay in one place and wait for the door to open than to wander further astray by choosing wrong. I still don't know if that decision was right. But making it, strangely, I felt easier. Having nothing left to choose was, somehow, a relief.*

I settled into the shallow water right in front of the gate. Back against the wall, knees drawn up, and sat.

The light leaking from the crack above seemed to dim a little at a time. It might have been my imagination. But watching that change, I felt for the first time that time was still passing, even in this dark. I simply had no way to measure it.

*I stared at that light for a long time. Whether the sun was setting, or something else had drifted across that gap, I couldn't tell. But watching it grow fainter, I felt real fear for the first time. Not losing my way. Not the gate being shut. It was that time was flowing on, leaving me behind. That was the most frightening thing.*

---

*I stop, every time I write this part. From here I want to make excuses. That this was an accident, that I didn't know, that I'd never have come if I'd known when this gate closed. All of it is true. But being true doesn't make it an excuse.*

*I wondered, at the time, why this gate was shut. I didn't think anyone had closed it to stop me. There was no reason to think that. No one knew I was there. I just thought I'd had bad luck.*

*I was wrong. It wasn't bad luck. This was simply the schedule.*

---

A plate was fixed to the gate. Letters engraved into metal. I read it by the faint light off the wall.

**Scheduled Closure. Alternating Duty Section.**

Below that, in smaller letters, a few numbers. I guessed they must indicate times. What time it closed, what time it opened. But not knowing what time it was now made those numbers useless to me.

*I ran my hand over that plate again and again, as if doing so might change the numbers. In that moment I understood, for the first time. This door hadn't shut to punish me. This door simply closed at this hour, always. I had only, unluckily, happened to be right here at that hour.*

*For a long time I couldn't tell punishment from accident apart. Punishment is something someone hands down after seeing you. An accident happens without anyone seeing you at all. I'd thought the two were the same, in outcome — either way, I was trapped in the water. I know now. They're different, from the inside. Someone being punished at least knows why they're there. Someone in an accident doesn't even have that. I learned, in front of that gate, that there's something harder to bear than punishment.*

*I later learned the details of how this sluice worked, from someone else. Every city joined by this waterway, including the Reckoning, shares the same schedule. While one section is open, another must always be closed. Otherwise one city floods and another runs dry. So this gate opens and shuts several times a day. Someone like me getting caught in that gap couldn't have been the first time. I'd simply had one of the ordinary things happen to me.*

*That fact made me feel smaller still. I'd wanted, somewhere, to believe I'd been singled out by especially bad luck. That would at least have made this whole thing into a story that made sense. But it wasn't. I was just an ordinary case. The world hadn't recognized and imprisoned me — it hadn't recognized me at all, and had simply gone on running as usual. And I happened to be inside that usual.*

*I thought of the Reckoning's sentence. A hundred and twenty thousand people. Maybe that city was going through something similar, I thought, for the first time. The Scales hadn't measured that city's weight because it hated it. It simply measures. Cross the limit, a disposition follows. That too is scheduled operation. In that moment I felt, faintly, that being trapped in this gate and the sentence handed down to that city were the same kind of thing. Neither had happened because anyone hated anyone. That thought gave me no comfort at all.*

*These cities' waterways run on an exact schedule. Some sections open, some close, so the water never overflows, and each city gets exactly what it needs. That schedule had no place in it for something like me. No reason it should. While the Scales was weighing a hundred and twenty thousand lives, this gate weighed nothing at all and simply closed on its own time. It was the first rule I'd met, in this world, that had no idea who I was.*

---

I stared at that plate for a long while.

A strange thought came to me. My whole life, I'd always been inside some rule or another. The Scales's rules. Herald training's rules. The supervisor's rules. Every one of them had seen me. They weighed my weight, they judged my voice, they wrote my name on a list.

This gate did not see me.

*And that was more frightening still. Up to then, I'd always believed punishment was more frightening than being ignored. I learned that day. Being ignored is more frightening. Punishment at least knows who you are, before it falls. This door didn't know who I was. Had no need to.*

Water kept flowing past my ankles, ceaselessly, through that narrow gap. That sound of water was the only regular thing left in this place.

I listened to it and thought of the Scales. The Scales sees everything. It weighs one lie, one stolen loaf of bread, down to the last exact figure. This waterway had no such eye. No one was weighing me here. No one was recording that I existed here at all.

*I felt a strange relief and a strange terror, both at once, in that moment. The relief was this — nothing here was being added to my weight. The terror was this — nothing here was on my side, either. When the Scales was weighing me, I at least knew someone was counting my existence. In front of this door, I didn't even have that.*

---

I leaned my back against the wall. My legs had no strength left in them at all.

I set my bag on my knees. The wax envelope wasn't wet yet. I checked, and put it back in the bag.

*Even in that moment, I was worried about that document. Laughable, thinking about it now. Sitting in a place where I might drown, I was checking whether a piece of paper that held a city's fate had gotten wet or not. The human mind, even before the largest things, reaches first for the smallest.*

I checked the flask. Half full. Five loaves of bread remained.

*I began counting numbers again, then, for the first time. I wrote in the last chapter that I'd stopped. That wasn't true. I hadn't stopped — I'd only paused. The moment I sat down in front of that gate, I went back to being someone who counted. There was truly nothing else left for me to do, by then.*

How much water was there. How many days would the bread last. When would this gate open again. How many days left until proclamation day.

I set those questions up in order and began filling in the answers, one by one.

The first question had an answer. Half a flask. The second had one too. Five loaves of bread, one a day — five days. The third question had no answer. I had no way to read the numbers stamped on that metal plate. The fourth had no exact answer either. I didn't even know how long I'd been walking.

*It was then I noticed, for the first time, that I was setting unanswerable questions beside answerable ones. I solved the ones with answers first. The flask, the bread. Those were easy. I left the unanswerable ones standing as questions. When does this gate open. How many days are left until proclamation day. I didn't throw those questions away. I just set them aside. Like someone who believes an answer will come, eventually.*

My body shook. I'd been in the water too long. I pulled myself further up, out of the water, toward the wall. The stone floor was damp too, but better than the water.

*That night — though I didn't even know if it was night — I tried to sleep. I closed my eyes. The sound of water filled my ears entirely. I drifted in and out. I don't know how many times. That night I learned, for the first time, that some things can't even be counted.*

Once, waking, I forgot for a moment where I was. I thought I was in my own bed and started to turn over, before the touch of the stone floor startled me fully awake. Those few seconds of mistake hurt worse than anything else that had happened that day.

*It was a brief moment, but I remember it. Those few seconds of mistaking this for home. That became, for a long time after, the most vivid moment of everything I went through. A person knows exactly where they are, most precisely, in the instant their most comfortable illusion breaks.*

I closed my eyes again. The sound of water hadn't changed. That something, at least, stayed constant felt, strangely, like a comfort, in that moment.
