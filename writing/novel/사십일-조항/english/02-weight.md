# Chapter 2. Weight

> _Previously_ — Pheugon was the trainee who best mastered reading without
> feeling. He became a full herald at fourteen and read a hundred and twelve
> cases over three years, not one of them a misjudgment. As autumn begins,
> the supervisor assigns him a whole-city sentence of demolition — the
> Reckoning. Hearing the name, Pheugon is startled that his hands do not
> shake.

The Reckoning isn't the only city that uses the Scales.

Every city in this region uses it. They differ only in size and precision. Ours was a small one. Three terminals in the square, two at the market's edge, one in the proclamation office corridor. Six in all, measuring the weight of the whole city's people, in real time.

I'd heard that the limit differs from city to city. Some cities have high limits, tolerant of most wrongs. Some have low ones, where a handful of small lies is enough to push a person out. I didn't know then which the Reckoning's limit was. I learned later — the Reckoning had a high limit. And still it was crossed.

People put a hand up to the terminal. A number appears on the screen. That's all. The Scales doesn't explain. It doesn't tell you why this number, why it rose or fell since yesterday. It only gives the number.

*As a child I thought that was unkind. I think differently now. The Scales doesn't explain because it isn't unkind. It has nothing to explain. A number is a number. A number has no reason.*

---

No one knows exactly how weight is assigned. The calculation happens inside the Scales, and there's no way to bring that process out in a form a person can see. We can only guess from the result.

One lie is worth roughly this much. Theft, heavier. Striking someone, heavier still. Weight has units, though no one bothers with what to call them. It's just a number. Low is good, high is bad. That's all, and that's all this city knows.

When accumulated weight crosses an individual's limit, that person is pushed out of the city. Pushed out isn't quite the right word. No one lays a hand on them. Everything that person used — the house, the job, the market pass, the ration — simply stops, one thing at a time. I learned very young that the Scales can push a person out of a city without ever touching them.

When a whole city's accumulated weight crosses the city's limit, a sentence of demolition falls on the city itself. That's rare. Individual cases come out every month, but a whole-city case, as the supervisor said, comes once a decade, if that.

*The Reckoning was that rare case. The day I was assigned it, I didn't grasp how rare it was. I understood only after I stood in that city myself.*

---

I remember the day school first taught us about weight. I was seven.

The teacher lined us up in front of a terminal. Had us put our hands up one at a time. When the number came up, the other children all saw it. Some laughed. Some cried. It wasn't that the crying children's numbers were especially high. They were just afraid of having their number shown in front of everyone.

"This isn't a punishment." the teacher said. "It's just knowing. Knowing where you stand right now."

I believed her. For a long time.

*I know now. It was a punishment. In a world with no line between knowing and being punished, "this isn't a punishment" wasn't comfort — it was already, in itself, a piece of the punishment.*

The teacher taught us how to keep our numbers low. Don't lie. Don't touch what isn't yours. Don't get angry. That was how the school told us to be good. Not goodness. A figure.

I was the child who learned it best.

---

From around twelve, I checked my weight every day.

If you asked why, I had no real answer. I called it habit. It wasn't habit. I checked every day because I wanted to see the number hadn't dropped — no, not dropped, hadn't risen. I wanted to confirm it was staying low.

I tried not to lie. In ambiguous situations, I just said nothing at all. I never touched anyone's belongings. If my hand brushed something, I pulled it back. When something made me angry, I kept my mouth shut. Keeping it shut enough, the occasions for anger themselves grew fewer. No — not fewer. I simply stopped calling it anger.

My weight was the lowest in my cohort. Low enough that the supervisor checked it, and checked it again.

The other trainees thought it was just my nature. That I was good, and that was why. Whenever I heard that, I said nothing. Saying nothing was, for me, the easiest answer there was.

Had was different. Had was afraid to check his own number. Every time he passed the terminal in the training hall corridor, he looked away on purpose.

"Aren't you scared?" Had asked me once. We were passing the terminal together.

"…Of what."

"Seeing the number. I hold my breath every time I walk past it."

I didn't answer. I didn't know it then. Had thought I wasn't afraid, but in truth I was more afraid than he was. Only the direction of the fear was different. Had was afraid his number would rise. I was afraid that something was wrong even though mine was low.

*I had no words for that difference then. I have them now. Had was afraid of what he had already done. I was afraid of what I hadn't done yet. I already knew I would do it, someday.*

---

The morning after I received the Reckoning's sentence, I passed the terminal of the Scales as I always did.

I put my hand up. A number appeared on the screen.

Low. As always.

*I felt reassured looking at that number. But it was a strange kind of reassurance, I know now. I wasn't reassured because I was a good person. I was reassured because I hadn't done anything yet. I didn't know that the word "yet" was already inside that reassurance.*

People were lined up at the market's terminal. A morning scene, every day. Hand up, look at the number, move on with a smile or with no expression at all. Smiling is rare. Mostly people just check. Low, and they're relieved; high, and they're careful.

Near the front of the line stood a stranger, a man. He put up his hand and a fairly high number came up. A few of the people behind him quietly stepped back. The man lowered his hand without a word and turned away. No one held him. No one pushed him either. People just spread out, little by little.

*That was how this city punished people. No shouting, no hands laid on. Just a slow stepping-back. I never saw that man in the market again, after that day.*

*I didn't think of the people stepping back as cowards, back then. I'd have done the same. There was a rumor that standing near someone with high weight for too long could somehow affect your own weight. No one knew if it was true. There was no way to check. And yet people acted as if they believed it. Not because they believed it — because behaving as if they did was safer.*

I caught the eye of the old vegetable seller as I passed the line. Every morning she stood at the Scales for a long while, staring at it. Even though the number never changed.

"…How's it look today." I asked, passing, as a kind of greeting.

"Same." she said. "Same every day. That's the frightening part."

I let it pass. I didn't understand it then.

*I understand now. What frightened her wasn't a number going up. It was the fact that a number never comes down. The Scales only knows how to add weight. It doesn't know how to subtract. No one has any way to lower their own weight themselves. However much time passes, however well you live, the number stays exactly where it was set. The Scales has no function for erasing a past wrong. Only for adding.*

*I thought that was only natural, back then. Wrongs don't disappear. I think differently now. That a wrong doesn't disappear, and that a wrong cannot disappear — those are two different claims. The Scales never distinguished between them. Not that it had no wish to distinguish — it had no way to.*

---

I should say a little about my family. Not all of it — only as much as I can now.

I knew everyone's number. All low. Everyone in my house lived carefully, like me. Whether we were careful because our numbers were low, or our numbers were low because we were careful, I still don't know.

There was exactly one exception.

*I won't tell that person's story here. Not yet. I'll only write this — because of that one person's number, the rest of our low numbers stopped meaning anything. The Scales calculated each of us separately. For each of us, separately, the calculation was correct. But as a household, as a unit, that calculation explained nothing at all.*

*The Scales measures individuals. It doesn't know how to measure a family. I learned that too, only later.*

After that, every time I checked my number, I found myself picturing that person's number alongside it. Laid side by side, one gave comfort and the other didn't. But to the Scales, those were simply two numbers sitting next to each other. The low one doesn't offset the high one. The Scales doesn't do that kind of arithmetic.

*I think I wanted, for a long time, to believe that my low number could pay something back. Even though no such arithmetic exists anywhere.*

---

In our last year of training, we studied demolition procedure. Not the individual kind — the city kind. No one had actually seen one. We only learned it from the textbook.

The textbook read like this. Once a sentence takes effect, that city's connections to the Scales are severed, one at a time, in order. Rations stop first, then passes, and last, residency itself is voided. People have to relocate to another city within a set period. Those who don't must live on land without the Scales, however that land lives.

The textbook then added: "In some cases, no city will take them in." No explanation followed that sentence. Just a line break.

I remember staring at that line break for a long time.

*I thought, then, that sentence had nothing to do with me. Just one of the hundreds of sentences in the textbook. Three years later, I would be standing in front of real people, reading that sentence with my own voice. I had no idea, back then.*

The teacher also asked us this: "If a city is demolished entirely, what happens to the infants inside it?" No one could answer. The teacher didn't answer either. She just turned the page.

*I forgot that question for three years. It came back to me the night I received the Reckoning's sentence. For the first time, briefly, I tried to think of an answer. I stopped before I found one. I think I didn't want to find it.*

---

Walking to the proclamation office, I reached into my bag and touched the Reckoning's sentence document again.

I didn't know exactly how much a city's accumulated weight had to reach before it crossed the limit. The supervisor hadn't given me the precise figure either. He'd only said, "it crossed."

A hundred and twenty thousand people had each added a little, and it had come to that number. Some a great deal, some very little. The Scales adds it all together. Only adds. It doesn't know how to subtract.

*That morning I had a thought for the first time. Of a hundred and twenty thousand people, how many stood every morning at a terminal, checking their own number, the way I did. Did they too know that number could never come down.*

*Thinking about it, I felt strangely nothing. Standing before a hundred and twenty thousand faceless numbers, I thought only that the city was getting what it deserved. How cleanly, how casually that thought had settled inside me — I had no idea, at the time.*

At the front gate of the proclamation office, I stopped for a moment before going back in.

There was a terminal by the door too. I didn't raise my hand. I'd already checked that morning. But there was another reason too.

*Standing at that terminal, I felt afraid to see a number for the first time. I hadn't done anything yet. Hadn't even decided to run. My body seemed to already know. That I was about to enter a different kind of relationship with that number.*

*Up to that point, in fifteen years of living, I had never once doubted the Scales. Never once thought it could be wrong. That morning, I still believed exactly that. The Scales is right. Entirely right. The problem wasn't the Scales. The problem was what the Scales's being right could make me do.*

Our city has a totalizer board too. A large board mounted on the wall beside the proclamation office, showing the whole city's accumulated weight as a single number. People don't usually look at it. Their own number is already more than enough to carry, without minding the whole city's.

That morning, for the first time, I stood in front of it and looked at our city's number. Far from the limit. Safe.

*I thought the Reckoning must have a board like this too. The people there must have stood in front of it every morning, checking not their own number but the whole city's. I couldn't imagine, then, what that would feel like. I only understood after I stood in front of one myself — that standing before a board like that, and watching it creep closer to the limit day by day, are two entirely different things.*

I just opened the door and went in.
