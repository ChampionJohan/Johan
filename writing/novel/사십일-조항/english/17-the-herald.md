# Chapter 17. The Herald

> _Previously_ — Trope's mother had exceeded the weight limit and been pushed out of the city five years before. Trope didn't resent the Scales for it — "Because it was right." That sentence unsettled Pheugon for a long time; he'd never once been able to say the same about his own family's ruin, and for the first time he noticed he was becoming, little by little, a person of this city.

Starting the next day, I took on one more job.

Trope hadn't asked for it. The old man had asked first. "Could use another pair of young hands." Carrying goods for the market stalls. I said I would. No other reason. It beat sitting in the shed just counting numbers.

*I thought, at the time, that this was just a way to pass the hours. I know now. It wasn't. Doing that work, I began learning the names of this city's people, one by one. Learning a name means lifting a person out of a number into a person. I did that work every day without understanding, for a long while, what it really was.*

---

The market work was simple. Helping set up the stalls in the morning, carrying goods, helping close up in the evening. The merchants knew I was a stranger, but within a few days they stopped minding much.

"Had." The vegetable seller called my name. Very naturally attached to me by now. "Move that sack over there."

I still reacted half a beat late every time someone called that name. Exactly as Trope had once pointed out. But lately that half-beat was shrinking, little by little. I thought it was only becoming familiar.

*It wasn't that. It wasn't familiarity — it was dye taking. I didn't know the difference then. Growing familiar means adjusting only on the surface. Being dyed means that name becoming real from the inside out. I was becoming Had, little by little, every day, and Pheugon was receding by that same measure.*

---

About a week into working the market, something like an incident happened.

There was a wooden board at the market entrance where the day's notices were posted. Well maintenance, ration schedules, things like that. That day an official came to post a new notice, but the person who usually read it aloud wasn't there.

"My throat's gone," the usual reader said beside the official, his voice hoarse. "I won't be able to read for a few days."

People had gathered. Everyone seemed curious what the notice said. The official looked briefly at a loss, then glanced around.

"Anyone here who can read?"

No one stepped forward readily. The people of this city didn't seem to enjoy standing up to represent something. I too stood still, up to that point.

Then a paper crossed in front of my eyes.

"You." The official looked at me. "You're young — you can read, can't you?"

I froze.

*In that moment, I thought two things at once. One: no. The moment my voice slipped out, three years of training would show plainly. The way of not stopping, not catching, not holding my breath at a name. If I read in a way this city's people didn't know, someone was sure to ask what it was.*

*The other thought was scarier. In that moment, I already wanted to read. I wanted to hold the paper in my hands. I'd gone too long without doing the thing I'd done every day for three years.*

I took the paper.

---

The content was nothing much. A notice that the west distribution station's hours were changing. A sentence composed by the Scales. Short and dry.

I took a breath. Then I read.

Without stopping. Without catching. Straight through.

Looking up when I finished, a few people were watching me with strange eyes. The official was one of them.

"…That's good reading," the official said. "Where'd you learn that?"

"…Nowhere," I said. "I've just read a lot."

*Not a lie. But that not-quite-a-lie answer was, in its own way, a bigger lie. I was someone who had read a hundred and twelve cases. Shrinking that down to "I've read a lot" — in that very moment, I was erasing the weight of what I'd done, myself.*

The official didn't ask further. I was relieved.

"Mind doing it again sometime?" the official asked. "Just until his throat's better."

I hesitated over my answer.

*I should have refused, then. I know now. I had a hundred reasons to refuse. And yet I agreed. Why, I couldn't properly explain to myself, even that night.*

"…All right," I said.

---

From then on, roughly every three days I stood in front of that board. I read the notices. Ration times, well maintenance, changes to market rules. All trivial sentences. And yet every time I read those trivial sentences, I felt something loosening, little by little, somewhere deep inside my body.

*I didn't know what it was, at the time. I know now. I'd been trained into someone who couldn't live without using his voice. Reading a hundred and twelve cases, I'd thought that training had turned me from a person into a tool. But in this city, in a place where no one knew me as a herald, I began using that voice again. And when I found that the voice was still mine, I felt strangely relieved.*

*I wasn't a tool. I was just someone who happened to be good at reading. I hadn't known until then how much that distinction mattered.*

One day the old man was listening to me read and said.

"Your voice, it's good," he said, leaning on his cane. "No anger in it, no rushing. Just pleasant to listen to."

I had no answer to that.

"Where'd you learn to read like that?"

*I nearly gave him the true answer, standing in front of that question. At the herald training grounds, I nearly said. The words rose to my throat and went back down. I swallowed it that day too. But the swallowing felt different from before. Before, I'd swallowed it out of fear of being caught. That day — I'm not entirely sure, even now. Maybe it was a swallowing born of wanting to say it.*

"…Just, ever since I was little," I said.

The old man nodded. He didn't ask further.

After that, people started coming to me first whenever there was something to read. I read distribution slips aloud for old people who couldn't read them, looked over contracts brought by unfamiliar merchants. Each time, I walked a little further into this city.

*I thought this was kindness. Helping people who needed it. I know now. It wasn't just kindness. I was still doing, in the city I hated most, the thing I did best. Every time I did it, people trusted me a little more, and the more of that trust I received, the less able I became to go far from this place.*

---

That week, one evening, the old man invited me to his table. Trope was there, along with the woman who couldn't see well and a couple of other neighbors. A small courtyard, a single table, everyone gathered around it.

"Sit, Had," the old man said. "There's extra food."

I first tried to decline. But Trope had already set a bowl in my place. There was nothing to do but sit.

The meal was quiet. This city's people rationed their words even at meals. But the quiet wasn't awkward. It was, instead, comfortable. I found that unfamiliar. Where I came from, someone's voice was always raised over the table. Here there was only the sound of spoons.

"Does Had have family?" one neighbor asked. A question tossed out without much thought.

I stopped mid-spoonful.

*It was probably the simplest question at that table, that evening. Something everyone else would have let pass without a thought. Not for me. In front of that question, I thought of my house all at once, of the man from the Reckoning, of my scattered brother. And I swallowed all of it.*

"…I'm alone," I said. This time, not a lie.

"Ah," the neighbor said. "That's how you drifted all the way here, then."

I didn't answer. Trope glanced at me and looked back at her bowl. As if trying to change the subject. But someone else spoke up first.

"Why'd you come to this city?" the old man asked. No ill intent. He always asked that way.

I sat there with no answer prepared.

*It was at that table that I felt, for the first time, that lying to these people wasn't easy anymore. When I'd first told Trope "Had," there'd been no weight to it at all. Just a fake name thrown at a strange child. Lying now, at this table, in front of people who'd sat me down to dinner — that was an entirely different weight.*

"…Passing through," I managed to say. "Just ended up staying."

The old man nodded. He didn't press further. No one did. This city's people respected each other that way. I thought I had no right to receive that respect — and yet I received it.

*I would remember that table for a long time. The clink of spoons, the old man's laugh, the sound Trope made clearing the bowls. Listening to those sounds, I thought, for the first time: if this city disappears, these sounds disappear too. I couldn't hold onto that thought for long. It felt wrong to hold onto.*

---

Nearly a month passed, and I gained a quiet place among this city's people. Had, who helps with Trope's errands. Had, who helps with errands at the Scales. Had, who reads the notices. People started attaching "that young man" instead of just "him" after my name. It meant becoming someone whose face was known.

*I only thought that was good, at the time. I believed I was succeeding at hiding. But hiding was supposed to mean disappearing. Instead of disappearing, I kept getting filled in. A spot for me was slowly opening up in this city's fabric.*

*Later, much later, I would learn how dangerous those weeks had been. What I'd hidden was one name. But while hiding that name, I failed to hide something much larger. Myself. I had blended into this city, and someone who's blended in can no longer see that city as a single number. I didn't know that then. Nor did I know it would become the thing that hurt me most, later.*

---

That evening, I stood a long while in front of the Scales terminal for the first time. I didn't raise my hand. I just stood, looking at that smooth surface.

*I tried, that night, to bring back why I'd come to this city. The Reckoning. The sentence of demolition. Article Four, Clause Eleven. But those sentences no longer surfaced as vividly as before. Instead, the old man's voice, the vegetable seller's name, the way Trope hoisted a water jar onto her shoulder — things like that kept pushing into their place.*

*I was afraid of that. I'd come to hate this city. But to hate, I needed a faceless mass. Once faces began to appear one by one, hatred no longer stood as easily as it used to.*

*I first heard, that night, of a place called the room of records. The old man mentioned it in passing. "If you want to know why the Scales judges the way it does, go to the room of records behind the square. There's something called precedent, I hear, but I don't know much about it myself." The moment I heard that, I felt like I'd found something to hold onto.*

*Thinking back on it now, that wasn't an accident. I was afraid of myself, starting to be dyed into this city, and I was looking for a place to set the Scales up as my enemy again. The room of records was exactly the refuge I needed.*

---

That night, before I fell asleep, I took out the sentence document I'd buried deep in my bag. It had been a long time. The oiled paper still bore the marks of water, but the lettering was intact.

The Reckoning. Sentence of demolition. Forty days.

I sat holding that paper for a long while.

*I felt something strange in that moment. This paper was still the reason I'd fled. But that reason no longer burned the way it used to. I couldn't tell whether it had simply dulled, or was turning into something else. I know now. It hadn't dulled. I was already, by then, holding two feelings at once. The wish that this city would fall, and the wish to sit again at the old man's table. I learned for the first time, that day, that both feelings could live in the same person.*

I wrapped the paper back in the oiled cloth and put it in the bag. Then I lay down.

*I decided, that night, to go to the room of records the next morning. With the intention of finding a crack to bring the Scales down. But beneath that intention was another one I hadn't quite looked into myself. By finding that crack, I wanted to find a reason to hate this city again. A city that kept growing faces — I wanted to turn it back into a number.*

*That night I didn't know how differently those two feelings weighed. One was logic. The other was fear. That what I believed was logic was actually something fear had ordered — I wouldn't learn that until much, much later.*
