# Chapter 38. The Proclamation

> _Previously_ — The whole city gathered in the square. The substitute herald was already standing there, but Pheugon stepped forward in his place. The Adjudicator nodded. Pheugon unfolded the paper.

The first thing you learn when you learn to read is not your voice. It's not stopping.

I had carved this into my body for three years. Not stopping at a name. Not stopping at a charge. Not stopping in front of someone crying. That day in the square, I had no idea where that training was about to take me.

*I still think about this sometimes. What would have happened if I'd been someone who knew how to stop. If I had stopped, I would have stopped in front of that sentence. Because I didn't stop, I made it all the way to that sentence.*

The hand that unfolded the paper felt strangely light. Forty days before, the first time I'd seen this paper on my desk, my fingertips had trembled. Now they didn't. The trained hand had come back. I was glad of that, and afraid of it, at once.

At the training grounds, the supervisor used to say: "A herald's hand should feel only the weight of the paper. It must not feel the weight of the person written inside it." I'd believed that for a long time. That day in the square, I learned for the first time that both weights could be felt in the same hand at once.

---

"The Reckoning." I began.

My voice carried out across the square. It wasn't a loud voice. A trained voice never is. Quiet, but it carries far.

"This settlement, under Article 3, has been found to exceed its accumulated weight limit. The date of judgment was—"

I read the date. It was the same day, forty-one days ago, that I had first held this sentence document in my hands. That day I had laughed, silently, in the middle of a hallway. I never imagined that laugh would lead to this square.

The square was silent. Silent, but the silence was full of held breath. That's the sound a hundred twenty thousand people make when they hold their breath together. For the first time, I felt that number as people.

The people far in the back couldn't have heard my voice well. And yet no one moved. I found that strange. If you couldn't hear, you'd think you'd push forward. But everyone stayed exactly where they stood, as if watching the shape of my mouth was enough, without needing to hear the sound. Maybe they already knew what this sentence meant. Forty days had given them plenty of time to guess.

*I learned to count when I was twelve, so I could handle numbers. That day, for the first time, a number handled me.*

---

I read the grounds.

They were the items the Scales had produced. Lies, theft, neglect, cruelty. Each one converted into a number. It even listed when the city's accumulated total had crossed the limit. Three years and six months earlier.

At that date, my voice nearly wavered. Three years and six months ago wasn't far from the time my own family had come apart.

*I wanted to believe that was a coincidence. Even now, I can't fully believe it. The Scales never connected those two events. It had no reason to. The one who connected them was me.*

My voice didn't waver. Three years of training saved me, in that moment. Or so I thought at the time.

The items went on and on. I read every one of them, without dropping a single line. The Scales wrote in numbers only, but I knew there was a person behind each number. Behind a line reading "neglect item, accumulated total twenty-three thousand," there was probably a child. Days without food, greetings no one ever offered. The Scales reduced all of that to a single number. I was the one who turned that number back into a voice.

Beside me, the substitute herald stood with the document in his hand, watching me. Something passed across his eyes. I understood only later what it was. It wasn't relief. It was awe. He wasn't looking at me and thinking he was lucky not to be the one standing there — he was looking at himself, and seeing that he hadn't been trained enough to stand where I stood.

*I would go on to remember his face for a long time. Forty days earlier, I would have stood in that place with that same face. Unshaken, uninvolved. Whether losing that face was fortunate or a loss, I still haven't decided.*

---

At last I reached the disposition.

"Regarding this settlement: following a grace period of forty days from the date of judgment, the sentence of demolition shall be executed."

At that instant, a sound rose from the square. Weeping. Not from one person, but from many at once. The sound of breath, held back too long, breaking all at once. A woman holding a child pressed the child's head to her chest. Two old men gripped each other harder.

I didn't stop.

I looked at Trope. She wasn't crying. Her face was different from everyone else's. It was a face that had already expected this sentence. From forty days before — or perhaps from long before that.

*Watching that face, I felt a strange certainty settle in me. A certainty that Trope was the least surprised person in that whole city to hear this sentence. She had already believed, for a long time, that she deserved exactly this.*

Close beside me, the baker had both hands pressed over her face. The market merchant sank to the ground, still clutching the scale-weight he'd calibrated himself. Someone let out a low groan, calling out not a person's name but the city's. "Reckoning." Someone said it that way — as if the city were a person, as if to comfort it.

I heard those sounds and did not let them break my voice. That's what the supervisor had taught — the discipline of not listening. But I was listening. Listening while pretending not to — that day, for the first time, I truly put that skill to use.

---

Maybe I should have stopped there. Most people do stop there. Once the disposition is read, the proclamation is considered finished. I had heard, back at the training grounds, that the clauses attached below were often left unread by custom.

I didn't stop.

Because I had learned how not to stop. How not to stop at a name, a charge, a person crying. There was no exception to that discipline. As long as there were letters left on the page, I read them. That was who three years had made me.

*I still return to this moment sometimes. If I had been someone less trained — if I hadn't been, as the supervisor put it, someone who "doesn't stop" — I would have folded the paper right there. The city would simply have fallen. The clause would have gone back to being a dead letter.*

*The very thing that had made me hate this city was, in the end, what carried me to this place. And now the same discipline that had once made me run — that same discipline was keeping me from stopping.*

I thought of Had, at the training grounds. The boy who stopped three times at his own name. "Why don't you stop?" Had had asked me that once, and in that instant, standing in the middle of the square, his question came back to me. I hadn't been able to answer then, and I couldn't now. Only this time, I kept reading without an answer.

*Had stopped at a name and never became a herald. I never stopped at anything, and became one. In that square that day, I suddenly envied him. At least he'd known how to stop.*

I turned the page. On the next line, in small type.

**Article 4, Clause 11.**

---

I had seen those letters once before, forty days earlier. At my desk, alone, in an empty room at night, tracing them with my fingertip. That time I had been alone. Now I stood before a hundred twenty thousand people.

That night, I'd read this sentence three times. I never asked who was meant by "confirmed" — by whom. I'd assumed the Scales would confirm it. I should have already known, even then. The Scales cannot measure direction. If the Scales couldn't confirm it, confirmation had to belong to someone else. That night, the answer had been right in front of me, and I hadn't seen it. Or rather — I had seen it, and chosen not to. My plan to run was already in place.

*Forty days lie between that night and who I am now. In those forty days I was trapped in the conduit, washed up in this city, and came to know its people's faces one by one. Without those forty days, I couldn't have read this sentence now. I could have read it — but I would have read it differently.*

My voice came out a beat late. It was so brief that no one in the square could have noticed. But I felt it. Something inside me asked. *Are you really going to read it.*

I didn't answer. There was no time to answer. The paper was in my hand, the letters were in front of my eyes. Reading was the only thing I knew how to do.

"Article 4, Clause 11." I said. "Should this settlement be confirmed to have turned within forty days, the present sentence shall be void."

*Reading that sentence took less than three seconds. But I can still say, even now, that those three seconds felt longer than forty days.*

---

The square stirred. A low murmur spread among the weeping. Many people were hearing of this clause for the first time. It made sense — no adult besides the Adjudicator had known it existed.

The Adjudicator was watching me from the platform. I read something in his expression. It wasn't surprise. He'd already known I knew about this clause. What surprised him was something else — the fact that I had actually spoken it aloud.

I looked over at the Scales. A terminal stood in one corner of the square. Nothing on the screen had changed. The Scales hadn't logged this reading as anything special. To the Scales, it was simply one sentence turned into sound.

The substitute herald glanced my way. The same clause was surely printed in the document he held too. He would never have to read it aloud himself now — I had stepped forward first. I couldn't tell, in that moment, whether he was relieved or disappointed. Maybe neither. He was simply watching, as he'd been trained to, what was happening right in front of him, exactly as it was.

*I didn't understand that moment. I felt something enormous had happened, and yet nowhere in the world was there any sign to prove it. No light came on. No sound rang. Not one figure on the Scales had changed.*

*I think I'd been expecting something strange. The sky splitting open. The ground shaking. Nothing happened. The most important sentence in the world had just become sound, and the world was still turning exactly the way it had turned forty days ago. I didn't know then that this frightened me. Now I know. A great thing happening with no sign at all — that is the most frightening thing in this world.*

*Now I know. The confirmation the clause required was never the Scales' work. It was a person's work. And the condition for becoming that person was exactly one thing — to read this sentence aloud, to the end. I had just met that condition.*

*I met it without knowing I was meeting it. That made it more frightening. I felt like someone who'd pulled a trigger without knowing what he'd fired.*

---

Somewhere in the square, a low voice asked, "What does that mean." Someone beside them answered, "It means all it has to do is turn." Another voice overlapped. "Who decides that." No one had an answer. Even I hadn't known it, myself, until a moment before.

One line still remained on the paper. In very small type, just below the clause.

"Confirmation is established by the herald's declaration."

I read that line and lifted my eyes from the page.

The square was watching me. A hundred twenty thousand eyes. The Adjudicator. The substitute herald. Trope.

Trope's face had changed. It was no longer the resigned face of a moment before. She understood now what this line meant. Not as fully as I did — but she understood.

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out.

My throat was dry. In forty days I had never once let myself imagine this moment. More precisely, I had worked hard not to imagine it. When I'd run, the goal had been to make sure this moment never came at all. Now that the goal had failed, I had never been trained for what came next. Training had only ever taught me how to read the Scales' verdict. No one had ever taught me how to overturn one.

*In three years I read one hundred twelve cases without stopping once. On the hundred thirteenth, I stopped for the first time. It was after I had finished the clause. All that remained was one sentence. That sentence would not come.*

The Adjudicator took one step closer from the platform. He said nothing. He simply stood nearer. I couldn't tell whether that was urging me on or standing with me.

Trope didn't move. Her hands still clasped in front of her, she was looking straight at me. Something was in her eyes, but I couldn't tell whether it was fear or hope. Maybe it was both. Even someone who believes mercy will never come for herself can still wish for it to come to someone else.

The wind rose again. The edge of the paper stirred. I didn't fold it. I couldn't fold it yet. A sentence was forming inside me. It hadn't become sound yet.

*How that sentence became sound, I set down in the next chapter. Here I will say only this — that in that moment, I did not know what I was about to say. To say I knew would be a lie. My body knew first. My mind followed a long while after.*

The square was still waiting for me. A hundred twenty thousand held breaths, all stopped, aimed at one sentence.
