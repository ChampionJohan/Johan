# Chapter 45. Withering

> _Previously_ — The Adjudicator found an old list of herald names. Trope brought it up the hill. Pheugon — to flee. Trope — turning. Pheugon laughed for the first time in his life. Trope stayed quiet for a long time.

The moment I opened my eyes, my body knew before I did that something was different.

It was the light. Every day until then, even with my eyes open, there had been shade over my face. Today the light was falling straight onto it. It was too bright.

I sat up.

My body was stiff — the result of three nights spent going back and forth between bare ground and rock. I rolled my neck side to side. It cracked. I nearly laughed at the sound. Yesterday's laughter still seemed to linger somewhere in my body.

---

The vine had withered.

It looked like it had happened overnight. Every leaf hung downward now. What had been green had turned brown, and in some places, already black. The wide arch had sagged and collapsed down onto the rock. When I touched the stem, there wasn't a trace of moisture left in it.

*I couldn't think anything, in that moment. I just stood there and looked at the withered leaves. What had grown in one night had withered in one night too. If you wanted to call that fair, I suppose it was. But I couldn't feel it as fair.*

I touched one of the leaves. It broke. It turned to powder between my fingers.

I tried to remember the night before. The vine had been perfectly fine before I fell asleep. I had run my hand over one leaf, said "Thank you" very quietly, and fallen asleep. What had happened in between, I had no idea at all. I didn't remember wind, didn't remember rain. I had simply fallen asleep and woken up, and in between, everything had died.

It bothered me, strangely, that this was the day after Trope had come. The day after that afternoon she'd gone quiet for so long, hearing the meaning of her own name — the vine had died the very next day. I knew there was no connection between the two. And yet I kept placing them side by side, as if one thing had to end before another could begin.

*It struck me as strange that I hadn't felt a thing, through that whole night. I hadn't felt anything while it grew, asleep. I hadn't felt anything while it died, either. Both life and death had passed quietly while I slept. That quietness frightened me.*

---

I was angry.

*Even writing this sentence, I still surprise myself. Two days before, I had watched an entire city live, and gotten angry. Angry, watching a hundred twenty thousand people survive. And now, watching one vine wither, I was feeling an anger of about the same size.*

I looked at the roots again. Where they'd begun in that crack in the rock, only a dry stub of stem remained now. I tried pulling it. It didn't come loose. The roots were still holding on. Only everything that had grown above them had died.

"…Why." I said it aloud. Alone, on the hill, no one there to hear. "Why this."

*In that moment I noticed my own voice was exactly the voice I'd used shouting at him near the gate two days before. Same pitch, same tremor. Only the target had changed. Then it had been a person. Now it was a dry stem. I felt ashamed of that. And still my voice didn't quiet down.*

*I keep hesitating, writing this part. Is it all right to set this down exactly as it happened. Admitting I got angrier over one vine than over the lives of a hundred twenty thousand people — doesn't that make me look far too small a person. But when I first decided to write this book, I made myself one promise. To write what happened at the size it actually happened. To keep that promise, even this shameful part can't be cut.*

I looked around. There was no one on the hill but me. No birdsong either. The bird that had briefly landed here yesterday and flown off hadn't come back. Only wind, passing through the dry grass.

I thought of him. The dry well near the gate. It occurred to me that he might still be there. I felt an urge to go to him again, and shout, this time about the vine. To ask why he'd given it and then taken it away. That urge didn't last long. I already knew he wouldn't get angry this time either. And I had no confidence I could bear that silence again.

---

I sank down where I stood.

*I really was angry, in that moment. More angry, watching this one small vine die, than I had been watching the city live. I wanted to deny that. But I couldn't. My body had already reacted first.*

I touched the withered leaves one by one. They all crumbled. Two days before, they had been smooth and thick. Now the moment my fingertip touched them, they turned to powder.

*I set that powder in my palm and looked at it for a long while. Why did this feel bigger to me than the lives of a hundred twenty thousand people? I asked myself that question, and for the first time, I didn't run from it. I sat with it.*

I looked at the spot where Trope had sat yesterday. Where she'd folded the paper and pressed it into my hand. I set that memory beside the dry stem in front of me. Yesterday I'd learned the meaning of my name and laughed. Today the vine had died and I was angry. In one day I felt like a completely different person. But really I was the same person. The one who had laughed learning his name, and the one now breaking apart in front of this vine — both of them carried the name of someone who flees.

*I realized then that I had been waiting for something, the whole time I'd been on this hill. First I waited for the city to fall. Then I waited for comfort under the shade. And now I was waiting for this anger to pass. I had always been someone who waits. It was only the direction of the waiting that kept changing.*

---

I didn't move from that spot until noon.

The sun fell over my whole body again. There was no more shade. My throat was dry. There was still a little water left in the jug Trope had left the day before. I didn't drink it. I didn't want to.

*Writing this part now, I know how foolish I must have looked that day. Not drinking water that was right there. That wasn't an expression of grief — it was just stubbornness. I think I wanted to punish something. The vine, or myself, or whatever it was that had sent this vine and taken it back — I hadn't decided precisely which.*

I gripped the withered stem again. It made a breaking sound. I hated that sound, and kept gripping it anyway.

I thought of Trope's mother. The handprint left on the well's stone rim. A mark that hadn't disappeared even after five years. Trope had said that every time she saw it, it felt like meeting her mother again. Gripping that dry stem, I understood that I too was holding on to some trace of something. Except my trace had lasted only two days, and hadn't been carved into stone. It was simply crumbling apart in my hand.

*In that moment I felt a strange envy. Trope's trace could still be touched, even five years later. Mine hadn't lasted a single day. I felt that difference was unfair. And I found myself laughable, thinking it. I was the very person who had just been shouting, days before, that it was unfair for the city to live. Now I was feeling it was unfair that one vine had disappeared so fast. I seemed to have a talent for finding evidence of unfairness in everything in the world.*

---

After a long while like that, I suddenly stopped.

*In that moment I felt a strange sense of watching myself from outside. As if I were standing outside my own body, looking down at myself sitting on this hill, gripping a dead vine. And from that vantage, I asked myself. Pheugon. What are you doing right now.*

I opened my hand. The broken bits of leaf blew away on the wind.

I got to my feet and looked down at the base of the hill. The city was still there. Roofs, walls, smoke from chimneys. The same view as three days before. Looking at that view, I wondered what those hundred twenty thousand people would say if they knew that, right now, I was falling apart like this over one vine.

*Probably no one would understand. I didn't understand it myself. But in that moment I realized something else too. Maybe Trope would understand. For five years she's held on to one handprint left by her mother. To her, that one handprint matters more than the whole city's survival. I had never once thought her strange for it. And here I was doing exactly the same thing, and thinking myself strange for it.*

*I noticed something for the first time, in that moment. When the city lived, I had gone to shout. I'd gone to find him and poured out thirty minutes. Now that the vine had died, I hadn't gone to find anyone. I had just sat here alone, working the anger down by myself. The same size of anger, and I was handling it in a completely different way.*

*And that difference was the strangest part of all. I was someone who reacted more strongly to one vine with a few leaves than to a hundred twenty thousand lives. I didn't know whether I should be ashamed of that, or simply accept it as fact.*

I sat back down. This time with my back to the dry stem. I didn't want to look at it anymore.

*In that moment, for the first time, I saw my three years differently. I had always thought of myself as someone who never did wrong. Someone who never got angry. And yet in two days on this hill, I had fallen apart twice. Once in front of a person, once in front of a dry stem. I wasn't someone who never got angry. I was someone who stockpiled anger. And anger that's been stockpiled eventually breaks out — at any time, over any size of reason at all. I learned that principle in my body, that day.*

---

By afternoon, I simply lay down beside that dry stem.

The sun was hot. I didn't shield myself from it. Only then did I feel, in my whole body, that the shade that had covered me until yesterday was gone. My back stung. My throat burned.

*Feeling that heat, I thought: I only had this shade for two days. Not forty days, not three years — barely two days. And watching myself fall apart this badly over its loss, I understood for the first time that the size of my anger didn't match the size of what caused it.*

*The city had been a matter of a hundred twenty thousand lives. And in front of that, I'd shouted, built arguments, reasoned about fairness. The vine had only been two days of shade. And in front of that, I simply fell apart. No logic at all. Just pain.*

I think I dozed off for a while in that heat. When I came to, the sun had already sunk far to the west. I didn't know how long I'd lain there. My throat was badly parched. Only then did I finally reach for the water jug.

The water was lukewarm. I drank it anyway. A few mouthfuls brought a little clarity back.

*I realized something strange, in that moment. I had refused water out of anger at a vine. But my body couldn't overcome anger. When you're thirsty enough, you drink water, in the end. I found that sad. I'd thought I was enduring some great sorrow. Really, I was just a person with a body that got thirsty.*

---

I closed my eyes. The sun glowed red through my eyelids.

*I think that was the first time I saw the true shape of my anger. I hadn't wanted justice. If it had been justice, the sizes of my anger shouldn't have come out backward like that — bigger for one vine dying than for a hundred twenty thousand people living. I had wanted what was mine. What I couldn't bear was losing something I'd received, something I'd felt was mine. The city was never mine. I only saved that city — I never owned it. But this vine had been there for me, and only me, for two days. It was my shade alone.*

*When it was gone, I fell apart more than I had watching the city live. That day, for the first time, I noticed that fact myself. Noticing it, at the time, brought no comfort at all.*

The wind carried off fragments of dry leaf down the hill. I didn't follow them with my eyes.

Once the sun had fully set, the hilltop grew cold again. I sat there in that spot, with no shade, no blanket. I couldn't even tell anymore whether I was angry because the shade was gone, or just cold.

*That night, for the first time, I understood that the anger inside me wasn't only one thing. There was anger three years old, anger accumulated over the forty days, anger that had begun two days before, and today, one very small anger, born of one vine. They were all different angers, and yet inside my body they all boiled at the same temperature. That night I tried to sort them apart, one by one. It didn't go well. Before I could sort them, they were already mixed together.*

I drew up my knees and hugged them. Stars began to rise, one by one. The same sky as two nights before. Except tonight there were no leaves left to screen them.

*That night, meeting the third night on this hill, I asked myself for the first time. How long am I going to stay here. No answer came. But that I'd asked myself the question at all was, in itself, a little different from before. I was no longer waiting for the city to fall. I was just waiting for something to end. I still didn't know what.*
