# Chapter 42. The Hill East of the City

> _Previously_ — Pheugon went to find him and shouted. "I knew this would happen. That's why I ran." He didn't get angry. Instead he asked, "What you're shouting at me right now — that isn't aimed at the Scales." Pheugon started walking east.

The hill was about an hour's walk from the city.

I didn't know the direction exactly. I just walked away from the city. As I walked, the ground rose little by little, and before I knew it I was climbing a hill.

It wasn't a tall hill. The kind everyone from this region knew about, but no one ever had a reason to go to. At the top there was dry grass and a few rocks. Almost no trees. Nothing that could give shade.

*I wasn't thinking about shade then. Someone who needs shade is someone waiting for something. I was climbing that hill without even knowing what I was waiting for.*

---

From the top, the whole city came into view.

The Reckoning was a city of low roofs packed close together. A square at its center, streets radiating outward around it. A city wall wrapped the whole thing in a circle. Seen from here, the city looked smaller than I'd thought. Living inside it for forty days, it had felt like the whole world. Seen from here, it fit in the palm of a hand.

I sat looking down at that palm-sized city.

*I should have asked myself, in that moment, what I was doing. I didn't. I just sat. I drew up my knees, wrapped my arms around them, and looked at the city.*

The wind blew. It was stronger up on the hill than down in the city. My clothes kept flapping.

Inside the walls, I could see the square. Tiny, ant-sized figures were moving. Of the hundred twenty thousand who'd filled that square yesterday, all I could see now from here were a few scattered points of motion. I couldn't tell which point was Trope, which was the Adjudicator.

*In that moment I felt a strange relief. No one could recognize me here. I wasn't one of those points either, here. I was outside them. I found that comfortable. And being comfortable made me ashamed.*

East of the city, low fields spread out. In forty days I had never once gone out that way. A narrow path ran between the fields, and that path led all the way up to this hill. Probably farmers had once walked it often. Now it was half-buried in grass.

*Walking that path, it felt strangely good that no one was there. In forty days I had always been inside someone's eyes. As a herald, as a strange boy, later as a savior. On the road to this hill, for the first time, I became a person in no one's eyes at all.*

---

I sat there for a long while.

For the first hour or so, I wasn't honest with myself. I tried to think I'd just come out for some air. But as time passed, it became clear that was a lie.

*I was waiting for the city to fall.*

*Even writing this sentence now, my hand shakes. I am the person who invoked the clause. The person who saved the city. The person who spoke those six words with his own mouth. And yet, less than a day after saying them, I was sitting on a hill, waiting for that city to fall.*

*Some will call this a contradiction. I want to say it wasn't. I never once wished for that city to live. I simply could not keep from saying those six words. I learned, precisely, for the first time on that hill, that a wish and an act can be two different things.*

I recalled an old story I'd once heard — a herald ordered to proclaim a city's fall, who ran because he couldn't bear the thought of the city being forgiven. It was something the supervisor had once mentioned in passing, at the training grounds. When I first heard it, I hadn't understood that person. Why hate being forgiven? Why couldn't he stand a city he hated surviving?

*I understand now. That person in the story hadn't hated a single city. He must have believed the world had to be fair. The rule that the guilty are punished and the righteous are kept safe. Rather than watch that rule broken, he'd chosen to be the one who never showed up to keep it. I know that feeling in my body now.*

---

Noon came.

The sun stood overhead. With no shade, I took the sun on my whole body. My throat was dry. I hadn't brought water.

*I thought, then, that not bringing water had been a mistake. I know now. It wasn't a mistake. I think I wanted to punish myself. For what, I didn't know then, and I don't know precisely now. I only know I didn't want to be comfortable that day.*

Being thirsty brought back old thoughts. The three days trapped in the conduit. In that darkness, water up to my knees, I'd suffered not from too little water to drink, but too much. Now it was the opposite. On this hill there was no shade and no water. I found the contrast strangely funny. Three years ago I'd learned to speak while submerged in water. Now I was losing words for lack of it.

*I wrote, in Chapter 10, that those three days were the first time I spoke my own story aloud. On this hill I was doing the opposite. I said nothing at all. There was no one to speak to. I was the one who had left the place where he'd been.*

---

I thought of the dinner table.

The table where the four of us used to sit. Father, mother, brother, me. And for the months that person stayed with us, five. I don't remember those evenings exactly. Only a few fragments remain. The window growing dark outside. Someone laughing. That laughter never heard in that house again after.

*I brought that table back to mind on that hill. It was strange. I had already written about that table in Chapter 3. I'd thought it was a memory already put away. But with hunger and thirst pressing in, the memory came back raw. I couldn't even remember what we'd eaten that evening, but the sense of that empty place stayed with me, somewhere in my stomach.*

Almost nothing of the city's sound reached this far. The wind was too strong. Now and then something like a bell seemed to carry, faintly. I couldn't be sure.

I kept watching the city. There was no sign of it falling. The roofs stood where they were. The wall stood where it was. A few threads of smoke rose from chimneys. Someone was cooking a meal.

*Watching that smoke, I had a strange thought. What would a city look like as it falls? I'd never once imagined it. More precisely, I'd avoided imagining it. For three years I had wished this city would fall, and never once pictured what that falling would actually look like. Now that I tried to picture it, nothing came.*

---

The afternoon came.

I was still there. My legs went numb, and I shifted position a few times. I tried moving into the shade of a rock, but the rock was small, so was its shade.

Nothing happened.

*I still can't say precisely what I was expecting, then. Was I expecting the ground to split? The Scales to reverse its verdict again? Or just expecting anything at all to happen, so my anger would have somewhere to go?*

*Nothing happened. The city stayed exactly where it was. The people went on living inside it. Whatever I thought, sitting on that hill, the city didn't care.*

*At first that felt unjust to me. Then it felt empty. I had spoken the most important sentence in the world. And the day after I said it, the world went on turning in a way that took no notice of me at all.*

I closed my eyes and remembered the Adjudicator's voice again. "It feels strange today. I'm happy, but it doesn't feel like happiness." He was a man who had waited forty years. I was a boy who had run for forty days. And yet on this hill, our two strange feelings, standing in different places, wore the same shape.

*In that moment, for the first time, I wondered. Was the Adjudicator, too, sitting somewhere alone right now, looking into this same unfamiliar feeling inside him? He couldn't be as angry as I was — he hadn't had my three years. And yet he too had looked like a man who'd lost something. The face of someone who had waited forty years for a place, only to watch it filled by someone else's mouth.*

I opened my eyes. The city was still there.

---

The sun began to set.

I was hungry. My throat was parched too. As my body wore out, my thoughts grew simpler. I stopped waiting for the city to fall. I just sat.

*Looking back later, I thought this was the most honest moment of all. When I'd first climbed the hill, I'd been someone waiting for something. By evening I was simply someone exhausted. In the place where waiting had been, nothing was left. Even my anger had grown tired.*

Far off in the city, lights began coming on one by one. Houses preparing dinner. Forty days before, I had once watched those lights from a distance with Trope. Back then those lights had been the place we had to return to. Now they were the place I'd left.

*The same lights, and yet the heart that watched them had completely changed in between. I can't say precisely when that change happened. Maybe it was the moment I said those six words in the square. Or the moment I shouted at him near the gate. Or maybe it was here, now, on this hill.*

---

Night came.

The wind grew colder. I drew my collar closer. The city's lights glimmered far off. Stars began to appear. I turned my back to the city and leaned against a rock.

I thought Trope might be looking for me by now. Maybe she'd asked the Adjudicator. "Where did he go." The Adjudicator would probably have told her the truth. That he went east. Beyond that, he wouldn't have known either. This hill wasn't a place anyone had any particular reason to go.

*That night I think I wanted Trope to come find me. And at the same time I wanted no one to come at all. Both feelings were there together. I didn't know, then, which one was real. Maybe both were. A person's heart doesn't come out as a single line, the way the Scales does.*

A dog barked once, far away. Then quiet again. I looked at the sky. Stars were multiplying, one by one. Without the city's lights up here, far more stars were visible than inside the city.

*I tried not to think anything, watching those stars. It didn't work well. Thoughts kept multiplying too, like the stars. My brother's back as he walked away. The day my mother said it was time to go. My father's, "Take care of yourself." The substitute herald's, "Congratulations, I think you did the right thing." The baker's hand. Trope's, "The reason isn't gone." His, "What you're shouting at me right now isn't aimed at the Scales." Thoughts came up out of order and sank back down.*

*That night I tried not to look toward the city. My head kept turning that way anyway. I kept watch over that spot without even knowing what I was guarding.*

I was hungry and thirsty. And still I didn't want to go down. Going down felt like it would mean admitting something. I didn't know what. Only that as long as I stayed on this hill, I could still be someone holding on to something.

*Looking back now, that was strange logic. I had already saved the city. Whatever I did on this hill wouldn't change that fact. But at the time, I felt that staying on this hill was itself a kind of resistance. Resistance to what, I didn't know. Not to the city, and not to him. Probably to myself. I still hadn't fully accepted the thing I had already decided — those six words.*

Out of the habit of counting one hundred twelve cases, I began counting the stars. Twenty, thirty, forty. I gave up counting. It wasn't something you could count. There were too many. I realized, in that moment, that I had only ever lived inside things that could be counted. People's names, charges, dates, one hundred twelve cases. And this thing inside me now — whether it was anger or grief, I still wasn't sure — this couldn't be counted.

I fell asleep on that spot that night. No shade, no blanket, my back against the cold rock.

*I don't remember what I dreamed that night. But I remember one thing. Just before I fell asleep, I murmured, very quietly, to myself. "…It's still not over." What wasn't over, I couldn't explain then, and I still can't now.*

*Thinking about it now, that sentence was a kind of threat. I don't even know who it was aimed at. Maybe at myself. That night, on that hill, I think I wanted to believe something more had to happen. Whether the city fell, or the clause was overturned, or even if some sign fell from the sky. That night, I wasn't yet ready to accept that nothing at all would happen.*

The wind slowly died down. I curled up between the rocks. The stone was cold, and my back ached against it. I fell asleep that way. No shade, no blanket, no answer.
