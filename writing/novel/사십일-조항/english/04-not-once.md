# Chapter 4. Not Once

> _Previously_ — In the archive room, Pheugon reads the Reckoning's sentence
> in full. Unlike every other clause, Article 4, Clause 11 carries no
> footnote. The clerk tells him that twenty years ago the same clause
> existed but was never triggered, and that city was demolished all the
> same. That night, Pheugon mouths the clause twelve times without sound.

The next morning I knocked on the supervisor's door.

I had an excuse ready. Checking proclamation procedure. First city case, so wanting to go back over the steps — that shouldn't sound strange.

I repeated that excuse to myself several times outside the door. It felt strange. Normally I'd have just knocked and gone in. That day I stood there a long while with my hand on the handle.

*Looking back, that wasn't hesitation. I already knew what I was going to ask. My body just seemed to already sense where that question would take me. Those few seconds with my hand on the door may have been the last few seconds I was still allowed to have decided nothing.*

"Come in."

The room was cool as always. The supervisor didn't look up from his papers.

"What is it."

"…Wanted to check something about procedure."

"What."

I paused a moment, then asked.

"…Article 4, Clause 11."

The supervisor looked up from his papers.

---

"What about it."

"Saw it reading the full text. There's no footnote."

The supervisor leaned back in his chair. His face was weighing something.

"There isn't, no." the supervisor said. "In this city — no, as far as I know, across this whole region — that clause has never actually mattered."

"…Not once?"

"Not once." the supervisor said. "I've done this thirty years. This makes three city cases I've seen. All three times, that clause just sat there on the paper. Nothing ever happened."

I nodded, hearing that. But a different question was rising underneath.

"…Why did nothing happen?"

"Because they were demolished." the supervisor said. "All three cities ran out their forty days and were demolished. The clause never triggered because it never triggered — what other reason would there be."

*I know now that the supervisor said that without thinking at all. But how it landed on me, he had no idea. He was calling that clause a dead letter. I heard it a different way.*

---

"…So the clause is just there, then? Meaning nothing at all?"

"That's how rules are." the supervisor said. "A lot of old rules nobody uses anymore. But nobody strikes them out either. No reason to."

"…Who wrote it, that clause?"

The supervisor thought for a moment.

"Don't know. I don't know either. The Scales's articles have just always been there, they say. No one knows who wrote them. When I was new I asked a senior about it — he didn't know either."

"…Isn't that strange? Being applied without anyone knowing it."

"It's strange." the supervisor said. "But have you ever seen the Scales be anything but strange. That's just how it is. It runs whether we understand it or not. That's why it's the Scales."

Something caught on that answer. I couldn't have said exactly what, then.

*I can say it now. The supervisor lumped two things together under the same word — "strange." That the Scales runs and keeps running even when it can't be explained, and that one of the Scales's own articles leaves a judgment to a person. But those were two entirely different kinds of strangeness. One is a calculation no one can explain. The other is a spot the calculation couldn't reach, so it was handed to a person instead. The supervisor didn't know the difference. Neither did I, at the time. But I needed to know that difference far more urgently than he did.*

---

"…Why doesn't this clause attach to individual cases?"

"An individual case is one person." the supervisor said. "Whether one person suddenly turned or didn't, there's nothing to confirm. You just watch the next figure come up. Lower, it's lower. Not lower, it isn't."

"…But you said the weight already stacked up never comes back down."

The supervisor glanced at me.

"Right. So the clause isn't needed for an individual. What's already stacked is stacked, and after that, you just don't add more. A city is different. A city is a matter of whole or nothing, so that one clause decides everything."

I turned the thought over. A mercy an individual doesn't need becomes everything for a city. A strange structure.

"…Why was this clause written in the first place, then. If no one was ever going to use it."

The supervisor looked at me a moment, then set down his papers.

"I wondered that too, once. When I was new." the supervisor said. "I asked a senior. He laughed. Said maybe it wasn't written so no one would use it — maybe it was written in case no one could."

"…What does that mean."

"Don't know." the supervisor shrugged. "Just something that got said once. Don't take it too seriously."

*I took it very seriously. I turned it over all that day. A clause written in case it couldn't be used. A clause written and left on the page even while believing it would never trigger. Why write a clause like that. Why leave it on paper, knowing no one would ever use it.*

*I know now. That wasn't a clause the Scales wrote. Which is why the Scales's own logic could never explain it.*

---

"…What does it take, for it to trigger?"

The supervisor looked at me a moment.

"By the text — if the city is confirmed, within forty days, to have turned, that's it."

"…Who confirms it?"

"Not in the text." the supervisor said. "It just says 'confirmed.' Doesn't say who does the confirming."

I think I'd been waiting for that answer. Hearing it only made things more complicated.

"…So if no one confirms it?"

"Then it doesn't happen." the supervisor said, picking his papers back up. "The Scales calculates a city's state every day. But there's no category in that calculation called 'turned.' The Scales measures weight. It can't measure direction. That's why the clause has never triggered. Not because the condition was never met, but—"

The supervisor stopped.

"…No, I don't know. I don't know that part exactly either. It just never happened. Thirty years."

*I held onto the end of that sentence for a long time, that day. "Not because the condition was never met, but—" The supervisor had started to say something else and stopped. I wondered, then, what he'd meant to say. I know now. He knew it too, vaguely. That the condition wasn't unmet — there had simply never been a way to confirm it. But he never put that into words, not the whole way through.*

---

"…And this one, then."

"This one, what."

"…The Reckoning. What if it really does turn."

The supervisor looked at me. He paused a moment before answering.

"Pheugon. There've been three city cases, once-a-decade cases. All three times, it didn't happen. You just handle the procedure you've been given, exactly right. Read the clause too, along with everything else, exactly as it's written. What comes after that isn't your job."

"…Yes, sir."

"If you're worried, you don't need to be. That kind of thing doesn't happen."

*The supervisor meant that as comfort. To ease my mind. Afraid I might be frightened at the thought of a city of a hundred and twenty thousand people falling, he told me that kind of thing simply doesn't happen.*

*Hearing it didn't ease my mind. Hearing it, I felt some possibility become clear and sharp for the first time. It doesn't happen. But if the reason it doesn't happen isn't that the condition is never met but that it's never confirmed — then turned around, the sentence read like this too: as long as it's never confirmed, nothing will happen, no matter what.*

*I didn't know it myself, walking out of that room, that I was already reading the sentence turned around. That wouldn't come into exact words until much later, near the very end of the forty days.*

---

As I was leaving, the supervisor called me back.

"…Pheugon."

"…Yes."

"Why are you asking so much detail about this. The other trainees never ask like this."

I stopped short for a moment.

"…No reason. It's just my first case like this."

The supervisor looked at me a moment, then turned his eyes back to his papers.

"All right. Being thorough is a good thing."

*The supervisor must have seen something in my face that day. If I'd ever had the chance to ask him directly, much later, I would have. I never got that chance. What the supervisor saw that day, I still don't know exactly. But I know one thing. He could have stopped me, that day. He didn't.*

---

I went straight back up to the third-floor archive room. The clerk looked a little surprised to see me.

"…Back again."

"…Could I see the past city cases? Three of them, I heard."

The clerk went in and came back out with three case files. Thin ones. Less than half the thickness of the Reckoning's.

I looked through them right there. All three cities had unfamiliar names. Places I'd never heard of. The clerk said their Scales connections had been severed so long ago they no longer even appeared on maps.

All three cases ended the same way — the full forty days passed, and demolition. But one of them, the most recent one, had a strange sentence attached. A short note in the margin of the results report, written by hand, it looked like.

> Day thirty-nine: incidents of theft within the city dropped sharply. Observed as a meaningful change, though not reflected in the Scales's figures.

I read that sentence twice.

"…What does this mean?"

The clerk looked at the note and tilted her head.

"Couldn't say. Probably something the case officer jotted down at the time. It's not part of the official record, though. Margin notes carry no effect."

*I traced that note with my fingertip. Day thirty-nine. One day before the city fell. A sharp drop in theft meant that someone who'd been stealing had stopped. But that change hadn't been reflected in the Scales's figures. The weight already stacked up doesn't come back down.*

*And the next day, that city was demolished.*

*I wondered who had written that note. That person must have seen something too, the way I had. That the city was changing. But they only wrote it in the margin. Never said it officially. Why not. I didn't know the answer, then. I know now. That person had no way to confirm it either. What you see and what you can declare are two different problems.*

The other two files had no margin note like it. They just read, cleanly, "forty days elapsed, clause not triggered, demolition executed." I wondered what the difference was between those two and the third. Maybe those two cities really had changed nothing at all. Or maybe they had changed, and no one had written it in the margin.

*What frightened me most was that there was no way to tell the two apart. If it isn't in the record, a thing that happened becomes a thing that never happened. I thought for the first time that what the Scales fails to record might far outnumber what it does.*

I held the files a while longer before returning them.

"…Thank you."

"Come back if you need to." the clerk said. "But I've never seen a herald dig into a city case like this before."

I didn't answer. I went down the stairs.

---

I walked the corridor past the wall where the herald roster hung. Numbers beside the names. Beside mine, 112. Beside the others, mostly two-digit numbers. Of them, only three on this list had ever handled a city case. The three cases the supervisor had mentioned.

I looked for those three names. Unfamiliar names. People from a generation well above mine. They must have read that clause too. Had they stopped, at that last line, the way I had? I wondered, but there was no one to ask.

*I would never come to know what those three people felt, reading that line. Whether they saw something in it the way I did, or simply read it as procedure and moved on. But I can guess one thing. None of them can have been to that city what I was to mine. I didn't know it yet, that day, that this fact set me apart from the other three in a place entirely different from where they stood.*

Walking out of the proclamation office, I looked up at the sky. Overcast. It looked like rain was coming.

I stood a moment at the door. I counted the days left until proclamation day. A notice for the Reckoning case was posted on the proclamation office board. Twenty-one days from today.

*Twenty-one days. Seeing that number, I began, for the first time, counting time. Until then I'd thought of the clause only as a rule with a triggering condition. From that moment it was different. I felt the clause as something with a deadline. Having a deadline meant there was someone who could do something within it. I didn't yet think that someone was me. But that the clause needed a "someone" inside it at all — that much, I was already feeling that day.*

Rain started on the way home. I had no umbrella. I just walked through it. Feeling my wet clothes grow heavier, I felt, strangely, glad of that weight. At least this was a weight the Scales wasn't measuring.

*That night I began planning to run. Though at the time, I didn't call it that. I called it, "needing time to think." I know now. That wasn't needing time to think. I had already finished thinking.*

*And what that thought had been, I kept hidden even from myself, for a very long time.*
