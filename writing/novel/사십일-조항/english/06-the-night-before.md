# Chapter 6. The Night Before

> _Previously_ — At the bottom of the Reckoning's sentence document,
> Pheugon found Article 4, Clause 11. If the city turns within forty days,
> the sentence becomes void. He admitted, for the first time, that this was
> mercy — and that this mercy had never once come to his own house. That
> night he made a quiet decision: that door had to stay shut.

Departure was three days off.

The supervisor had set it that way. Two days' travel to the Reckoning, a day's rest on arrival, then the proclamation. The forty days begin the moment I read the first line in that city's square. So the days I spend traveling don't count against the forty. That's simply passage.

I don't remember well how I spent those three days.

*It's strange. I remember all hundred and twelve of my sentences. The merchant's face, Had stopping three times on his own name, even the cool air in the supervisor's room. But those three days are a blur. I must have already been half a different person, by then. Someone who makes memories and someone who has decided not to leave any live different lives.*

The third night came. The next morning, I was due to set out on the road to the Reckoning.

---

On the second day, I went to the training hall.

There was no reason to go. I was already a full herald; there was nothing left to learn in that room. And yet I walked past it and opened the door anyway.

The room was empty. High ceiling, no windows. The room where my own voice used to come back to me. I stood in the middle of it and said nothing at all.

Our cohort's names were still up on the wall. Had's name too. Not erased. Not erasing the names of the washed-out was this city's way. Weight gets erased. Records don't.

*I stood a moment in front of that name. Had's question came back to me. Why don't you ever stop. That night, for the first time, I tried to think of an answer. Because if I stop, something catches up to me. But that night I already knew. I wasn't trying to stop, then. I was about to veer off onto an entirely different road. That was a different thing from not stopping. I didn't know the difference, at the time.*

*I think I wanted to confirm something, standing there. Whether this room still recognized me. I know how that sounds. But that's exactly the frame of mind I was in, standing there. The room didn't answer. The wall was just a wall. With no one to make a sound, there was no sound to send back.*

I walked past the supervisor's door on my way out of that room. It was shut. There was someone moving inside. I didn't knock.

*What would have changed if I had. I've asked myself that question many times since. The answer is always the same. I wouldn't have known. And because I wouldn't have known, I still sometimes picture myself standing in front of that door with my hand raised, and staying that way. A hand that, in fact, I never raised.*

On the morning of the third day, I watched from my window as the cart for the Reckoning was made ready. The driver was watering the horse. One of the crates loaded onto that cart would have my name tag on it. A herald's travel provisions.

I didn't watch the cart for long. I looked once and closed the curtain.

---

I packed my bag.

What a herald carries on the road is fixed. A change of clothes, a water flask, a wax envelope for the original sentence, and a wristband proving identity. The wristband connects to the Scales. Crossing between cities, it has to be scanned for the passage to be logged.

I packed to that list. And on top of it, I added things not on the list.

Six loaves of dried bread. A second change of clothes. Kindling. I left the wristband out.

*I knew exactly what it meant, leaving that wristband behind. Without it, I'm not "recorded" anywhere. No city keeps me on record as having passed through. I wanted to call that an oversight. It wasn't an oversight.*

When the bag was packed, I pushed it under the bed. And I sat there a long while, looking at the space under it.

I set the wax envelope on top too. The one holding the Reckoning's original sentence. That was an item on the list. A herald must always keep the sentence on his person. Packing it, I asked myself. Why am I taking this. I'm not even going to read it.

*I had an answer then, and I have one now. I couldn't throw it away. Throwing it away wouldn't have made it cease to exist — it would still be sitting somewhere in the Scales's record, waiting to be assigned to someone else. I couldn't stand that. Neither that someone else might read that sentence, nor that someone else might discover that clause. I decided, then, that this document should belong to exactly one person, in the whole world. Me.*

*It was a strange kind of possessiveness, thinking about it now. Deciding to hold, all by myself, a document that could save an entire city. At the time I believed that was caution.*

---

In the evening I thought about going to see my father.

To be precise, I'd already decided not to go, and only pretended to think it over so I could confirm that decision again. I changed my clothes. Walked as far as the door. Went back inside.

*I've thought a long while about why I didn't see my father that night. I wanted to write that I was afraid — afraid he'd look me in the eye and know. But I know now. It wasn't that I was afraid my father would stop me. I was afraid he wouldn't. If my father had said just one thing — "all right, don't go" — I would have used that as my excuse, and truly not gone. I didn't need an excuse. I already didn't want to go.*

I took out a sheet of paper instead. I meant to write a letter.

I wrote three lines and crossed them out. Wrote two more and crossed those out too. In the end nothing was left at all.

*I didn't understand, for a long time, why I couldn't write that letter. I understand now. A letter needs a reason written into it. And I couldn't write my real reason down. Writing it would have meant admitting to myself that it was real. I wasn't ready to admit that yet. So I chose to leave nothing at all. It felt, at the time, like the more honest choice. It was, in fact, the least honest one.*

Instead I walked all the way to the other side of the city. To the mouth of the alley where my father lived.

I didn't go up to the house itself. I stood at the mouth of the alley and looked at the window. The light was on. My father stayed up late.

*I watched that light for about ten minutes. I started toward it twice, and stopped both times. I still wonder. Did my father know I was standing in that alley. I never asked. I don't think I'll ever get to ask.*

The light shifted once, as if my father had crossed in front of the window. I saw that shadow and turned away.

*I didn't say goodbye to my father, that night. Even writing this sentence now, it's the thing that troubles me most. Everything else I've managed to put into words. This one, I still can't.*

---

I left the house past midnight.

I didn't lock the door. There was no need to. Only one person lived in this house now, and that person had nothing left to take.

The street was quiet. In our city, the lamps went out one by one as night fell. I walked through that dark with ease. Fifteen years of living on this street.

There was a terminal of the Scales on every corner. Every time I passed one, I pushed my hands deeper into my pockets.

*I had no intention of raising my hand. My body moved that way regardless. Habit outlives reason. That night I felt my body still moving as the old me, while my mind had already gone somewhere else.*

I walked not toward the Reckoning, but the other way.

The road passed through the market. Full of people by day, only bare stalls remained by night. The spot where the merchant who'd tampered with his scale weights used to stand was there too. My first proclamation. That man came back into the market six months later, and I saw it from a distance. He didn't recognize me, that time. Even if he had, he probably wouldn't have let on.

*Passing that spot, I had a strange thought for the first time. That man had served out his full disposition and gone back to standing in the market, while I was now running in exactly the opposite direction. He had stood his ground before the Scales; I wasn't standing mine. I didn't hold that thought long. Holding it felt like it would stop my feet.*

---

There was a gatekeeper. Even at night, someone stands watch.

I had to pass him. Without the wristband, I couldn't pass through officially. But the gatekeeper knew me. Proclamation office people get through gates on their faces alone.

"…Where you off to?"

The gatekeeper asked. His voice was sleepy.

"…An errand." I said. "By the supervisor's orders."

*A lie. My second lie that night. The first had been in the supervisor's room, saying "no" when he asked whether I had some connection to the Reckoning. The Scales would have calculated each of those two lies. Precisely. And what those two lies together would carry me toward — that, the Scales could never have calculated. It has no category for that.*

The gatekeeper nodded. He didn't ask anything more. That I was a herald, that I was under the supervisor's orders, was enough. No one suspects a herald. A herald is someone who reads without feeling, and someone who reads without feeling is assumed to have no reason to run.

*I understood, in that moment, for the first time, that the blankness I'd trained into my face was protecting me. The supervisor had taught me how not to stop. He hadn't taught it only to be used while reading. That night I put that same face to a different use.*

The steps carrying me out past the gate, I'd expected to feel heavy. They didn't.

*That struck me as strange. This was the step turning my back on a city I'd lived in fifteen years. Something should have caved in. But I only put one foot in front of the other. Looking back, I don't think that was strength. I think I had already been practicing this step for a long time. In my mind. My body was only following the practice.*

The gatekeeper called something after me. I think it was a good night. I didn't turn around. I only raised one hand.

---

Half an hour's walk from the gate, an old road led down to the riverside.

At the end of that road was a well, I'd learned during training. Not part of the formal curriculum. A senior had mentioned it once, in passing. That the wells watering each city are joined underground to one another. Originally a waterway, and for a long time now, a road people walk too.

"The authorities don't know?" I'd asked.

"They know." the senior had said. "They just don't bother to close it off. Whoever uses that road is usually someone who wants to leave the city. Letting them go is a simpler calculation for the Scales than stopping them."

I'd let that pass at the time. I thought it had nothing to do with me.

*I'd forgotten that story for three years. And yet that night, walking out past the gate, it was the very first thing that came back to me. What I'd thought forgotten hadn't been erased. It had just been waiting, quietly, until it was needed. I wanted to believe that was coincidence. I don't believe that anymore.*

---

The well stood at the end of the riverside road. It looked long abandoned. Moss on the coping stones, an old plank laid across it instead of a cover.

I stood in front of it and hesitated for a long time.

*I wrote hesitated, but even now I find it hard to say exactly what I hesitated over. It wasn't whether to go in. I'd already resolved to go in. What I hesitated over was something else — that doing this meant I could never be the person I was before, again. Crossing that line took time.*

I pushed the plank aside. Cold air rose from inside. Mixed in with the smell of water was another smell, not water at all.

I lowered my bag first, tied to a rope. I heard the sound of it vanishing into the dark. No splash. Which meant the drop to the bottom was quite deep.

I looked in. I couldn't see anything. My fingers found iron rungs set into the wall. Less a stairway than a ladder.

*In that moment I thought of the road to the Reckoning. The cart, the highway, the inn we'd stop at along the way, the procedures waiting on arrival that couldn't even be called a welcome. That road was set, recorded, predictable. This well was the opposite. No one knew. No one was waiting for me. I liked that difference. Looking back, that was the trouble. I chose not the safe road, but the one no one knew about.*

And I set my foot down.

*I remember that moment with strange clarity. The touch of the first iron rung against the sole of my foot. Cold, and slick. Stepping onto it, I thought, for the first time, how deep this stairway might go.*

*Whether that thought came from fear or from curiosity, I couldn't answer that night. I can answer now. I was afraid. And at the same time I was calculating. The two carried the same weight in me, in that moment.*

I stepped onto the second rung. The third. I heard the plank settle back over the opening above me. I hadn't pushed it shut myself. Might have been the wind. Might not have been.

*I still remember that sound. Wood meeting stone, short and dull. That was the last sound of the world above ground I heard that night. I gave it no meaning at the time. I hear it differently now. As though someone had closed a door for me. Whether that helped me go, or kept me from coming back — I still don't know.*

The sound of water grew nearer beneath my feet. I hadn't touched it yet. The air against my toes just grew steadily damper.

I climbed down. I did not look back.
