# Chapter 25. In Search of the Text

> _Previously_ — When Pheugon failed to come, a substitute herald was
> assigned to the Reckoning, and he had already set out for the city.
> Only then did Pheugon understand that fleeing, by itself, had
> stopped nothing.

The news reached me from Trope's mouth.

It was dawn. A line had already formed at the well, and Trope had
gotten there before me and filled two water jars. These days I helped
her every morning. I'd started because I owed her something; somewhere
along the way it had just become habit.

"A new herald is coming," she said, setting down a jar. "The one who
was supposed to come didn't, so they've swapped in someone else.
There's a notice posted outside the Adjudicator's office."

I couldn't say anything.

My hand, winding the bucket rope, stopped for a moment, then moved
again. Luckily Trope wasn't looking at my face. She was peering into
the jar.

"Strange, isn't it," Trope went on. "That a herald just doesn't show
up. I've never seen that happen before. Maybe he caught a cold."

*I nearly laughed. A cold. Trope thought I had a cold. Looking back,
that would have been kinder. A cold, at least, gets better.*

"…When's he coming?" I asked.

"Not sure. People are saying about a week. The notice didn't give an
exact date. Just 'as soon as possible.'"

I counted the number silently. Subtract seven from the days
remaining, and the city still had some room left. But that room
wasn't room for me.

"Why do you look so shocked, Pheugon." Trope looked at me. "Is a
change of heralds really such a big deal?"

"…No." I picked up the jar for her. "It's just news to me, that's
all."

Trope looked at me a moment longer, then didn't press. That was who
she was. She never dug. I was grateful for it, and guilty in exactly
the same measure.

*She didn't know I was a herald. Because she didn't know, she could
hand me that news as if it were nothing. I was living off that
nothing. At the time I didn't even know to call it what it was.*

---

That whole evening, I thought about one sentence.

*If, within forty days, the said city is confirmed to have turned,
this sentence shall be rendered void.*

In a hundred and twelve readings, I had never seen a sentence like
it. It wasn't attached to individual cases. It appeared only on
city-wide ones — a sentence that showed up maybe once a decade.

The first time I read it, I'd skimmed past the words "confirmed to
have." I'd assumed the Scales would do the confirming. The Scales
confirmed everything.

But these past few days, the phrase kept catching on something.

*The Scales measures weight. The Scales does not measure direction.
I already knew that. It was something I'd learned in the first ten
days after I arrived in this city. But faced with that sentence, I'd been deliberately forgetting
it. Why I'd been forgetting it, I still wasn't honest enough with
myself to say.*

The new herald would arrive in seven days. When he arrived, he would
read all the way to that sentence too. And then — would it be
confirmed, or not?

I didn't know. And not knowing was unbearable.

*Even then, I couldn't explain to myself why it was so unbearable.
Without that explanation, I went ahead and decided what to do next.
That's the strangest part of this whole story — I knew what answer I
wanted before I knew what the answer was.*

---

I spent that whole morning walking the square.

There were boards all through the Reckoning announcing the days
remaining. When I'd first been washed up in this city, the number had
read thirty-nine. Now it had dropped much further. People passed the
boards without lingering on them anymore. They'd grown used to it. I
found that strange — that in a city whose demolition date ticked down
every day, people could just walk past the number.

I stopped in front of the bakery. The owner was handing over the last
of her bread to a customer who'd come after closing. In the old days
she'd have slipped it over quietly, off the Scales' books. This time
the customer insisted on paying, and she flatly refused to take it.

I watched the scene for a long time.

*That day I took it for ordinary kindness. It was only later I
learned that scenes like it had been multiplying across the city
around then. But my head was too full of other things that day to
take notice. Looking back now, I know what a mistake that was.*

I crossed the square. There really was a notice posted outside the
Adjudicator's office. Short. **Substitute herald assigned. Arrival
date not yet set.** I stood in front of it for a long time. A couple
of passersby read it and moved on without comment. To them it was
just an administrative notice.

Not to me.

---

As I walked, I thought about what kind of person the substitute
herald might be.

Faces from training came back to me. We'd all been taught the same
way — how not to stop, how not to listen, how not to snag on a name.
Whoever came wouldn't be very different. Someone who read without
feeling, precisely, to the last line.

*At the time I thought of him as something faceless. A tool, moving
exactly as it had been trained to move. That I had once wanted to be
that tool myself — that, in the moment, I didn't remember.*

*He would have had a supervisor too. He would have had a name too. He
must have, at some point, practiced reading a false sentence with his
own name written into it. I didn't think of any of that then. I only
began seeing him as a person much later, after he actually appeared
at the gates of this city.*

When he arrived, he would read this sentence document. From beginning
to end, precisely. And he would read down to the last line — Article
4, Clause 11 — without stopping. Just as I had.

I stopped walking mid-thought. I was in the middle of the square.
People passed around me.

*He didn't know this city. Didn't know Trope. Didn't know that the
baker had refused to take her change, or who had helped whom at the
well. To him, the Reckoning was just a name on a page.*

*I thought that fact should have reassured me. It didn't. The
opposite feeling kept rising instead, and I was afraid to give it a
name.*

---

The room of records sat behind the square, three flights down.

I'd been there before — it was where I'd found Mena's record. That
time I'd gone digging through precedents, the past rulings the Scales
drew on. This time what I was after wasn't a precedent. It was the
original text the precedents quoted — the clause itself.

"I'm looking for the original text of Article 4, Clause 11," I said.

The archivist was an elderly woman. She studied me a long moment over
her glasses.

"It's not here."

"…Not here?"

"The precedents are here. Records of precedents citing the clause.
But the clause text itself, we don't keep here," she said, pushing a
drawer shut. "That falls under the Adjudicator's authority, not the
herald office's."

"Why?"

For the first time, the archivist looked me straight in the eye.

"I don't know. I've worked here twenty years and never once seen the
original text of that clause."

"Twenty years."

"I was twenty-two when I first came here." She took off her glasses
and wiped them. "In that time I've seen exactly three city-wide
cases. This one makes four. And I've never once heard of that clause
actually firing."

"All three?"

"All three." She put her glasses back on. "They fell. All of them."

"And why the clause never fired—"

"I don't know." She went on straightening her papers. "I'm just the
person who files the precedents. I don't even know who wrote the
clause. Nobody does. I hear even the Adjudicator doesn't know."

I made her say it again.

"Even the Adjudicator doesn't know?"

"He said so himself. Long ago." She glanced at me. "Ask him yourself,
if you want."

I stood there a moment.

"I can see the precedents, though," I said.

"You already have. Last month."

She remembered me. I couldn't say anything.

*Only then did it strike me that I hadn't really been hiding in this
city at all. People remembered me. They didn't know my name, but they
remembered my face. Hiding, it turned out, wasn't the same as being
erased. It just meant no one asked.*

---

Climbing back up the stairs, I passed a Scales terminal.

There were terminals like it all over the Reckoning. Rest a hand on
one and your own weight came up. People did it out of habit, hand on,
hand off.

I hadn't touched one since arriving in this city. I hadn't touched
one back home, either, not since the day I walked out of the
supervisor's office. Forty days now, coming due, and not once.

*I can explain, even now, exactly why I didn't. It wasn't that I was
afraid the number would be low. It wasn't that I was afraid it would
be high, either. I knew that number would be accurate, at that very
moment as at any other, and it was the accuracy I was afraid to see.
Accuracy always frightened me. That was the sickness running through
this whole book.*

I stopped in front of the terminal, then walked on without raising my
hand.

At the end of the alley, I saw the man who doesn't get angry again —
the same man I'd seen in the square before. This time he was pulling
a handcart, and its wheel had dropped into a ditch. A few passersby
stopped as if to help, then said they were busy and went on. He
didn't get angry. He struggled alone to free the wheel until,
finally, a passing child helped him lift it out.

I watched a moment, then walked on.

*At the time I just thought he was unlucky. A wheel in a ditch, no
one helping. An ordinary day. I didn't even wonder why he stayed so
calm about it. I had no room for that. My head was already full with
a different sentence.*

---

The Adjudicator's office was on the third floor of the Scales
building, at the end of the corridor. I'd been there twice before —
once because he'd summoned me, once because I'd gone looking for him.

This time I was the one seeking him out again.

The door was closed. I stood in front of it and hesitated.

*I tried, standing there, to work out what I was actually asking.
"Show me the original text" would have been enough. But behind it I
was hiding what I really wanted to ask — whose job the confirming
was. Whether that job passed to the man arriving in seven days. Or—*

*I didn't finish that thought. If I finished it, I was afraid I'd
catch myself out — find out exactly what it was I wanted.*

I knocked.

There was no answer for a long while. Just as I raised my hand to
knock again, a voice came from inside.

"…Come in."

*I still remember the silence between those two words. I asked him
later why he'd taken so long to answer. He didn't answer that either.
By then I already knew that not answering was one of his ways of
speaking.*

I opened the door.

The Adjudicator was sitting at his desk. He didn't look surprised to
see me. He'd taken to acting, these days, as if he already knew I was
coming. Maybe he really did.

As always, papers were piled on his desk like a mountain. Through the
window, the square lay below, and the board announcing the days
remaining was visible from there too. His desk was positioned exactly
where that board could be seen best. I didn't think that was an
accident.

"What brings you," he asked.

I steadied my breath.

"Article 4, Clause 11," I said. "I want to see the original text. Not
the one line attached to the sentence document."

The Adjudicator set down his pen.

"Why that."

"I feel like I need to know it for certain."

"Know what."

I couldn't answer right away. I hadn't sorted out for myself what it
was I needed to know.

"…I heard a new herald is coming."

The Adjudicator's eyes narrowed briefly. He said nothing. He glanced
out at the square once, then back at me.

He looked at me a long time. I couldn't read what that look meant
then. I can read it now. It wasn't judgment. It wasn't worry, either.

It was resolve.

"Sit," he said. "This will take a while."

I sat. The Adjudicator rose and walked to the bookshelf. On the
bottom shelf was a drawer with a lock on it. While he took out the
key, I watched his back.

It looked like the back of a man who had put this moment off for a
very long time.

The key turned in the lock with a short, heavy sound. I listened and
looked out the window. The board in the square still showed its
number. One less than what I'd seen passing through just before.
Midnight must have come and gone while I wasn't watching.

*That was the first day I actually felt the number drop. Before then
it had just been a sign. From that moment, it wasn't.*

"No one's ever shown you this," the Adjudicator said, drawing an old
scroll out of the drawer. "In twenty-some years, I've taken it out
exactly twice."

*Even hearing that, I had no idea. What kind of person I'd be when I
walked out of that room that night — I truly had no idea.*
