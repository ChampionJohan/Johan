# Chapter 11. The Third Day

> _Previously_ — On the second day, Pheugon spoke aloud for the first time.
> Reciting the sentence document from memory, the words turned into his own
> story. What began as "I was right" ended in "So why am I here." He
> released that question into the water and lost consciousness. What he
> remembers next is the sensation of his body moving. He was not the one
> moving it.

On the third day, the water stopped rising.

My legs knew it first. It had stayed at my knees for days now, unchanged. I couldn't tell whether that was good news or bad.

*Bad news. I know that now. The water no longer rising meant this stretch was designed to balance out at exactly that level. No one had drained the water to save me. It was simply water flowing the way it always flowed.*

My body had been submerged too long; the feeling in my legs had gone dull. I pinched my calf now and then. It hurt, but the hurt seemed to arrive from far away.

The air hung heavy and damp. Every breath filled the inside of my nose with the smell of stone. Nothing but that smell. No sound, no light, no other scent.

*I didn't know, then, that my body was slowly leaving me. I only thought I was tired. What happens to a body that holds out three days in water — that was the first time I was learning it, myself.*

---

Two loaves of bread remained. The flask was nearly empty. I scratched a third line on the wall.

*I felt my own hand slow down, drawing that line. No strength left. Even scratching the stone with my fingertip had become work.*

I sat back against the wall. Something felt wrong with my body. I shook without being cold. I was hungry, and yet the thought of bread didn't come.

My head felt heavy. My neck, leaned against the wall, kept lolling to one side. I shook it a few times, trying to right it.

Once, opening my eyes, my vision blurred. I'm not even sure what "blurred" means, in a place with no light. But that's what it was. As if a thin film had grown over the inside of my eyelids.

*I only learned later that this was a frightening sign. At the time I just thought I was tired. I kept blinking. It didn't help.*

I opened the flask and took a mouthful. I'd rationed that water carefully, days before, and now I barely registered how precious it was. My body seemed to be taking what it needed on its own. My mind couldn't keep pace with the calculation.

*Writing this sentence, I realize freshly that by then, the person I'd been was already beginning to let go of the calculation. I wrote, earlier in this book, several times, that calculating was what let me hold out. But from the third night on, even that calculation started to falter. The strength to calculate was leaving my body along with everything else.*

Sometimes the water's sound seemed to fall into a different rhythm than before. Rising steadily, then swelling once, hard. I couldn't tell whether that was real or something my ears had invented.

---

I opened my mouth again.

The sentences didn't come as easily as they had on the second day. My throat was more ruined now. I managed only a few broken words, in a ragged voice.

"…I was right."

I began there again. The sentence I hadn't finished the night before.

"…I was right. That person came to our house, and our house came apart. The Scales measured it accurately. So I'm right."

Speaking it, I knew it was a repetition. I was saying, word for word, what I'd said the day before.

*I know now why I repeated it. Because that sentence had never been finished. A person repeats an unfinished sentence, over and over. Until they find the end of it.*

---

"…I was right."

I said it again. This time something new attached itself.

"…And yet here I am."

I said that sentence and stopped.

*That moment was strange. I had believed there was no relationship at all between being right and being here, in this place. And yet the moment I said those two sentences side by side, I felt something come loose. Why would a person who's right be here. That question began, for the first time, to take shape.*

I tried to push that dislocation away. My body shook once more.

"…No." I said aloud. "This is just an accident. I just came at a time the gate was closed. This has nothing to do with that."

*I was trying, desperately, to keep two things apart, in that moment. That I was trapped in the water, and that I had run. I wanted to stop those two things becoming the same story. One was just bad luck, an accident. The other was something I had chosen. I believed that as long as I kept them apart, I could still remain the person who was right.*

*A person wants to draw a line between what they did and what happened to them. As long as the line holds, the responsibility splits in half too. I held onto that line, and it held me through that night. Not for long.*

*But I know now. The two really were related. If I hadn't run, I would never have been in this conduit to begin with. The accident happened on the road I had chosen. It wasn't a thing "accident" could fully cover.*

---

I hugged my knees under the water. And spoke again.

"…I didn't run."

My voice cracked.

"…I just… wanted to see that city fall. Is that wrong? Our house fell apart. Isn't it wrong for that city, that one city, to go untouched?"

I said that and caught my breath.

"…I'm not a bad person."

*Saying that, I heard something different in my own voice, for the first time. Not certainty. A plea. "I'm not a bad person" isn't something a person says when they already know they aren't. It's something said by someone who doubts.*

I tried to erase that doubt by counting numbers again. A hundred and twelve cases. No misjudgments. My weight, the lowest in my cohort.

"…I read a hundred and twelve cases." I said. "Not one wrong. The Scales confirmed it."

*I felt, in that moment, for the first time, that I was using numbers as a shield. Numbers had always calmed me, in that dark. This time was different. The more I spoke them, the more unsteady I felt. That the hundred and twelve had been right, and whether what I was trying to do here, now, was right — those were two different questions. I was trying to bury them both under the same number.*

---

I stopped speaking for a long while.

*I stop longest, writing this section. Rereading what came out of me here is still hard. And still, I won't cut it. Without this part, what happens afterward makes no sense. I resolved, at the start of this book, that I would rather not write it at all than write it without the parts that shame me. Facing this sentence, I confirm that resolve again.*

My body swayed. Whether from sitting too long in the water or something else, I couldn't tell. I opened my mouth again.

I pictured my brother's face, then my mother's, then my father's, one by one, in order. Brother first, then mother, then father. Last I tried to picture my own face. Strangely, that was the blurriest of all.

"…Let my brother come back."

I didn't know where that came from. I hadn't meant to say anything like it.

"…Let my mother come back. Let my father… not live alone anymore."

*I still don't know whether I can call this a prayer. I spoke it not knowing who I was speaking to. The Scales? The Scales wasn't a thing that listened to this kind of thing. And yet I spoke as if something were listening.*

I fell quiet after that and listened. Not expecting an answer. Only out of habit — a feeling that speaking should be met with some kind of response.

The water's sound stayed the same. I wasn't disappointed by that. I hadn't believed, to begin with, that I had any right to expect an answer.

*Looking back, that's strange. I was asking for help while believing, at the same time, that I had no right to receive it. I never thought that contradiction was strange, at the time. It felt, to me, entirely natural. A right person has no need to ask for help, and a person who needs help has already done something wrong — that's what I'd been taught, growing up. Anyone raised in the world of the Scales thinks that way.*

And still I asked. I went on asking even while believing I had no right to. That this was strange, too, I didn't notice, at the time. I just couldn't help asking. There was nothing else left that I could do.

---

"…But that city must not be allowed to turn."

I came back to that, again. I don't know how many times I'd already circled back to it.

"…There was never a door like that for my house. That city — only that city — mustn't be allowed a door."

Saying it, I noticed something strange. I was praying for two things at once. That my house would go back to how it had been. And that the Reckoning would never, ever be allowed to go back.

*That those two wishes could come from the same mouth at the same time didn't strike me as strange, then. It does now. I wanted mercy for myself while trying, at the same time, to keep mercy from reaching someone else. How much of a contradiction that was, I couldn't see, that third night.*

I kept speaking, carrying that contradiction.

"…If there's mercy." I said. "It should have come to my house first. Why did it go to that city first."

I stopped partway through. What I'd just said sounded strange to my own ear. I seemed to believe mercy came in a line, taking its turn — someone first, someone after.

*In that moment, for a very brief instant, I felt something was wrong. If mercy comes standing in a queue, then I was, right now, furious that someone else had taken my place in line. But does mercy really come that way? I couldn't answer that question. I didn't even have the strength, then, to ask it. I just pushed the feeling aside and picked anger back up. Anger, at least, gave me strength to hold on.*

---

My body felt feverish. I touched my forehead. I couldn't tell, by hand, whether it was hot or cold.

*I learned later. I was in a state of mild exhaustion, by then. Three days with barely any food, my body constantly submerged in cold water — of course I was. But in that moment, I couldn't tell whether it was my body or my mind that was the problem.*

I closed my eyes and opened them again. The dark was the same. That closing my eyes and opening them made no difference at all felt, for the first time, genuinely frightening.

*I couldn't name that fear. Whether what I'd just said was prayer or curse, I couldn't tell, then. I still don't know, exactly, even now. Maybe it was both.*

Around then, a strange face rose in my mind. The face of the person who'd come from the Reckoning. Three years ago, at the edge of town, the face that had said to me, "You too. Be well." I didn't know why it came to me now.

*I tried, then, to hate that person. In the dark, my body coming apart, I tried to aim anger at that face. It didn't work well. Strangely, that face wouldn't hold still as a target for anger. It was only a sad face. That fact bewildered me. I had decided, long ago, to hate that person. And now, calling the face back up, my hatred just slipped out of my hands.*

---

"…Why am I here."

I said that. Like talking to myself.

Having said it, I heard the sentence again, as it came back in my own voice.

*I felt, in that moment, that the question carried two meanings at once. One was narrow. Why, of all places, was I trapped here, in this waterway, in front of this closed gate. That one had an answer. Because I'd entered during the closed hours. The other was wider. I was right — so why was I here, now, in this place. That one had no answer.*

"…I was right." I said once more. My voice had grown small.

"…So why am I here."

Only the water answered.

*That question followed me a long while after this. I still don't have a complete answer to it, even now. I only know this much. The moment I first put that question into words, out loud, I felt, faintly, for the first time in my life, that being right and being all right might not be the same thing at all.*

I repeated the question a few more times. My voice grew smaller each time. By the end only my lips moved, hardly any sound left at all.

"…Why am I here."

"…Why am I here."

*That sentence rose to the water's surface, and, unanswered, sank back down again.*

*I didn't count how many times I repeated it. Writing this book, this is the first time I don't regret not having counted. Some things stay whole only if they're never counted.*

My body slowly tipped to one side. I didn't try to stop it. Still leaning on the wall, I ended up half-sitting, half-lying. The water reached nearly to my waist. I could barely feel the cold anymore.

I didn't speak again that night. Not because my throat had fully given out. Not because I had nothing left to say. I simply stayed still, having let the question flow out into the water. Where that question drifted, I didn't watch. I no longer had the strength to.

*I can't tell whether I fell asleep or lost consciousness, there. The next thing I remember is the sensation of my body moving. I wasn't the one moving it.*

*I'll end this chapter here. What happened next, I'll write in the next chapter. This is the whole of what I was still able to do for myself, in that dark. Everything after this was no longer my own doing.*
