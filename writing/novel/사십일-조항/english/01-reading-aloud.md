# Chapter 1. Reading Aloud

The Scales calculates the sentence. But it takes effect only when a person reads it aloud.

I asked why, once. I was eleven. The proclamation supervisor looked at me for a moment, then went back to sorting the papers in his hands and answered.

"That's the rule."

"But why?"

"Because that's the rule."

I didn't like that answer. I didn't know yet that it wasn't an answer at all.

---

Herald training begins with the voice.

We read four hours a day. First short sentences, then long ones, then sentences with other people's names in them. A sentence with someone else's name in it is the hardest thing to read.

The training hall had a high ceiling and no windows. Sound struck the walls and came back. Read for four hours in a room where your own voice returns to you, and before long you can't tell whether it's your voice or the wall's.

The supervisor said he'd chosen that room on purpose.

"Out there, when you read, nobody answers." he said. "So get used to it in here. Your own voice, coming back to you."

What he taught wasn't pronunciation. By the third day everyone's pronunciation sounded more or less the same. What he really taught was **how not to stop.**

"Most people trip on the name." he said. "A name is just letters. Why hold your breath to read letters?"

We practiced reading names. Names we didn't know first, then names we did. In the last week we read fake sentence documents with our own names on them.

When my turn came, I read straight through without stopping once. The supervisor nodded.

"Pheugon. Well done."

The boy sitting beside me looked at me. He had stopped three times on his own name.

*I took that look for admiration, back then. I know now it wasn't. He wasn't envying me. He was afraid of me.*

That boy's name was Had. When training ended, Had didn't become a herald. He never broke the habit of stopping on names.

I said nothing the day Had washed out. Had said nothing to me either. But as he carried his things out, he turned once.

"Why don't you ever stop?"

I couldn't answer.

*I carried that question for three years. By the time I could answer it, Had was gone.*

---

What we read is not the verdict. The verdict has already been handed down by the Scales. What we read is the procedure that makes that verdict audible to the world.

The Scales knows everything. It calculates, without error, the weight of every act committed inside a city. It knows exactly what one stolen loaf of bread is worth, what one lie is worth, what one blow of a hand is worth. People adjust their lives by the figures the Scales gives them.

But the Scales does not speak its own verdicts.

This is the first strangeness of this world. The Scales weighs, the Scales sentences, the Scales executes — and yet reading the verdict aloud, that alone, is left to a person. And not just any person. Someone trained specifically for that one task.

Unread, it has no effect. However precisely the Scales calculates, if no one reads it aloud, the verdict stays a number written on paper.

*I used to think this rule was a flaw in the Scales. One old procedure left standing in an otherwise perfect system. Vestigial. Something that would be done away with, eventually.*

*I doubt I was the only one who thought so. No one asked why the procedure existed. Everyone was satisfied with "because that's the rule."*

*But that procedure wasn't vestigial. It was the one thing, across every one of these cities, that the Scales could not do — and someone had placed a person in that spot. Who did that, and why, I wouldn't think to ask until much, much later.*

---

The year I turned fourteen, I became a herald in full.

My first proclamation was small. A merchant in the market who'd tampered with his own scale weights. His weight hadn't crossed the limit, so he wasn't pushed out of the city — only barred from the market for six months.

I stood before him and read the sentence. He watched me the whole time. A man of about fifty, looking up at a fourteen-year-old.

I read without stopping. Name, charge, disposition, effective date.

I folded the paper when I finished. The merchant said something; I didn't hear it. Not hearing was part of the training too.

*I went home and washed my hands for a long time. I washed them without knowing why.*

That night I tried to remember what the merchant had said. I'd seen the shape of his mouth, so I could guess. Two words, maybe.

I couldn't remember.

*I thought that was the training taking hold. That not-listening had settled into my body.*

*It hadn't. I had heard him. And that same night, I had already erased it. Only the one who erases something knows what was erased. After that I erased a hundred and eleven more times.*

---

Over the three years that followed, I read a hundred and twelve cases.

I kept count, not to boast. I counted because I couldn't have borne it otherwise. While I was counting, it wasn't a person. It was a number.

Of those hundred and twelve, not one was a misjudgment. Not a single one. I checked. For days after each reading I went through the records, wanting to know whether the Scales had ever been wrong. If it had been wrong even once, my reading would have meant something different.

It never had.

*I think I felt reassured, then. If the Scales was never wrong, every sentence I read was a correct sentence. And a person who reads correct sentences has done nothing wrong.*

*I leaned on that logic for a long time. It took forty days to bring it down.*

---

I don't do wrong.

My weight is the lowest in my cohort. The supervisor checked it once, and then checked it again, as if to say, is this right.

I don't lie, I don't touch what belongs to others, I don't get angry. Not that anger doesn't come — I don't let it out. I know those are two different things.

Everyone assumed it was because I was conscientious.

*It wasn't. I wasn't afraid of weight. I believed there was a sentence I would one day have to read, and I didn't want to lose the right to read it.*

*What that sentence was, I had known since I was twelve.*

There is only me in my family now.

I don't intend to tell it all here. I'm not able to tell it all even to myself yet. I'll write down only this much — a few years ago, someone came to stay in our house, and after that person left, our family came apart.

That person came from the Reckoning.

The Scales calculated it. Calculated it precisely. A weight came out, and a disposition came down. I have no complaint about that disposition. It was precise.

*Precise, and nothing came back. I thought for a long time about how those two sentences could stand together, and in the end I stopped thinking about it. Instead I decided to do something else.*

*I decided to become a herald.*

---

Around the start of autumn, the supervisor called me in alone.

His room was always cool. The window faced north, and a list of heralds hung on the wall. A number beside each name — the count of cases that person had read. Beside my name it said 112.

The supervisor set a document on the desk.

"This one's big."

I looked at the document. In the first line was a city's name.

**The Reckoning.**

I looked at those letters. For a long time. The supervisor was explaining something. I couldn't hear it.

"…Pheugon."

"…Yes."

"Are you listening."

"…Yes."

The supervisor looked at me for a moment. Then he tapped the document again.

"This isn't an individual case. It's the whole city. Accumulated weight crossed the limit. A case like this comes once a decade, if that."

"…The whole city?"

"Sentence of demolition." the supervisor said. "Forty-day grace period. Meaning: get your affairs in order and get out, within that time."

"…And the people?"

"They scatter." the supervisor said. "Some go to cities that'll take them in. Some don't get taken in, and live outside."

"…How many people."

The supervisor turned a page.

"A hundred and twenty thousand."

I heard that number. A hundred and twenty thousand. A number I'd never counted to. Counting a hundred and twelve, I had thought that was a lot.

*I had no sense, then, that the number was people. I still don't think that was my fault. No one can feel a hundred and twenty thousand as people. I only think, now, that if even one face had been in it, something might have been different — and forty days later I would learn how wrong that thought was.*

*Even after a face appeared, I still wanted that city to fall.*

*I noticed, in that moment, that my hands weren't shaking. They should have been. Through a hundred and twelve readings my hands had never once shaken, but this time, they should have. They didn't.*

*Because they didn't shake, I knew I had been waiting for this.*

---

"The reason I'm assigning you is simple." the supervisor said. "You don't stop."

"…Yes."

"A whole-city sentence draws a crowd. People cry, they shout, they grab at you. Stop partway through, and you have to start over from the beginning. There have been heralds who stopped more than three times, reading in front of a crowd like that."

The supervisor looked at me.

"You won't stop."

"…Yes."

"That's not a compliment." the supervisor said. "And it doesn't mean this is good for you, either."

I didn't answer.

The supervisor slid the document toward me. He kept his hand on it a moment before he let go.

"Pheugon."

"…Yes."

"Do you have some connection to the Reckoning."

I looked at him. He was looking at the document.

"…No, sir."

The supervisor took his hand off the paper.

"All right."

*I told exactly one lie in that room that day. Weighed out, it would have been a very light lie. The Scales would have calculated it, and that figure is probably sitting in my record somewhere, even now.*

*The Scales never knew that lie was the heaviest one of my life.*

*I didn't know, then, that the supervisor was worried about me. In that room, that day, he was the only one who was.*

---

I walked out of the room with the sentence document in my hand.

The corridor was long. Windows at regular intervals let light and shadow cross my face in turns as I walked.

At the end of the corridor stood a terminal of the Scales. Put a hand on it, and your weight comes up. People at the proclamation office rest a hand on it several times a day. Habit.

I passed it and stopped.

I almost raised my hand, then didn't. I'd just told a lie; the figure would be up a little. Checking how much might have settled my mind. It would only be a small rise, after all.

*I didn't raise that hand that day. And for the forty days after, not once. I understood why only later. It wasn't that I was afraid the number would be high. I was afraid it would be low.*

*I already knew, somewhere in me, that what I was about to do would barely register on the Scales at all.*

I walked on, unfolding the paper. I didn't mean to read it through. I only wanted to see the city's name once more.

The Reckoning.

*I had known that name for a very long time. I had never once said it aloud. Not since I was twelve.*

*And now I had become the person who had to read that name aloud. And not just any line of it — the city's last line.*

I stopped in the middle of the corridor.

And I laughed. Silently, for just a moment.

*I remember that laugh. I couldn't have explained, then, what kind of laugh it was. I can now.*

*It was relief.*

---

At home I laid the sentence document on my desk.

I didn't read it. A herald isn't permitted to read a sentence aloud before proclamation day. That's a rule too. The moment it's read, it takes effect.

I only skimmed it with my eyes. City name, accumulated weight, basis of calculation, grace period, method of execution. The sentences were short and exact. The Scales always writes that way.

And at the very bottom of the page.

The type was one size smaller than the rest, set close to the margin. The first time I saw it, I took it for a printing error.

**Article 4, Clause 11.**

I stopped on that line.

It was one sentence.

> Should this city, within forty days, be confirmed to have turned, this sentence shall be void.

I read that sentence, and read it again, and a third time.

*Turned.*

Not a word the Scales uses. The Scales writes "weight decreases," or "the figure falls below the limit." It doesn't use words that name a direction. The Scales has no category called direction.

But this sentence had one.

In a hundred and twelve sentence documents, I had never once seen a sentence like it. This clause doesn't attach to individual cases. Only to whole-city cases. And a whole-city case comes once a decade, if that.

*I didn't understand, that night, exactly what the sentence meant. I never asked who "confirmed" meant — confirmed by whom. I assumed the Scales would confirm it.*

*It would take twenty more days from that night before I learned the Scales cannot confirm it. And standing in the place where I learned that, I would, for the first time, understand precisely why I had run.*

I traced the sentence with my fingertip. The paper was thin. The small type set into the margin sat slightly out of line with the body text, as though someone had added it in later.

*I don't know how many times I looked at that line again that night. I didn't count. It was the first time in my life I'd forgotten to count.*
