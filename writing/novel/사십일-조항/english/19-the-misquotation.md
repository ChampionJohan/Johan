# Chapter 19. The Misquotation

> _Previously_ — In the room of records, Pheugon learned that the Scales adjusts sentences based on past precedent. Records are made by people; the Scales only takes them in and calculates — he dimly sensed there was exactly one point where a human hand touched the process. Three days later he found a name on an old box. Mena.

I headed for the room of records early that morning.

I found the spot the record-keeper had marked and opened the remaining boxes one by one. Most had city names on them, and inside, the large and small incidents from that city, arranged in precedent form. A few boxes held almost identical content. Someone who stole something, someone who couldn't repay a debt, someone who quarreled with a neighbor. Stories not much different, city to city.

*I was growing tired in that repetition. I still didn't know what I was looking for. There was nothing to do but keep opening boxes until something caught.*

Then, on the bottom shelf, in a spot less noticeable than the others, I found the small box.

The box was smaller than the others.

The writing on the outside was faded, but readable. Not a city's name. A single person's name, and beneath it, in small letters: "Original testimony."

I lifted the box off the shelf and set it on the table.

*Until then I'd assumed every box carried a city's name. This one was different. It carried a person's name. I found that strange. Why was this one alone filed by name instead of city?*

I opened the lid. Inside were a few sheets of paper, and a scroll far more worn than the rest. The paper felt so thin it might crumble at a touch. Dust came off on my fingertips.

---

I unrolled the scroll.

Handwriting. Not a sentence printed by the Scales. An honest but unpolished hand. There were even traces of having been written in haste.

A name was written at the top.

**MENA.**

I began reading the sentences beneath it.

It was testimony. Something about a certain city, a certain incident, and a person named Mena who'd been involved with it somehow. Exactly what the incident was, I couldn't fully make out — the writing broke off in places and had worn away with age. But the final paragraph had survived relatively intact.

> I went to that person many times. I wanted to hear why he'd made that choice. Every time, he turned the conversation aside, or fell silent. On the last day I went to him, I asked. Why won't you say anything at all. He didn't answer. **This one never explained himself.** I am setting that down as it is. It is all I know.

I read that sentence twice. The room was so quiet that even the sound of turning a page felt loud. The sentence hadn't changed by the third reading either.

*This one never explained himself.*

A simple sentence. An observation about someone. Not a judgment — just a fact. Mena hadn't written that the man was bad. She hadn't written whether he had repented or not. She had only written that he never explained.

---

I was about to put the scroll back into the box when I noticed there was more paper inside.

Printed paper. The format the Scales produces. A precedent summary.

I unfolded it.

The first line held a summary of the case. Same incident as the testimony I'd just read. Same date, same names involved. My eyes moved down.

I wanted to see how the Scales had summarized this testimony.

> According to the testimony of witness Mena, **this one never repented to the end.** This is applied as a mitigating factor in future similar cases.

I read that sentence and stopped there.

---

I laid the two sheets side by side in my hands.

The scroll read: *This one never explained himself.*

The precedent summary read: *This one never repented.*

*I forgot to breathe, in that moment. The two sentences looked similar. As if only a few characters differed. But those few characters were saying something entirely different. "Never explained himself" was a statement about what the man had done. "Never repented" was a judgment about the man's heart. Mena had written only the first. But what survived in the Scales' record was the second.*

I stood up from my seat and sat back down again. My hands were shaking. I kept straightening the two papers on the table, over and over, though they hadn't actually gone crooked.

*I understood, immediately, what this meant. I'd spent three years confirming that the Scales never miscalculates. But this wasn't a matter of calculation. This was the material that had gone into the Scales, wrong, from the very start. Mena had said "he never explained himself," and the Scales had read it as "he never repented," and preserved that judgment, fixed in place, as a reference factor. And that reference factor was, by the record-keeper's own words, still adjusting the sentences of other cases, somewhere, at this very moment.*

---

I cross-checked the scroll against the summary several more times. I wanted to be sure I hadn't misread something.

I hadn't. "Never explained himself" and "never repented" were unmistakably different sentences. The original held the first; the Scales' record held the second.

I fought the urge to run to the record-keeper.

*I was, strangely, calm in that moment. The excitement, if anything, quieted me. For three years, reading a hundred and twelve cases, I'd lived leaning on the fact that the Scales had never once been wrong. That sentence was the pillar holding up my life. And now, in my own hands, I held proof of a crack in that pillar.*

I began thinking, in that moment, about what I could do with this.

---

I searched further through the box. I wanted to know who Mena was, what city she was from.

Behind the testimony scroll was a short introductory paragraph. Worn away by half, but a few sentences remained legible.

> …One who keeps records. Belongs to no city; travels between many, writing down what people say. Sees people from a place the Scales cannot reach, in a way the Scales cannot reach…

I read that passage several times over.

*It was the first time I imagined a person named Mena. Someone whose face and name I knew nothing of. And yet a single sentence she'd left behind was being used, after all this time, inside the Scales' grounds, with an entirely different meaning. I felt that was unjust. Whether I felt it was unjust on Mena's behalf, or on behalf of whoever that sentence had pointed to, I couldn't tell, at the time.*

The record-keeper passed by and glanced at the paper in my hand.

"Looking at Mena's record?" the record-keeper asked. She looked surprised. "Not many people know about that one."

Her voice dropped slightly. I didn't miss that change.

"…Is it a famous record, normally?"

"Not exactly famous." The record-keeper shrugged. "There's talk. That Mena's records are scattered across several cities, and each one quotes them a little differently. I've only heard it as rumor myself. Never checked."

I went stiff at that.

"…Several cities? Not just here?"

"So they say," the record-keeper said. "Though that's not particularly strange, really. It's common enough for a record's format to shift a little as it passes between cities."

*Listening to that, I was privately drawing a different picture in my head. It wasn't the format changing. It was the meaning changing. I thought, in that moment, for the first time, that this might not be a single simple mistake. If Mena's sentence had been misquoted not just in this city but elsewhere too, then the error was much older, and had spread much wider, than I'd assumed.*

---

I copied both sentences onto a piece of paper. I was afraid I might not be able to see the original again.

*Original*: This one never explained himself.

*Precedent*: This one never repented.

Written out side by side, the difference stood even clearer. Explaining is an action. You can do it or not. Repentance is a matter of the heart. The very thing the Scales couldn't measure itself, the thing closest to what the Adjudicator called "direction." I knew the Scales couldn't weigh that on its own. And yet here, right inside the Scales' own grounds, sat a judgment about someone's heart, brazen. And it was a judgment the original testimony had never made at all.

*I was certain, in that moment. This was the crack. If I brought this to the Adjudicator — no, if I told anyone about it before that — one of the Scales' grounds would collapse. And if that ground collapsed, other rulings built on top of it would shake along with it. For the first time, I felt like I had a clear plan.*

*I stayed drunk on that feeling all through the afternoon. For three years I had only ever been the one who read. The one who spoke someone else's sentence aloud. Now, for the first time, I felt like someone who knew more than the Scales did. That feeling was frighteningly sweet.*

*I didn't know, that night, how short-lived that certainty would be.*

---

I noticed a small notation at the bottom of the precedent summary. A case number, and a list recording which other rulings had cited this case as a reference factor since. Not a short list. Ten-some lines of case numbers were laid out.

*Looking at that list, a chill ran down my spine. This wasn't a single error attached to one person. Every case listed there might have received a slightly heavier sentence, on the grounds of that wrong sentence, "never repented." I hoped that even a few of them had happened right here, in this city. That would let me show that this error had already exercised real force, right here, in the very city I was standing in.*

At the very bottom of the list was an item with the most recent date. I copied that case number onto my paper.

*I didn't know. Where that number would take me. I just decided, that day, to follow it the next morning.*

---

The record-keeper came to tidy up the room toward evening and saw me.

"Late again today," the record-keeper said. "Find anything?"

I hesitated for a moment.

"…No," I said. I quietly closed the box. "Just looking around."

The record-keeper nodded without much thought and went back to tidying.

*I didn't feel guilty telling that lie, that night. Strange thing. I'd felt heavy in my chest every time I'd lied to Trope, these past days. But not in that moment. I believed I was doing something right. I learned, for the first time, that a lie grows lighter in front of something right. I would learn later how dangerous that lesson was.*

---

I met Trope at the well. Filling her jar, she caught sight of me and tilted her head.

"Your face looks strange today," Trope said. "Something good happen?"

I nearly let out a laugh.

"…Why?"

"Your steps are faster than usual," Trope said. "And you keep touching your chest. Hiding something?"

Only then did I realize I'd been unconsciously touching the paper inside my shirt.

"…It's nothing," I said.

*I nearly told her, right then. I found proof that the Scales is wrong, I nearly said. I imagined, briefly, saying that in front of Trope. I couldn't picture what expression she'd make. I couldn't hold that imagining for long. Her mother's face kept overlapping it. Would the Scales being wrong be a comfort to Trope, or would it be something else. I didn't know.*

"Strange one," Trope said, lifting her jar. She was smiling. "Well, you'll tell me eventually, I suppose."

Trope walked on ahead. I looked at her back for a moment, then followed.

*I thought about that smile all that evening. In front of it, I felt the weight of the paper I was carrying, differently, for the first time. It was no longer evidence for me alone. That evidence was aimed at the very city Trope lived in. I pushed that fact aside, hard. Not yet, I told myself.*

---

Walking home, I held the paper tight against my chest.

*I didn't toss and turn that night, for once. In fact, I slept easily, for the first time in a long while. I believed I'd found something. Proof that the Scales isn't perfect. Holding that proof, I felt I could go back to seeing this city as a number. A city built on an imprecise calculation. What was there to be sorry about in that city's falling?*

*That was what I was thinking as I fell asleep. The next morning, I took the paper and went back to the room of records. This time, I intended to trace all the way through to how this precedent had actually been used in a real ruling. If I confirmed that ruling had come out wrong, I'd have solid proof.*

*I didn't know, then, that what I'd find at the end of that trail would be entirely different from what I was hoping for.*

*That night I read those two sentences aloud several times over. Never explained himself. Never repented. Every time I read them back and forth, the difference felt larger. Someone who never explained himself could simply be a quiet person. Someone who never repented was a sinner. The Scales — no, the Scales' grounds — was turning silence into sin. I stayed caught in that thought for a long while, and eventually fell asleep.*
