# Chapter 13. You're Not From Here

> _Previously_ — Trapped behind a closed sluice gate, Pheugon spent three days in darkness. For the first time, he spoke aloud — words he had never said to anyone. On the third day the gate opened, and the current carried him out. He opened his eyes to an unfamiliar well and an unfamiliar sky. A passing child looked down at him and asked — "You're not from here, are you?" He looked up at the sign. The Reckoning.

The question came first, and the name of the city came after.

I remember the order. Exactly that order. I heard the question first, and only then did I lift my eyes to the sign. If it had happened the other way around, I think I would have passed out again before I could answer.

**THE RECKONING.**

Two words cut into stone. A bronze plate set into a low post beside the well's coping. Rusted. An old plate. It meant the city had been calling itself that for a very long time.

*I think I laughed, in that moment. No sound came out. My body had no strength left for sound. But the corners of my mouth moved. What kind of laugh it was, I still can't explain. Washed up in the very place I'd fled — after three days, no less, and naked and scoured by the water — whether that was funny or terrifying, I still haven't decided.*

---

"…Are you all right?"

The child asked again. I hadn't answered the first time.

I tried to answer, but only a watery sound came out of my throat. It felt like three days' worth of swallowed water was still inside me.

The child bent down and threw a rope toward me — one of the ropes that always hung at the well. I caught it with both hands. My hands had almost no strength left, but the child looped the other end around the post and began hauling, putting their whole weight into it.

Not a light body. Or rather — not weight so much as technique. I learned later that this child had grown up at the well. Someone who had learned rope work in their bones.

I was dragged up over the coping stone. And collapsed there.

The stone was warm. The sun was out. The first sun I'd seen in three days.

*I still remember that warmth. The first sensation I felt, after three days in the dark. Not resentment, not fear — just warmth. The body was that simple. Even while the mind was tangled, the body still knew warm as warm.*

My eyes slowly adjusted to the light. I turned my head to the side. A low wall ran around the well, and beyond it I could see rooftops. All the same color. Same tile, same pitch. In the city I came from, every roof was a different color — depending on means, depending on when it had last been repaired. Here it wasn't like that.

A few people passed by, glanced toward the well, and kept walking. No one stopped. As if pulling a person out of the water wasn't unusual here — or as if, even if it was, no one stopped for what wasn't their business.

*I thought, at the time, that this was coldness. I learned later that it wasn't. The people of this city weren't cold. They were all busy keeping their own numbers. Spending time on someone else's business, meddling in someone else's business — either one could be weighed. So no one stopped walking.*

---

The child crouched beside me. Around fourteen, I guessed. Maybe a year younger than me, but the eyes didn't match that.

"You came out of the conduit?" the child asked. "From down there?"

"…Yes."

"It should've been closed. It's not the hour it opens, these days."

I didn't answer. I had no strength to answer, and I didn't know what to say.

The child looked me up and down. Assessing eyes. Not rude — just trying to be accurate.

"Your wrist," the child said.

"…What?"

"There's nothing on your wrist."

I looked at my own wrist. Nothing there but wrinkled, waterlogged skin. I didn't understand.

"Everyone from here has one," the child said. "A tag. Fitted when you're born. You don't have one. So—"

The child stopped and looked at me again.

"—you're not from here, are you?"

*I didn't know, then, what the tag was. Two days later, in the square, I'd see one for myself. And the moment I did, I'd feel for the first time how differently this city ran. Until then, I just sat there in a stolen body.*

---

"…No," I said. "Not from here."

It wasn't a lie. It couldn't have been, really. I truly wasn't from this city.

"Where'd you come from, then?"

I couldn't answer. I had no idea what would happen if I said the real name of my city out loud. I knew nothing about whether people commonly used the conduits to reach this place, or whether that alone would raise suspicion.

The child seemed to read my silence differently.

"It's fine," the child said. "You don't have to say if you don't want to. Plenty of people here are like that."

"…People like that?"

"People with things they don't want to talk about." The child shrugged. "What's your name?"

I wasn't at all ready for that question.

*If someone asks your name, you have to give one. I froze in front of that simple rule. My name was the name that would be written on the Reckoning's sentence document. The name on the herald roster. I knew, by instinct alone, that I couldn't say it here.*

Something came out of my mouth before I decided anything.

"…Had," I said.

Only after I'd said it did I realize what name that was.

*Why that name came out first, I didn't know at the time. Had was the name of a boy who'd left the training grounds three years before. Maybe I picked it because I'd never run into him again. Or maybe it was for some other reason. I carried that question a long time after that day.*

"Had," the child repeated. "I'm Trope."

---

Trope stood and looked down at me.

"Can you stand?"

I tried and sat back down. My legs had no strength at all. Trope sighed and pulled me up by the arm. Strong grip.

Standing, I checked my bag. It had stayed on my back through the current, and the strap hadn't torn. I couldn't check whether what was inside had survived. I couldn't open it in front of Trope.

*Inside the bag was the sentence document. The Reckoning's sentence of demolition. I'd wrapped it in oiled paper, but after three days underwater I assumed it couldn't have survived. It had. I'd learn later that this was no accident.*

Trope started walking ahead. I limped after her.

"Where are we going?" I asked.

"Somewhere to dry off, first." Trope didn't look back. "You show up in the square looking like that, everyone's going to stare."

---

Walking, I saw the city for the first time.

Quiet. That was my first impression. The city I came from was loud in the mornings. The market opening, merchants shouting, children running.

This city wasn't like that. People walked. Quietly, at an exact pace. Neither hurried nor idle. The streets were clean. Not a single piece of trash. Every doorstep swept.

And people kept looking up.

*I didn't know, at the time, what that habit was. I thought they were checking the sky. Watching the weather. The next day I'd learn they weren't looking at the sky at all. They were looking at numbers hanging high on the building walls.*

An old man passed us and nodded to Trope. Trope nodded back the same way. I couldn't tell if they knew each other, or if that was just how everyone greeted everyone in this city.

"Are people here always this quiet?" I asked.

Trope slowed for a moment.

"It's not quiet," Trope said. "Everyone's counting."

"…Counting what?"

Trope didn't answer. She just started walking again.

*I thought, at the time, that not answering meant indifference. It wasn't. Trope wasn't holding back words from me — she was someone who had to re-confirm the answer to that question, herself, every single day. I only understood that much later.*

We turned a corner and a small square came into view. Not many people. Something stood in the middle — I only caught it in passing and didn't know what it was. A post about twice a person's height, with something fixed to the top.

"What's that?" I asked.

Trope answered without even looking toward it.

"A terminal. There are plenty more besides that one."

"…A terminal that measures what?"

Trope stopped walking and turned to look at me. For the first time, her expression shifted, just slightly. I couldn't tell if it was surprise or something else.

"You really don't know?" Trope asked.

I couldn't answer. I didn't know whether to choose pretending not to know when I did, or actually not knowing.

"…This is my first time seeing one," I managed to say.

A lie. Few people knew what a Scales terminal was as well as I did. But I truly had never seen one shaped like this city's before.

Trope didn't press further. She just started walking again. But after that, the way she looked at me seemed slightly different.

*I couldn't read that look, then. Now I know. Trope didn't start suspecting me at that moment. It was the opposite. She had never met anyone who claimed not to know the Scales, and she'd started to wonder what kind of person that made me.*

---

Trope led me to a small shed not far from the well. It looked like a laundry-drying place. A few empty lines hung there, and an old blanket lay folded in a corner.

"Dry off here," Trope said. "I'll come back in a bit."

"…Why?"

"Why what?"

"…Why are you helping me?"

Trope looked at me for a moment. There was something in her eyes I couldn't read yet.

"It'd be strange to just leave someone who nearly drowned," Trope said. A very simple answer. Simple enough that it made me suspicious.

Trope left the door half-open on her way out.

---

I wrapped myself in the blanket and sat huddled in the corner of the shed. I listened to the sounds outside. Footsteps, a door opening and closing, someone occasionally calling someone else's name. None of it carried any urgency. In the city I came from, evening brought the market closing and children being called home, all layered on top of each other at once. Here the sounds didn't layer. One at a time, in order.

About half an hour later, Trope came back. She was carrying a set of dry clothes and a piece of bread.

"Put these on," Trope said, tossing the clothes to me. "They're my brother's. He doesn't wear them anymore."

"…Your brother?"

"Doesn't live here," Trope said, curtly. A tone that said not to ask more.

I didn't ask more. I took the clothes and changed. Trope stepped outside and stood with her back turned.

I was halfway through the bread when Trope came back in and sat down across from me.

"Had." Trope used my fake name. "What are you going to do here?"

I hadn't prepared an answer for that.

"…I don't know," I said. This time it wasn't a lie.

"Do you have anywhere to go?"

"…No."

Trope looked at me a moment, then nodded. She didn't ask more. She was someone who rationed her questions. I couldn't tell, then, whether that was consideration or indifference.

"I'll come back tomorrow morning," Trope said, standing. "You can stay here. Nobody much comes by."

"…Why do you keep looking out for me?"

Trope stopped at the door. She seemed to be choosing her answer.

"I don't know," Trope finally said. "Just because."

Then she left.

*I thought about that "just because" for a long time afterward. Set against what I learned later, that answer wasn't a lie. Trope really didn't know the reason. No — it wasn't that there was no reason, but that she must have thought it was still too soon to put that reason into her own words. I wouldn't learn what that reason was until much later.*

---

I pulled the blanket around myself. Then I opened my bag and checked inside.

The sentence document was wet, but not torn. The lettering hadn't run. I wrapped it back in the oiled paper and pushed it deep into the bag.

*That was the first time I was truly alone inside this city. Stranger, somehow, than three days of darkness. In the dark, at least, I'd known where I had to go. Not now. I was sitting there with the very sentence I'd fled from, hidden in a bag, in the very city that sentence was meant for.*

Evening light came through the gaps in the shed. I sat leaning against the wall, wrapped in the blanket. My body was exhausted, but sleep wouldn't come.

I'd hated this city for three years. In those three years, I'd never once actually seen it. I'd only known its name. That hating a place for three years on nothing but a name was possible — until that moment, I'd never once thought it strange.

And now I was lying under this city's blanket, wearing this city's clothes, eating bread handed to me by this city's child.

*I didn't see that contradiction clearly that night. I had no strength left to see it. A body that had survived three days had no energy left to think. But I felt one thing. That hatred could go this quiet in front of the thing it hated. I was afraid of that. I didn't yet know what came next, once hatred went quiet.*

Somewhere in the distance, something like a bell rang once. Low and short. I didn't know what it signaled. Trope was already gone, and there was no one to ask.

I closed my eyes. And for the first time in a long while, I fell asleep without counting anything.

*I wouldn't learn until the next morning that this was the last night in this city I would fall asleep without counting.*
