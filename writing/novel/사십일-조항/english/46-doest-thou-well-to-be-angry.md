# Chapter 46. Doest Thou Well to Be Angry

> _Previously_ — The vine withered overnight. Pheugon was angrier than he'd been watching the city live — and he noticed it himself. That night, he no longer waited for the city to fall. He was only waiting for something to end.

I heard footsteps the next morning.

I assumed they were Trope's. But it wasn't the gait of someone carrying a water jug. It was slower, more even.

I looked up.

It was him.

*I wasn't surprised, strangely, in that moment. Four days had passed since I'd shouted at him near the gate and walked off. I hadn't once expected, in all that time, that he would come find me. And yet the moment I saw him standing there on the hill, I somehow felt I had already known this moment was coming.*

He hadn't walked up from the direction of the city. Exactly which direction he'd come from, I still can't say, even now. He was simply there, at some point. I didn't find that strange. Over these three days I had been learning how to grow used to strange things.

---

He stood beside the dry vine stem and looked down at it for a moment. He said nothing. Then he came and sat beside me, as if it were nothing at all.

I didn't push him away. I didn't welcome him either.

"…How did you get up here." I asked. My voice had no strength in it.

"…I walked." he said.

I nearly laughed at that answer. I didn't, quite.

*I noticed, then, that his clothes were covered in dust. There was dirt on his shoes too. The look of someone who had really walked. I found that strange. He seemed like the kind of person who could simply appear. And yet he'd taken the trouble to walk. I didn't ask why. I didn't ask, but I went on remembering those dirt-covered shoes for a long time after.*

---

We sat a while without speaking.

The city was visible below. Morning sunlight caught brightly on the roofs. A rooster crowed somewhere, faintly.

I looked at the dry stem. He looked at it too.

"…This." I said first. "Did you send this?"

"…I sent nothing." he said.

"Then what?"

He didn't answer. I accepted that silence, this time, as something familiar. A silence with no answer, and a silence that withholds an answer — I'd learned, in the time since, that those were different things. And this silence now was the latter. He wasn't staying quiet because he didn't know the answer. He was waiting for me to reach it myself.

---

"…You were angry." he said. It was hard to tell whether it was a question or a confirmation.

"…Yesterday?" I asked. "Or the day before?"

"…Both."

I picked up a dry leaf between my fingertips. It crumbled the moment I touched it.

"…I was angry both times." I admitted. "But strangely, today, I'm angrier about the vine dying. More than I was about the city living. I know how strange that is."

"…So you already knew." he said quietly.

"…Yes. I figured it out alone, yesterday. No one told me."

"…Did you tell the Adjudicator." he asked.

"…No."

"…Trope."

"…No. No one." I looked at the dry stem again. "It's not something worth telling anyone. The one who saved a city getting angrier over a dead plant — who do you say that to."

"…You told me."

I stopped at that.

"…You're different." I said. It wasn't an answer I could fully explain, even to myself.

---

He looked at me for a moment.

There was no judgment in his eyes. No surprise either. I tried to count how many times I'd shouted in front of this person. Provoking him. Getting angry. Nearly crying. Every time, he had looked at me with the same eyes. I wasn't afraid of those eyes anymore.

"Pheugon." he said.

"…Yes."

"Doest thou well to be angry."

---

I couldn't answer that question right away.

*I can still repeat that sentence back exactly, even now. He said it very plainly. Not loudly, not weighted. In the same tone you'd use to ask about today's weather. That made it more frightening, not less. If it had been a rebuke, I could have argued with it. But it was a real question.*

"…Which one are you asking about." I asked. "The city living, or the vine dying."

"…Whichever you'd rather answer."

I opened my mouth, then closed it.

---

"…The city living." I managed at last. "I think that's right. My being angry about that. I lost my family, and that city survived without paying anything for it. I spent three years keeping my own weight low, just enduring. And those people just got a little kinder, and got everything handed back to them. Is it wrong for me to be angry about that? I don't think so."

He nodded. Not agreement, not denial — just a nod.

"…You brought up Trope's mother too." he said. "Near the gate."

"…Yes. I did."

He glanced toward the city, then back at me.

"…Do you still think that, now?"

I thought for a moment.

"…That's changed a little." I said. "Back then I used Trope's mother as evidence for my own anger. Thinking about it now, that wasn't right. Trope's grief is Trope's. It wasn't mine to borrow."

"…When did you realize that."

"…Just now. While I was saying it."

He seemed to smile with his eyes alone, very slightly, at that. I couldn't be sure. I turned my head to look more closely, and by then his face had already returned to normal.

"…What about the vine." he asked.

I couldn't answer.

*Before that question, for the first time, I felt my own logic give out. My anger at the city had logic behind it. The logic of unfairness. But my anger at the vine had no logic at all. It was just anger at something of mine being gone. I was feeling both angers at the same size, and yet I could explain one and not the other.*

"…Am I strange?" I asked. For the first time, there was pleading in my voice. "This whole time, I thought all my anger had reasons. My family, Trope's mother, the unfairness of this world. But in front of the vine, I can't name a single reason. I'm just angry. I must be a much smaller person than I thought."

"…What do you mean by small." he asked.

"…Someone who gets angry without a reason. Not righteous anger — just greedy anger."

"…I've never divided the two that way." he said.

I looked at him. There was still no judgment on his face.

"…What do you mean?"

"…What you call righteous anger, and anger without a reason. I don't think they split that cleanly. A person's anger usually comes in layers. The reason on top looks the most convincing. But underneath it, other things are there too."

*Hearing that, I looked back into my own anger again. Beneath my anger at the city — was there really only outrage at unfairness there? Or was there, somewhere underneath, the same kind of thing I'd felt when I lost the vine — a hurt that what was mine hadn't been recognized? I had no confidence I could answer that question honestly.*

---

"…I don't know." I said. "The vine was just mine. Even though it was only two days. When it was gone, I got angry. Angrier than about the city living. I know that doesn't make sense."

"…Do you think it doesn't make sense."

"…Yes."

"…Why."

I was stuck on that question again.

"…The city is a hundred twenty thousand people. The vine is just a plant. There's no comparison."

"…Whose arithmetic decides there's no comparison." he asked.

*At that question I thought, for an instant, of the Scales. The Scales would calculate it that way. That the weight of a hundred twenty thousand people and the weight of one vine can't be compared. But I wasn't the Scales. I was a person. And I already knew that a person's heart doesn't calculate the way the Scales does.*

---

"…It's my arithmetic." I said. My voice grew smaller. "But my heart doesn't calculate that way. My heart feels that two days of shade, given to me, is bigger than a hundred twenty thousand lives. I know that's wrong."

I paused a moment, then went on.

"…For three years I kept my own weight low and believed that made me an accurate person. Like the Scales. But now I see I'm nothing like the Scales at all. The Scales weighs a hundred twenty thousand people and one vine the same way. I don't. I use a different scale for what's mine."

"…Isn't that just what being a person is." he said.

"…Is that supposed to be comfort?"

"…No." he said. "Just a fact."

"…I never said it was wrong." he said.

"Then are you saying it's right?"

"…I never said that either."

I looked at him. The frustration rose in me again.

"Then what is it. I want to know, right now, whether my anger is right or wrong."

---

He looked at me for a long time.

"…I can't give you that answer." he said. "It's not that I'm withholding it. That answer was never meant to be given as an answer, to begin with."

"…Then how is it given?"

"…By living."

I didn't understand what he meant.

"…What does that mean." I asked. "That I'll know after a few more years?"

"…It could take years. It could happen in a day." he said. "I can't set that time for you. But I know this much. This question isn't one that closes with an answer today. Today is just the day you heard this question, clearly, for the first time."

*Hearing that, I felt strangely at ease. I had been raised believing every question had to have an answer. A sentence document had a disposition. The Scales had a number. That you could carry an unanswered question and go on living — I heard that, for the first time, in that moment.*

"…So I have to go on carrying this question?"

"…Is that a bad thing."

I couldn't answer that question either.

*In that moment I wanted to press him further. Something stopped me. Before that question, I felt for the first time that it was all right not to know the answer. Or rather — it was all right to sit here, in this place, not knowing.*

---

We sat in silence again for a long while.

The city stayed below, exactly as it was. The dry stem lay between us. The wind blew. In that wind, I heard his question again. Doest thou well to be angry.

I repeated that question to myself, again and again. The first time I'd heard it, the sentence had felt like an arrow aimed at me. Each time I heard it again, it sounded slightly different. Now it no longer felt aimed at me — it felt like it was sitting beside me, facing the same direction I was. I learned, that day, that a question could sit with someone too.

*I still take this question out sometimes, even now. When I think about being angry that the city lived. When I think about being angry that the vine died. Each time, the answer wavers a little differently. Some days it feels like I was right. Other days, not. I've decided now to accept that very wavering as the answer. Only the Scales has an unwavering answer. I'm not the Scales.*

*I never did answer that question. Not that day, and not for a long time after. Even now, writing these words, I still don't have a certain answer to it. Some questions have to stay unanswered to remain questions. I may have already wished, in that moment, for this question to go on following me and never leave.*

He didn't stand. Neither did I. We just sat there, side by side, with that unanswered question between us.

The sun climbed a little higher. The city's roofs caught the morning light, one by one. Watching that glimmer, I thought of Trope down there somewhere, and the Adjudicator, and the baker, and countless other people whose names I didn't know, all beginning their day. None of them would ever know what conversation had passed on this hill today.

*That fact, strangely, didn't weigh on me. Some conversations don't need to be known by anyone. That day, for the first time, I felt that not everything needs to be proven or explained. In front of the Scales, I had always been someone under proof. By numbers, by records, by evidence. Up on this hill, there was nothing to prove. I only had to sit.*

The wind stirred the dry stem slightly. A few broken bits of leaf rose into the air, then slowly settled back down. I followed them with my eyes. He didn't. He was still watching me.
