# Chapter 41. Anger

> _Previously_ — The city lives. While everyone wept and laughed, Pheugon alone couldn't. Night came, and the Adjudicator sought him out. "It feels strange," the Adjudicator said. Pheugon still didn't know why his hands were shaking. He didn't sleep that night.

I watched morning come with my eyes open.

I hadn't slept a wink all night. My body was tired, but my mind was strangely clear. Leaning against the wall, I watched the darkness turn gray, and the gray turn blue.

*All that night I thought of exactly one thing. More precisely — I didn't think it. It simply grew larger inside me, on its own. I didn't try to give it a name. I felt that naming it would make it real.*

Once the sun was fully up, I got to my feet. My legs had gone numb. Feeling that numbness, a certainty rose in me for the first time. I had to find him today.

*All night I'd gone back over what Trope had said. "The reason isn't gone." She'd said the city's living hadn't changed her own share of the weight. When I heard it, I hadn't understood. Only that night did I understand. I had been saying the same thing. Even with the city alive, the reason our home had come apart hadn't changed. My brother still hadn't come back. My mother was still far away. My father was still alone. Trope's sentence and mine were the same shape. Only she had gone quiet before that sentence, and I hadn't been able to.*

---

Finding him wasn't hard.

In this city he was always somewhere quiet. Not a crowded place, not a truly remote one either — somewhere between. I'd seen him a few times before. The one who never got angry beside someone being unfairly cornered. The one who knew a person's weight before the Scales did. The one who never got angry even when I provoked him on purpose.

I found him at the dry well near the gate. He was sitting there alone. The commotion of the day before had already moved entirely toward the city center; here it was quiet.

He looked up and saw me.

"…Pheugon."

"…That's right." I said. "You knew my name."

"I knew it a while ago."

I stood in front of him. I didn't sit.

---

"I have a question." I said.

"Ask it."

"Why don't you get angry?"

*I'd asked this question once before. That time I'd asked it to provoke him. Back then it hadn't mattered whether he answered or not. Today was different. I wanted the real answer.*

He didn't answer. Instead he watched me quietly. There was no defensiveness in his eyes at all. That made me angrier.

"I'm angry." I said. My voice came out lower than I expected, even to myself. "And you're not. Why not?"

"…What is it that's making you angry." he asked.

That question became the fuse.

---

"This city." I said. "This city living. I knew it. I already knew. I knew this would happen. That's why I ran."

My voice began to rise.

"I've known ever since the night I first read that clause at the bottom of the sentence. That you — no, that this world, this clause — would end up exactly here. That mercy wins, in the end, even in the face of an exact verdict. I wasn't afraid of that. I hated it. That's why I ran."

He didn't move.

"If I hadn't run." I said. "This city would have simply fallen. No one would ever have spoken those six words. I wanted it to end that way. I ran wanting this city to die. Not out of fear. That was my calculation."

*In that moment I surprised even myself. I had already admitted this in Chapter 27. I'd said it once before, to the Adjudicator. But this time was different. This time I was saying it out loud, in anger. Admitting it and admitting it in anger are different things.*

---

"Our home." I said. My throat closed. "Our home never had a clause like this."

He still said nothing.

"There was a person from the Reckoning living in our house. For months. I still don't fully know what that person did, or how. But afterward, someone's weight in our home went up, and the Scales calculated it exactly. Exactly. I don't doubt that precision. I still don't."

I caught my breath.

"But we never had forty days. There was no vine. No one to confirm any turning. My brother just left. My mother just went away. My father is still alone. No one ever stood in front of our home and said, 'This family has turned.'"

*Saying that sentence, I realized how long I'd been holding it in. Three years. In three years I had never once spoken that sentence aloud. I had always been someone who read other people's sentences aloud. My own, I had never once read.*

"But this city." I said. "This city is a hundred twenty thousand people. A hundred twenty thousand people cheated the Scales, stole, neglected, were cruel. And yet, like a vine growing, all of them lived at once. Because I said six words. Is that fair? Is that really fair?"

"Trope's mother." I said. My voice couldn't stop now. "She was pushed out five years ago too. That was small lies, piled up too. Not one great sin — small things, piled up because life was hard. The Scales measured her exactly, and she was pushed out. Why didn't she have a clause? Why did no one ever say in front of her, 'This person has turned'? Trope still goes to the well looking for her mother's handprint. And her mother never comes back."

*In that moment I knew I was using Trope's grief as evidence for my own anger. That wasn't right. But I couldn't stop, not then. I needed more grounds to say this was unfair. Trope's mother was that grounds.*

---

He didn't answer.

I couldn't bear that silence.

"Say something." I said. "Aren't you angry? Even knowing this isn't fair?"

"…I'm not angry." he said quietly.

"Why not!"

*I know I shouted that. It was fortunate no one was near the gate. If there had been, I would have shouted at him exactly the same way in front of them.*

"Pheugon." he said. His voice stayed low. A lowness without any anger in it. "What you're shouting at me right now — that isn't aimed at the Scales."

I stopped at that.

*Until that moment I hadn't known clearly, even myself, who I was shouting at. His words picked it out. I wasn't angry at the Scales. The Scales hadn't saved this city. The Scales had only updated a number. What had saved it was the clause, and behind that clause stood someone. And that someone, from the very start, was the person sitting in front of me.*

"…That's right." I said. My voice shook. "I'm angry at you. The Scales was just accurate. You — you people — made it this way. So you can only give mercy once you know the whole sin. So you hand the key to that door to the one person who has the most reason to hate it. What kind of design is that? I never got to choose anything inside that design. I just stood where you people built the place to stand."

---

He looked at me for a long time.

There was no surprise in his eyes, no defensiveness, no irritation. I couldn't bear that. I think I wanted him to be angry with me. If he had been, it would have proven my anger was justified.

"…I" he said. "I know why you're angry."

"If you know, why don't you say anything?"

"What should I say." he asked back. It wasn't a scolding tone. It was a genuine question.

I couldn't answer.

*In that moment I realized, for the first time, that I didn't know what I wanted from this conversation. Did I want him to apologize? To explain himself? Or to get as angry as I was? I didn't clearly want any of those. I just wanted to see something else shake, the way I was shaking.*

"…You never get angry." I said. My voice grew smaller this time. "Not once, in this whole world. I find that frightening. Because I know not getting angry isn't weakness. I know not getting angry is far more powerful."

He gave no answer to that.

I caught my breath in that silence. My body was shaking. It was the trembling that comes after you've shouted.

"Am I wrong?" I asked. It came out almost a plea. "Is it wrong for me to be angry?"

"…I never said that." he said.

"Then why won't you say anything."

"…What you want to hear right now isn't an answer." he said quietly. "What you want is for me to be angry along with you."

I started to argue with that, and couldn't. Because he was right.

*In that moment I saw the true shape of what I'd wanted. I hadn't wanted to be justified. I already had that in hand. I wanted agreement. I wanted someone to raise a fist beside me at this world's unfairness. And in this whole world, the one person who had every right to do it — the only one — never once raised his fist.*

I sank down in front of him, as if my legs had given out.

"…I remember that night." I said. This time I wasn't shouting. I just spoke. "The night my father raised his hand at the Scales' terminal. He looked at the number, and didn't show it to me. He only said, 'It's fine.' I knew, even then, that he wasn't saying that to me. He was saying it to himself."

He was watching me.

"After that, I kept my own number low. As low as I could, always. I thought that if I did, it would somehow fill in for my father's share. It was a child's arithmetic. The Scales doesn't count that way. I know that. And still I lived that way for three years. Not getting angry, not doing wrong, being perfect."

"…That must have been hard." he said.

There was no pity in it. It was just a voice acknowledging a fact. That made it hurt more, not less.

"Whether it was hard doesn't matter." I said. "I thought if I could endure the hardship, something would come back. Nothing came back. Not my brother, not my mother, not a normal father. But this city — the moment I said six words, everything came back to it. A hundred twenty thousand of them."

---

We stayed like that a long while.

The wind passed over the dry well. Far off, from the heart of the city, people's voices still carried faintly.

"…I can't go back to the city." I said. It was a sentence I hadn't expected myself. "Not right now."

"…Where will you go."

I looked east. Beyond the wall, a low hill was visible. Somewhere no one much went.

"…I don't know." I said. "I just don't want to be here."

He didn't stand. He didn't try to stop me either.

"…Go, then." he said.

There was neither permission nor refusal in it. Just those words.

*I turned those words over for a long time that day. I think I half-wanted him to stop me. If he had, I would have gotten angry at that too. Because he didn't stop me, I lost the next thing I could have been angry at. He had, in effect, taken away one more target for my anger.*

*Even now I don't regret what I poured out at him that day. Every word of it was true. But I also learned, that day, that after I'd poured it all out, nothing became lighter. Anger doesn't disappear by being spoken. It only moves somewhere else.*

I turned and left him there.

Passing through the gate, I met the substitute herald. He was sitting alone in the shade at the foot of the wall. Same clothes as yesterday, same posture. He stood when he saw me.

"…Where are you going?" he asked.

"…I don't know." I said. "I just don't want to be here."

He looked at me a moment, then nodded. He didn't ask anything more.

"…Congratulations." he said. His voice was even. "I think you did the right thing."

*That wasn't comfort to me, not then. It made things heavier instead. He saw me as someone in the right. In that moment I wanted to tell him exactly who I'd just been shouting at, and what. I didn't.*

I only nodded and passed him by.

A few steps on, I looked back. He was still sitting there. Not an angry face, not a sad one. Just watching me.

*I didn't know, that day, what that face meant. Now I do. It was a waiting face. The face of someone who knew that after I'd finished shouting it all out, I'd come back eventually.*

I turned my back on that face and started walking east.

Following the wall, the sun climbed higher. Inside the city, yesterday's joy was still going on. The bell rang once more. I heard it behind me as I walked.

*I didn't know exactly where I was going, then. I only knew I wanted to get some distance — from this city, from that sound. I didn't admit, at the time, that this was running away. I admit it now. I was running away again. Not from the city, this time — from the feeling the city had given me.*
