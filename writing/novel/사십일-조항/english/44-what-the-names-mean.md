# Chapter 44. What the Names Mean

> _Previously_ — On the hill, overnight, a vine grew up around the spot where Pheugon had slept and made shade. No one seemed to have planted it. Beneath that shade, Pheugon slept comfortably for the first time.

On the afternoon of the third day, a human figure appeared again on the path through the fields.

This time it didn't vanish. The figure grew larger and larger, and soon I recognized who it was. A walk with a water jug slung over one shoulder. Trope.

I didn't stand. I just watched her come, still sitting where I was.

*I felt something strange in that moment. Gladness and shame at once. I wanted to see her again. And at the same time, I didn't want her to see whatever face I was wearing right now. Over these forty days I had felt those two things together many times. Each time, I'd thought it strange. Now I know it's simply how a person's heart works.*

Trope stopped for a moment where the field path ended and looked up at the hill. That must have been the first time she saw the vine too. Even from a distance, that green arch would have caught the eye. Watching her stop, I thought — she must be carrying the same question I was.

---

She only caught her breath after she'd finished climbing the whole hill. Sweat beaded on her forehead. She set down the water jug and looked at the vine. She looked at it for a long time.

"…What is this?" she asked.

"…I don't know." I said. "It was like this yesterday morning."

Trope stepped under the vine and touched a leaf with her hand. The same motion I'd made.

"…Who planted it?"

"…No one. No one's come up here."

She didn't press further. Instead she held out the water jug.

"…The Adjudicator was worried. You hadn't come down in two days."

I took the jug and drank. Water sliding down my throat had never felt so welcome.

---

We sat side by side beneath the vine's shade.

Neither of us spoke for a while. The city looked small down below. The wind stirred the leaves now and then.

"…The Adjudicator asked again." Trope said. "Whether you'll come back."

"…What did you say?"

"I said I didn't know." she said. "It wasn't a lie. I really didn't know."

I drew up my knees and hugged them.

"…I'm still angry." I said. I said it aloud for the first time.

"…I know." Trope said. "I can tell from your face."

"…I'm sorry." I said. "It's not you I should be angry at."

"…You've never been angry at me." she said. "I'm just the person who brought the water jug."

I laughed at that, a little. Not a real laugh. But something close to one.

*I was grateful, in that moment, that she didn't push me. The Adjudicator hadn't either. He hadn't either. In this city, strangely, no one had tried to pull me back until I walked out on my own. I wondered whether that was trained restraint, or something this city had learned in these forty days.*

---

"…The Adjudicator told me to bring you this too."

Trope pulled a sheet of paper from inside her coat. Old, worn paper, its edges a little frayed.

"…What is it?"

"A list of herald names, he said." she said. "A very old one. It turned up from the very back of the proclamation office's storeroom. He said heralds used to be given names for a reason, long ago. Names drawn from an old language, all of them meaningful. No one speaks that language anymore, so no one knows the meanings now. But this list has the meanings written beside them."

I took the paper.

*In that moment I didn't know what I was holding. Now I do. I was holding, in my hand, the answer to a question I had never once wondered about in three years. That I'd never once wondered — that itself was strange. Why had I never once wondered what my own name meant?*

*Thinking about it now, the answer is simple. I had only ever handled names as sound. In the course of reading one hundred twelve cases, I read hundreds of names. Never once did I wonder what any of them meant. A name was a signpost pointing to a person — not a sentence saying something about that person. At least, that's what I'd been taught. But now, this paper in my hand was telling me that was wrong.*

I thought of the training grounds. The day Had had stopped three times at his own name. "Why don't you stop?" he'd asked. I suddenly wondered — what had Had's name meant? Why had he stopped at his own name? Maybe he too had never known what his name meant, and his body had simply reacted before his mind did.

*I thought of looking for Had's name on that list, and stopped myself. That wasn't mine to do today. Today was my turn.*

---

Trope flipped through the paper.

"…The Adjudicator said he found this yesterday. He was looking for something else — he found it by accident. But the moment he found it, he told me to bring it straight up to you. He didn't say why. I didn't ask."

*I could guess, then, why the Adjudicator had done that. He was a man who had waited forty years for this clause. Now that it had finally been invoked, he probably knew exactly what I'd be doing on this hill. He hadn't sent me comfort. He'd sent me this. I thought that was very like him.*

Trope ran her finger down the list, searching for the name.

"…Pheugon." she said.

I held my breath.

---

"…φεύγω." she read the old letters, awkwardly. "It says the meaning here. 'To flee.'"

I heard those words.

For a while I couldn't react at all.

*I've gone back over this moment hundreds of times since. Each time I remember it a little differently. At first I remembered it as shock. Later, as relief. Sorting it out now, it was both.*

"…Pheugon." I said my own name aloud. For the first time in my life, I heard it as something strange.

"To flee."

And I laughed.

---

I laughed out loud. Not loudly. But it was unmistakably laughter.

Trope looked at me, startled.

"…Why are you laughing?"

I answered without being able to stop.

"…I've wondered my whole life why I turned out this way. Why I always choose to run, every single time. When the person from the Reckoning wrecked our home, instead of getting angry, I ran into endurance. When I got the Reckoning's sentence, I actually ran. Just now, I didn't go down into the city — I ran up to this hill instead."

*In that moment, for the first time, I could see my whole life as one sentence. I'd had that name since I was born. Whether my parents knew its meaning when they chose it, or just liked the sound, I don't know. But I had lived exactly according to that name. Without a single deviation.*

"…My name already knew." I said. "It knew from the start that I'd run."

Trope said nothing, just watched me.

"…Aren't you angry?" she asked, carefully. "That it was decided from birth like that?"

I thought about the question.

"…Strangely, no." I said. "I feel like I should be angry, but I'm not. It's almost lighter." I looked at the light coming through the leaves. "All this time I lived without understanding why I'm this way. Carrying something you don't understand and carrying something you understand — the weight is different. The thing itself is the same, but it feels different to hold."

Trope nodded. Not a face that fully understood. But a face trying to.

"…Did the Adjudicator know?" she said. "This meaning."

"…He probably didn't until yesterday." I said. "That's probably why he found it yesterday."

*In that moment I imagined the moment the Adjudicator had found this paper and read my name first. A man who'd stood beside this clause for forty years, learning at last that the boy who had finally invoked it was named 'to flee.' What expression had crossed his face? I felt I might never get to ask. And yet, somehow, I felt I already knew.*

---

As my laughter faded, I noticed I had a little water in my eyes.

*I couldn't tell whether it was tears of sorrow or tears of laughter. Now I know. Both. For three years I had lived without understanding why I was always a person who ran. Living without that understanding is heavy. A name doesn't remove that weight. But the weight got a name. Once something has a name, at least you know what it is you're carrying.*

"…It's strange." Trope said quietly. "I've never seen you laugh before."

"…Neither have I." I said. "Not in this book. Not in my whole life."

---

Trope looked back down at the paper. Her finger moved slowly down the list. She was clearly looking for her own name.

I didn't stop her.

Her finger stopped.

"…Trope." she read, very quietly. "τροπή."

She didn't read the rest. The paper in her hand trembled slightly.

"…What does it say?" I asked.

She didn't answer.

I looked down at the paper. I read it in her place.

"'Turning.'"

---

Trope said nothing at all.

I looked at her. She stayed exactly as she was, paper in hand. Her expression didn't move. She didn't seem to blink.

*I felt, then, like I should say something. But nothing came to mind. Some silences shouldn't be disturbed. I learned that for the first time that day.*

Time passed. The wind stirred the leaves. A bell sounded faintly from the city. Trope still hadn't moved.

*Even now I can't say precisely how long that silence lasted. It felt very long to me. She had lived five years not knowing what her own name meant. In those five years, without knowing what her own turning meant, she had checked her weight at the Scales, every single day. And just now, she had learned that her name had been pointing that direction from the very start.*

I looked at the side of her face. There were no tears. But the absence of tears looked heavier than tears would have. Some feelings don't come out as tears or laughter. They just stay pooled inside the body.

*I set my own laughter and her silence side by side, in my mind, in that moment. Both of us had just had the same weight of feeling suddenly given a name. I let mine out as laughter. She couldn't let hers out at all. I know it wasn't because I was somehow better than her. My name had only confirmed a self I already knew. Her name pointed to a self she had never once let herself be. Confirming is easy. Letting yourself is hard.*

---

A long while later, she finally spoke.

"…Did Mom know?" she said. Her voice was very small. "When she named me. This meaning."

"…I don't know."

"…Does Mom think she's turned, right now?" she said. It sounded like a question to herself. "Or does she think I'm the one turning, right now?"

I couldn't answer that. It wasn't a question that could be answered.

*I didn't fully understand, that day, why Trope stayed silent so long. I understand a little more now. I heard my own name and laughed. My name only confirmed a self I already knew. But Trope's name pointed to a self she had never known at all. She had lived believing she was the one pushed aside, the one left behind. And her name had been calling her, from the very start, the one who turns.*

---

The sun began to sink toward the west. The shadows lengthened.

Trope carefully folded the paper again. Instead of putting it back inside her coat, she pressed it into my hand.

"…You keep it." she said.

"…Why?"

"…You're the one who reads things." she said. Then, very briefly, faintly, she smiled. It wasn't a full smile. "I don't have the courage to read this one aloud myself yet."

I took the paper.

*Taking it, I understood that the two of us had gone through completely different things that same afternoon. I laughed, and she went silent. I understood, and she was left more confused. And yet both of those responses had come from the same root. We had both, that day, for the first time, looked again at a self we had each thought we already knew.*

Trope went down the hill before the sun had fully set. I watched her back until it disappeared among the field paths.

Partway down, she stopped once. She didn't turn around. She just stood there a moment, then walked on again. I didn't ask what that pause meant. She probably couldn't have explained it either.

*That evening, sitting alone beneath the vine, I read the two names on the paper again. To flee. Turning. I found it strange that those two words sat side by side. Someone who fled and someone who turns had passed through the same forty days, in the same city. And in the end, it was six words from the mouth of the one who fled that had saved the city. I still didn't know how to make sense of that.*

*I don't know who gave us those names. Neither does Trope. Neither did the Adjudicator. What was in the hearts of the people who once spoke that old language, when they named two newborns this way — I may never know, not even in the years ahead. But that night, I came away with one certainty: that these names were not a coincidence. There was no ground at all for that certainty. And still, the certainty stayed.*
