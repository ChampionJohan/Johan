# Chapter 10. The Second Day

> _Previously_ — On the first day trapped, the water rose to Pheugon's
> knees. He held out by rationing bread and water, counting steps in place
> of time he had no way to measure. In that calculation, he found a strange
> comfort in treating his own dwindling time and the Reckoning's dwindling
> days as the same thing — all while, for no reason he understood, saving
> his own voice.

I scratched a second line onto the wall.

The water seemed to have risen a little higher than yesterday. I couldn't be certain. The bread was down to three and a half loaves. The flask was a third full. I confirmed those numbers and leaned back against the wall.

On the second day, I made a sound for the first time.

To be precise, I spoke. Not a groan, not a cough — a sentence. With no one there.

*This is the chapter hardest for me to write. To explain that moment exactly, I have to start by saying why I began with that particular sentence, of all sentences.*

*I've rewritten this chapter more times than any other in this book. What I wrote first was far shorter. I didn't want to write this night in detail, at the time. Not out of shame. It was hard to hear that voice again. But I came to understand that without this part, nothing else in this book makes sense. So I wrote it again.*

---

I was hungry. Hungrier than yesterday. My body wanted something bread couldn't fill.

I sat with my back to the wall and opened my mouth. I didn't know why I opened it, at the time. I know now. I wanted to hear a sound. Not water. A human voice.

*The only voice I could make, in this world, was one. My own. I realized that, then, for the first time.*

Sitting against the wall, I opened my mouth. No sound came. My throat had been shut too long. I cleared it a few times. The sound rang strange. Like someone else's cough.

*At the training hall, the supervisor taught us to treat the voice as part of the body. That it stiffens if it isn't used every day. I'd forgotten that. Barely a day and a half without using it, and my throat had already become a stranger to me.*

I drank a little water. More than I'd rationed. My throat needed loosening. In that moment, that mattered more than saving the flask.

*It was the first time I broke a rule I'd set for myself. A small thing, in itself. And yet I remember breaking it, still, clearly. The one rule I'd believed I could keep, I broke myself, just to make a sound. The wish to speak was greater than the wish to keep the rule.*

The first thing to come from my mouth was the sentence.

---

"…The Reckoning."

I said those two words and stopped. My throat was raw. Unused to making sound.

I swallowed and began again.

"…Given that the accumulated weight of this city's acts has been confirmed to exceed the limit."

A trained sentence. The way of speaking bred into my body from a hundred and twelve readings. I hadn't even known I had it memorized. It came out of my mouth on its own.

*I've wondered a long time why that was the sentence I started with, of all sentences. The answer was simple. It was the one sentence I owned completely, word for word. A person with nothing else reaches for the most familiar thing first. The most familiar thing I had was a sentence document.*

---

I kept reading. The entire Reckoning sentence. I made several mistakes. Reversed the order, corrected myself, repeated one line twice.

*If this had been the training hall, the supervisor would have made me start again. Here, no one made me start again. I kept going, wrong as I was. The first time. Reading on, uncorrected.*

City name, accumulated weight, basis of calculation, grace period, method of execution. I read those sentences again, in the dark, once more. I said the numbers too, as best I remembered. A hundred and twenty thousand people. Accumulated weight past the limit. Saying those numbers, I felt, for one brief moment, that they weren't numbers but people. It faded again quickly.

This time it carried no effect. The rule that reading it aloud gives it effect — that was only true in its set place, its set procedure. Here, however loudly I read, it was only sound.

*I found that fact, strangely, comforting, in that moment. Whatever I did here, nothing would happen to the Reckoning. I could play with that sentence freely, with an easy mind.*

---

The last line came.

"…Article 4, Clause 11."

I said that line slowly, letter by letter.

"…Should this city, within forty days, be confirmed to have turned, this sentence shall be void."

Having said it, I went quiet a long while. Only the water's sound filled that silence.

*That was the first time I'd ever spoken that sentence aloud. Reading it on paper and speaking it with my mouth were different. Paper, your eyes can pass over. Your mouth carries the sentence through your body. I felt, the moment that sentence crossed my throat, for the first time, exactly how heavy it was.*

---

I said the sentence again. And again.

The first time, it was only a sentence. The second time, something caught in my throat. By the third, it had begun to change.

"…Within forty days… if I turned."

I didn't know what I was saying. My mouth moved before I did.

"…I won't turn."

*I remember exactly the moment I said that sentence. In the dark, my legs sunk in water, listening to my own voice come back to me. I didn't even know, then, who I was speaking to. The Scales? That city? Or just the wall?*

After a brief silence, I spoke again.

"…I won't turn. I have nothing to turn from."

Having said that, I laughed. No sound, just my throat moving.

*I couldn't explain, at the time, why I'd laughed. I can explain it a little, now. In that moment I realized, for the first time, that I wasn't speaking toward the Scales, but toward the clause. That clause had been made to confirm whether a city had turned. I wasn't a city. And yet I kept answering as if the clause were asking me the question too. That I wouldn't turn. Why I answered that way, I still don't fully know, even now.*

---

"…I was right."

I said that. For the first time, clearly.

"…I have the right to hate that city. My brother left. My mother went. My father lives alone. That was the accurate weight. The Scales measured it that way."

My voice grew louder. So as not to be drowned out by the water.

"…So why does only that city get a door. A door that never came for my house."

I stopped a moment after that, then went on.

"…My father's weight was accurate. The Scales measured it precisely. No one shaved any of it off. I was taught that's how it should be. But the Reckoning. That city has had its door standing open from the very start. Whatever a hundred and twenty thousand people did, if they only turn within forty days, all of it just — never happened."

My voice cracked. I kept going.

"…Is that fair. Is that what the Scales does?"

Only the water answered.

*I didn't cry, saying that. Strange, thinking about it now. It was a thing I'd held back so long, and still, in the dark, I held back the tears. Even with no one watching. I know where I learned that habit. The night my brother left home, I taught myself, that night, for the first time, how not to cry aloud. What I learned that night had followed me all the way into this dark.*

---

Once the words broke loose, they didn't stop.

I talked about herald training. About Had. About the merchant. A hundred and twelve cases, the erased words. I said all of it to the water, to the wall, to the dark.

I drew my knees up and rested my chin on them. My voice grew a little softer.

"…The night my brother left, he turned back once, at the door." I said. "I couldn't tell if he was looking at me or just checking his direction. I didn't say anything, then. I still don't know, even now, what I should have said to him that night."

I paused a moment, then went on.

"…My mother cried in the kitchen twice. The second time was worse. I never knocked on the door."

*Copying these sentences out again, my hand slows down. I realize, all over again, that I had never told anyone these things before that night. Most of what I set down in Chapter 5 was, in truth, first spoken aloud in this dark. I only wrote it onto paper later. This waterway is where it first became words.*

I talked about my father too.

"…My father said 'it's fine,' once, at the terminal of the Scales. He wasn't saying it to me. I knew that, at the time. I didn't say anything."

The more I spoke, the stranger it felt, how much easier my voice became. Strange, that easing came with pain. I doubted that ease even as I kept speaking. Even while I doubted it, my mouth didn't stop.

*I know the reason now. Those words had been pooling inside my body for three years. Water that pools goes stagnant. That night, for the first time, I let it flow out. Not to the right person, not in the right place. I only let it flow to a place I could let it flow. Even so, it was better than letting it stagnate.*

"…I've done nothing wrong."

I said that.

"…I don't lie, I don't touch other people's things, I don't get angry. My weight is the lowest there is."

Having said it, I went quiet.

The water's sound rose loud again. The moment my voice stopped, the water filled that space back in. I found that strange. The world flowed on the same, whether I spoke or not. And still I wanted to go on speaking.

*Right after I said that sentence, I felt a strange unease. I know now what it was. In that moment I remembered, for the first time, the lie I'd told. "No, sir," when the supervisor asked whether I had some connection to the Reckoning. The lie to the gatekeeper. I knew the Scales had calculated both those lies. Precisely. I could no longer be certain, any longer, that my weight really was the lowest.*

I spoke again, trying to shake off that unease.

"…And still, I'm right."

Once more.

"…I'm right."

Trying to say it a third time, no voice came. Not because my throat had given out. I felt, in that moment, that I myself no longer believed the words. The third sentence simply scattered, inside my mouth.

*I still haven't finished that scattered sentence. What was meant to come after "I'm right" — I went on searching for it for a long time after. I didn't find it that night. I couldn't say, even now, that I've found it completely.*

*I found a different sentence instead. Much later, after I'd come to this city. I won't write what that sentence was, here. I haven't reached that place yet.*

---

I kept talking, on into that night, without stopping.

The sentences grew shorter. Eventually only words were left. "…Heavy." "…Turn." "…Why."

*I remember every one of those words, even now. Everything that came out that night was something I was saying for the first time. Words I'd never said to anyone. I pulled them out, for the first time, into a dark with no one there. That it was a strange kind of first confession, I didn't know, at the time.*

I kept talking until my throat gave out entirely and no more sound would come. After that, only my mouth moved. Without sound.

The water kept on the same as before. Nothing answered.

I asked, a long while after. My voice was barely more than a whisper.

"…Are you listening?"

Of course no one answered. And yet, asking that, I couldn't shake the strange feeling that I was asking it toward someone. Not the Scales. The Scales wasn't something you asked that of. So who, then? I didn't know, at the time. I still don't know exactly, even now.

*I would go on to ask that question again, in other places throughout this book. Writing this chapter, I realize freshly that it was the first question that ever rose out of this dark. A question with no answer. But that it had no answer doesn't mean it was asked for nothing — I would only learn that much later.*

My body gave out and I leaned against the wall and closed my eyes. My voice used up entirely, my body felt strangely light. As if something had been emptied out of it. I was still hungry. But that hunger was a little more bearable than before.

*And that night, for the first time, I felt the strange sense that I wasn't entirely alone. Not that someone was there. I mean that, for the first time, I heard my own words. In three years. Until then I had only avoided, erased, and swallowed my own words. That night, for the first time, I let them out, into the open, and heard them come back to me from outside. That is different from someone hearing them for you. And still, it was not nothing at all.*

*Even now, thinking of that night, I can't tell whether it was prayer or just rambling. Maybe there's no need to tell them apart. I had only, for the first time in three years, used my own voice as my own. Not a sentence I'd been told to read — a sentence I had made myself.*
