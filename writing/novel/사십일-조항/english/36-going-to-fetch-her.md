# Chapter 36. Going to Fetch Her

> _Previously_ — Trope left a note behind and walked out the gate at
> dawn, believing her own weight made the city heavier. Pheugon held
> that letter in his hand and, with a single day left, set out
> through the gate after her.

Walking out through the gate, I didn't fully know what I was doing.

*I had spent three years trying to hate this city. And now I was
walking out its gate to find one of its children. I hadn't known,
until that moment, that both those sentences could come from the same
mouth.*

*As I ran, I kept turning that strange fact over. If someone had told
me, forty days ago, that I would one day risk my life running to save
a child of the Reckoning, I'd have laughed in his face. And yet here
I was, running. No one had told me to. Not the Scales, not the
Adjudicator. I just ran.*

The plain was vast. There was only one road. A dirt track running
straight west. I half ran, half walked along it.

The sun stood high. Dust rose beneath my feet. Every time my breath
grew ragged, I stopped and looked around. Not a single figure in
sight.

*As I ran, I kept saying her name inside myself. Trope. Trope. I
didn't know what the name meant. That was the rule of this world. No
one ever explains what a name means. I only knew that the more I said
it, the heavier that name grew inside me.*

Some way along, I realized this was the second time I'd walked this
road. Forty days before, I had walked it from the opposite direction,
toward this city. Back then I hadn't thought of it as walking — I'd
thought of it as being swept along. Now I was clearly walking. On my
own feet.

---

About an hour in, I saw a low pile of stones by the roadside.

At first I took it for just a heap of stones. Coming closer, I saw
names carved into them. Small letters, one at a time. Some looked
old; some looked fairly recent.

I guessed what it was. The names of people driven out of the city.
Who had built it, I didn't know. Family, maybe. Or the people
themselves, maybe.

I searched among the stones for one name. I didn't know Trope's
mother's name. I couldn't find it.

*In that moment I thought I understood why Trope had taken this road.
She hadn't fled. She was walking the exact road her mother had
walked. Like someone trying to line up the order right.*

I knelt for a moment in front of that pile of stones. I didn't know
why. It just felt like something I should do.

*Looking at those stones, I thought of my own family. Our family had
never built a pile of stones like this. My brother had simply
vanished; my mother had simply left. No one had carved a name into
anything for either of them. For the first time, I couldn't say which
was better — carving a name, or simply forgetting. This city carved
the names of those it had driven out. My own family, we had simply
erased each other.*

I stood up and brushed the dirt from my knees.

I ran again.

---

I ran, calling her name. No answer came. The plain was too wide, and
my voice too small.

The sun sank further. More than once I wondered if I'd taken a wrong
turn. Even with only one road, I kept glancing back to make sure I
was going the right way.

*In that moment, for the first time, I felt what a world without the
Scales was like. Inside the city, anything could be confirmed with a
number. Weight, date, location. Outside the walls, none of that
existed. I could only walk, only call out, only hope.*

I found her as the sun began to lower.

Far off, a little off the road, on a low hill, a single figure sat.
Not a water jar this time — just a bundle beside her, knees drawn up.

I called her name.

She turned. She wasn't startled. As if she'd known I would come.

"…Go back," I said. My breath was ragged.

"…Don't follow me," she said. Her voice had no strength in it. "This
is right. For me."

"…What's right about it."

"If I'm not there," she said, half burying her face in her knees, "at
least one thing becomes certain. If the city doesn't live, it won't
be my fault. If it lives, that won't be because of me either. I just
want out of this equation."

"…The Scales' number won't change whether you're there or not."

"I know," she said. "In my head. But Pheugon, I can't shake this
feeling that something would be different, just because I wasn't
there. I know it doesn't make sense. I know that."

I had no answer for that.

I sat down beside her. Neither of us said anything.

*I tried, in that moment, to find something to persuade her with. The
Scales doesn't measure by family, the Adjudicator told me so, your
leaving won't change the city's weight at all. All true things. But
none of it came out of my mouth. I already knew those words would do
her no good. Trope hadn't come this far by numbers.*

The sun sank further. The wind was cold.

"…You're cold," I finally said.

"…Yes," she said.

That was all. I didn't try to persuade her further. She didn't push
me away further either.

We sat like that for a long while. Wind, and somewhere far off, a
bird crying. Nothing else.

"…Are you scared?" I asked. A long while had passed.

"…Of what."

"Being out here alone."

She didn't answer, just hugged her knees tighter.

"…I was scared," she said, very quietly. "But strangely, when I saw
you running toward me over there, do you know what the first thing I
thought was?"

"…What."

"'It's over now,' she said. 'I thought, I don't have to be scared
anymore. That's what scared me most. That I could be reassured that
easily."

I had no answer for that either.

After a long while, I held out my hand.

She looked at it a long time. Didn't take it. Then, very slowly, laid
her own hand on top of it.

---

We walked toward the gate together after that.

It was a long road. Until the sun sank completely below the horizon,
neither of us said a word. She didn't pull her hand away. I didn't
speak again either.

*In that silence I kept asking myself, over and over, why I was
there. I had spent three years trying to hate this city. And now I
was walking, hand in hand, leading one of its children back — a
child who had tried, of her own will, to leave it. I never found the
answer. I still don't fully have it, even now.*

Walking, I suddenly thought of the man who never gets angry. What
would he say, if he could see me now. I imagined it for a moment,
then stopped. He wasn't here now. In his absence, I felt like I was,
for the first time, alone doing something he might have done. That
struck me as strange.

*I wondered, in that moment, for the first time, whether he had ever
once walked out like this to fetch someone. I didn't know the
answer. I didn't know, then, that I would come to ask him one day.*

Far, far ahead, a small light appeared.

At first I took it for a star. Then the light grew. Not just one —
many. In the dark, they gathered one by one into a single mass.

The Reckoning.

*I stopped walking in that moment. For two months I had lived only
inside that city. Seen from within, that light had just been the
lamps that came on each evening. One at a time, familiar. Seen from
outside, it was different. From this distance, all those lights
became one. Not scattered people — one city.*

*I looked at that light as if seeing it for the first time. It really
was the first time. I had lived two months in that city and had never
once seen it from outside.*

*From inside, each light had been its own. The bakery's light, the
Scales office's light, someone's window, lit. I had counted those
lights one by one, living among them. From outside, none of that was
visible. All I could see was one thing. The whole city gathered into
a single light, floating in the dark.*

*A strange thought came to me in that moment. Did the Scales see the
city this way too. Not as individual people, but as one mass. But I
soon knew that thought was wrong. The Scales has never seen it from
this distance. The Scales only sees from inside, only one by one.
Seeing a city as one whole, from this distance — only a person could
do that.*

Trope stopped walking too and looked at the light.

"…It's pretty," she said, very quietly.

I had no answer to that. I'd never once thought of that city as
pretty. And yet, in that moment, I was agreeing with her for the
first time.

We started walking again. The light grew larger.

---

It was already the middle of the night when we reached the gate.

The gatekeeper came to meet us with a lantern. He asked nothing. He
just opened the gate for us.

As we stepped back through the gate, Trope spoke for the first time.

"…Why did you come to fetch me."

I hadn't prepared an answer.

"…I don't know."

"You came without knowing?"

"…I felt like I had to go fetch you. So I went."

She neither smiled nor cried at that. She just lowered her head a
little. Then walked on again without another word. I walked beside
her, and that was the whole of what we did that night.

*I was afraid of that silence. I couldn't tell if it was angry
silence or simply exhausted silence. I know now. It was both. And
there was a third thing in it too. Shame. She had, that day, been
caught — for the first time — wanting to live.*

We walked without speaking all the way to her door. She stopped in
front of it.

"…Go in," I said. "Get some rest."

She nodded. Before opening the door, she turned to look at me once.

"…It's tomorrow," she said.

I knew what that meant. The fortieth day. The square. Two heralds.

"…Yeah," I said.

"…Pheugon." She called again. "I learned something to be ashamed of
today."

"…What."

"I thought choosing to die was the brave thing," she said. "But when
you came all that way to that hill, I was happy. Because I wanted to
live. I was ashamed of that."

I had no idea what to say to that.

"…Nothing to be ashamed of," I finally managed.

"I don't know," she said. "Not yet, anyway."

She said no more and went inside. The door closed quietly.

---

I didn't go back to the shed that night. I just sat on the steps
outside her door.

*I still couldn't explain, that night, why I had gone to fetch her. I
still hadn't decided whether to save this city or not. And yet I
hadn't hesitated for even a moment about saving one person named
Trope. That those two were the same question — I still hadn't
managed to connect that, even sitting on those steps.*

*I know now. That night, for the first time, I did something without
calculating. For three years, and even after coming to this city, I
had calculated everything. Weight, cases, dates, reasons. But running
out through that gate, I hadn't calculated anything at all. I had
simply run.*

*I didn't know, that night, what that meant. I thought I'd understand
by tomorrow. I didn't fully understand tomorrow, either. Only now,
little by little, do I understand it.*

Far off, the square's board glowed in the dark. I didn't look at the
number written on it. I didn't need to.

Tomorrow it would be zero.

*I sat on those steps, unmoving, deep into the night. My lantern had
gone out long before. In the dark, I looked down at my hand again.
It had trembled that morning. It wasn't trembling now.*

*I didn't know, at the time, what that difference meant. I know now.
This morning's trembling was the trembling of a man whose calculation
wasn't finished. This hand, not trembling now, wasn't because I'd
reached an answer. I still didn't know the answer. Only, that night,
I stopped doubting one thing at least. That I was someone who could
go and fetch another person. For three years I hadn't known whether I
was that kind of person or not.*

As dawn drew near, I rose from where I sat. My body was stiff. I
walked a few steps toward the square. Far off, the board's number was
faintly visible. Still one.

*A few hours from now, two people would stand in that spot. One who
didn't know this city, and one who could no longer say he didn't.
What face I would wear, standing there — I still couldn't picture it.*

I walked back to Trope's door. No sound came from behind it. I sat
back down in front of that door, waiting for the sun to rise.

*I didn't know, then, what it meant to go and fetch someone. I know a
little now. Going to fetch someone means not asking first whether
that person is right or wrong. It just means going. That night, for
the first time, I did that. Whether I could do the same thing again
for someone else, the next day, I still didn't know.*
