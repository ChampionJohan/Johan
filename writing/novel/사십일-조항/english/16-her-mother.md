# Chapter 16. Her Mother

> _Previously_ — In the main square of the Reckoning, Pheugon finally refused to check his own weight. Trope found this strange, and before they parted, quietly said she'd already guessed his name wasn't really Had. Pheugon ended the day believing he hadn't been caught.

The next morning, Trope came late.

I sat in front of the shed and waited a long while. The sun was well up before she appeared. Her expression was a little different than usual.

"…Is something wrong?" I asked.

"No," Trope said. Too quick an answer.

*I didn't press her further, then. I thought I had no right to ask. Looking back, it wasn't a matter of right. I was simply afraid. Not ready to hear someone else's heavy story.*

---

We walked toward the well that day. Where Trope drew water every day. The same well I'd first washed up at.

Filling the water jar, Trope suddenly said.

"I used to play here a lot. When I was little."

"…Alone?"

"With my mother."

I stiffened a little, hearing that word. It was the first time Trope had brought something up on her own.

Trope kept talking, fiddling with the jar's handle.

"My mother taught me how to use a rope, here. You saw how good I am with a rope, right? I learned it from her."

"…And now?"

Trope finished filling the jar and stood up.

"She's not here," Trope said. A flat voice. "She got pushed out."

Trope hoisted the jar onto her shoulder and ran her hand once over the well's coping stone.

"My mother's hand left a mark here, probably," Trope said. "She drew water here every single morning. The stone's a little worn. There, that spot."

I looked where she pointed. The stone really was slightly worn — a mark left by the same hand brushing the same spot for years.

"…Do you remember? Her face."

"Yes," Trope said. "Clearly. It's strange. It's been five years and it hasn't faded at all. I remember her voice too. Waking me up in the morning, calling my name."

Trope paused for a moment.

"But I don't remember much about the last day," Trope said. "Strange, right? The most important day. I came home from school and she was gone. What the weather was like that day, what I ate — none of it, I don't remember any of it."

---

I knew the phrase "pushed out." I knew it as a concept. When accumulated weight exceeds an individual's limit, everything that person uses stops, one by one. Housing, employment, market pass, rations. Without anyone laying a hand on them, that person ends up outside the city.

I only knew it as a principle. I'd never seen who it actually happened to.

"…When?" I asked.

"Five years ago," Trope said. "I was nine."

Trope picked up the jar and started walking. I followed alongside. I didn't know what to say.

"What happened?" I asked, carefully.

Trope stopped walking for a moment, then started again.

"It wasn't anything big," Trope said. "At least, not any single thing. But it added up. After my father left first, my mother raised me alone for years, little by little. Cheating on a price, or lying to get more rations. Nothing big, any of it. But it all added together."

---

"…So it went over the limit?" I asked.

"Yes," Trope said. "One morning her market pass was blocked. The following week her rations stopped. Then the house too."

"…And you?"

"I was a child," Trope said. "None of that attaches to children. I got to stay."

Something felt wrong to me about that. A structure where only the child stayed and only the mother had to leave. But I couldn't put words to exactly what was wrong.

"Where did your mother go?"

"I don't know," Trope said. "All I know is she left the city. No word since."

"…Alone? Leaving behind a nine-year-old?"

"There was nothing to choose," Trope said. Her voice sped up a little. For the first time, a defensive tone. "The house was cut off, the rations were cut off — from that day on, my mother had no way to live in this city. I could stay, so she left me here. She didn't abandon me."

Trope seemed to turn that sentence over in her own mind after saying it.

"…She didn't abandon me," Trope said once more. This time it seemed less spoken to me than to herself.

We walked in silence for a while. The jar was heavy, and Trope's pace slowed a little. I offered to carry it instead; she shook her head.

*I thought, in that moment, how many times Trope must have said that sentence to herself. She didn't abandon me. I understood for the first time then that there are people who have to say a sentence hundreds of times just to get through today.*

---

Near the shed, Trope set the jar down. I found myself curious and asked.

"So who did you live with after that?"

"Just alone, the first few days," Trope said. "Then that grandfather came looking for me. The one at the Scales. He used to be our next-door neighbor. He came and cooked for me, found me a place instead of home. From then until now."

"…He's not family, though."

"No," Trope said. "Does that matter?"

I couldn't answer.

"The Scales doesn't know family," Trope said. "It doesn't weigh who looks after whom, anything like that. But people weigh it. Grandfather just weighed it and came. A nine-year-old left all alone."

*Listening to that, I felt a strange comfort. That someone stepped in, in a spot the Scales couldn't reach, even inside this exact, precise city. I thought, at the time, that this was an exception. I know now. It wasn't an exception — it was a different kind of arithmetic that this city had made for itself, in every spot the Scales could never finally reach.*

Trope set the jar down and sat against the wall. I sat beside her.

"Aren't you angry?" I asked. "At the Scales."

Trope looked at me. Her expression shifted, for the first time, but it wasn't anger.

"Why would I be angry?" Trope asked back.

"…It took your mother."

"The Scales didn't take her," Trope said. "The Scales just did the math. It only calculated what my mother did."

"…Still, though."

Trope looked up at the sky for a moment.

"Because it was right," Trope said.

I had no answer to that.

"What my mother did — it was real," Trope went on. "The lies were real, the cheating was real too. I didn't know when I was little, but it came back to me piece by piece as I grew. I know everything the Scales counted. Every last piece of it. I'd like to say the Scales was wrong. I can't. All of it's true."

---

I felt something strange, standing in front of that.

*I hadn't known, until then, that knowing the Scales is right and enduring the loss of your family to the Scales could exist inside the same feeling. I'd thought the two stood opposed. If it's right, you can bear it; if it's unjust, you get angry. Trope stood somewhere in between. Knowing it was right, and still, every day, that spot stayed empty.*

"…You don't resent her?" I asked again. "Your mother."

"Resent her?" Trope asked back. "I know what she did wrong. But that's different from resentment. My mother did it to feed me. I know that too."

Trope pulled her knees up and hugged them.

"The Scales doesn't count reasons," Trope said. "It doesn't weigh reasons. It only weighs what was done."

---

That stayed with me a long time.

*It was the first time I felt the Scales' precision as something frightening. For three years, I'd thought that precision made me safe. Precise meant nothing to feel wronged about. Sitting next to Trope, I first understood how narrow a thought that had been. Being precise and not feeling wronged were different problems. Trope's mother had been calculated with precision, and that calculation hadn't been wrong. And still Trope was left alone at nine.*

*I thought of my own family's story. Someone from the Reckoning had done something that ruined us. The Scales had calculated that precisely too. The disposition had come out precise too. I'd had no complaint about that disposition for a long time. Because it was precise. But that day, sitting next to Trope, I felt for the first time how thin that sentence had been. Precise, and yet nothing came back. Trope's words laid themselves exactly over my own old sentence.*

"…I'm sorry," I said. I didn't know what I was apologizing for either.

Trope looked at me strangely.

"Why are you sorry, Had?"

I couldn't answer.

*That was the first time I began to see one person of this city not as part of the city I hated, but simply as a person. It wasn't something I'd wanted. I kept wanting to bundle this city together as the number a hundred and twenty thousand, one mass. It was easier to hate that way. Trope kept slipping out of that bundle.*

---

Trope suddenly changed the subject.

"Pheugon," Trope asked. "Was it like this back home? Someone getting pushed out."

I couldn't answer right away.

"…It was," I finally said. "Not often, though."

"Have you seen it? Someone getting pushed out."

I thought of the hundred and twelve cases. The sentence documents I'd read aloud myself. Several of them had ended in the exact same disposition as Trope's mother. Exceeded the limit, market access suspended, then pushed out. I couldn't remember a single one of their faces. I'd erased them. A hundred and eleven more times over.

"…Yes," I said. "I've seen it."

"How did it feel?"

*I nearly told her the truth, right then. I'm the one who reads those sentence documents aloud, I nearly said. The words rose to my throat and I swallowed them. Swallowing made everything inside me heavier. I didn't know, then, where that heaviness came from. I know now. It wasn't the weight of the lie. It was the weight of realizing, in front of Trope, for the first time, that I wasn't a bystander — I was the one who carried it out.*

"…I don't know," was all I said.

Trope didn't press further. She just nodded and stood.

"Let's go. Need to draw water once more before dark."

---

We went back to the well. While Trope lowered the bucket, I sat on the coping stone and looked again at the spot she'd pointed to earlier. The place worn by a hand. A mark that hadn't disappeared in five years.

*Looking at that worn stone, I thought: the Scales can't measure that. That's not weight. It's a mark time left behind, and the person who left it is nowhere in this city anymore. But the stone still testified that she had been there. I thought, for the first time, that things the Scales couldn't measure might be piled up like this all over the city. I didn't hold onto that thought for long. I wasn't ready yet to see what it meant.*

The bucket came up. Trope poured the water into the jar and glanced my way.

"Why do you keep looking at that spot?"

"…It's nothing."

"That's not a nothing expression," Trope said, hoisting the jar to her shoulder. "Fine. I won't ask."

*Every time she said that, I felt something strange. Trope was someone who didn't press. But not pressing, and knowing nothing — those were different things. I mistook the two for the same, at the time.*

---

On the way back to the shed, the sun was sinking low. At the end of the alley, the plate that announced the days remaining came into view. Trope passed it without lingering on it. By now, so did I. Watching a number every day eventually does that to a person. I felt that as frightening for the first time that day.

Trope left first, and I sat alone in front of the shed, watching the sky darken.

*"Because it was right." That one sentence followed me all through the night. I set it against my own story. When someone from the Reckoning wrecked our family, the Scales had calculated that too, precisely. The disposition had come out precise too. And yet I hadn't been able to say what Trope said, in front of that disposition. Because it was right. I'd never once put that sentence into my own mouth.*

*Trope could say it in front of the Scales that had taken her mother. I couldn't say it in front of the man who'd wrecked our home. The same sentence, but one of us lived by it while the other hadn't managed to swallow it in thirty-one years. I didn't know, that night, where that difference came from. I only knew clearly that the difference unsettled me.*

*And in that unease, I noticed one small thing more. I'd already been in this city for several days now. Under the name Had, helping with errands at the Scales, drawing well water, asking after an old man's health. I was becoming, little by little, a person of this city, day by day. And as I became that person, I kept forgetting the sentence I'd brought to this city, more and more often.*

*I didn't think, that night, that this was a dangerous thing. I know now. Forgetting and hiding are different things. I thought I was hiding my name. In fact I was forgetting myself. And of the two, the one that would hurt me more, in the end, was the second.*
