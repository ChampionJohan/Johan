# Chapter 40. Void

> _Previously_ — Pheugon said, "This city has turned." The clause activated. It took the square time to understand those six words. A low sound came from the Scales' terminal.

The screen at the terminal took three seconds to change.

I was counting those three seconds. The habit of counting numbers comes out even at times like this. One, two, three. After three, letters appeared on the screen.

**Sentence of Demolition — Void.**

That was all. No large lettering, no bell. The Scales displayed that sentence at exactly the same size as every other sentence.

*Even now, I find that indifference strange. It was the moment a whole city lived. But to the Scales, it was only one more update. A single item had changed, nothing more. The Scales didn't rejoice, and it didn't grieve. That was only natural. But that day, for the first time, that very naturalness hurt.*

Beneath the sentence, in small letters, the grounds were listed. "Confirmer: Herald Pheugon. Declaration received. Article 4, Clause 11 invoked." That was the first time I saw my own name appear on the Scales' screen. Through all one hundred twelve cases, my name had never once appeared there. I had always been the one who read — never the one who was recorded.

*That day I entered the Scales' record for the first time. Not as an item, but as a cause. The Scales did not weigh me. It only wrote down what I had done, as grounds. The difference didn't seem large to me then. It feels large now.*

A child standing beside the terminal reached out and touched the screen. Fingers landing, then lifting away. Not understanding what it meant, the child looked up at the nearest adult. The adult lifted the child into their arms.

---

The square began to make sound only after that.

At first it was one person's weeping. Then several. Then it turned into a sound that couldn't be told apart anymore — weeping mixed with laughter, names being called, people simply crying out.

The baker threw her arms around the person beside her. The market merchant sank to the ground and wept into both hands. The woman holding her child lifted the child up into the air. Not understanding a thing, the child laughed along with her.

Two old men held on to each other. One pressed his face into the other's shoulder and said, "We lived. We lived." The other only nodded, without answering.

*I recall that sight often, even now. Not many people ever hear a hundred twenty thousand voices weep at once. It wasn't the sound of sorrow. And yet, though it wasn't sorrow, it wore exactly the shape of sorrow. I learned the difference between the two, that day.*

---

Trope didn't cry.

I looked at her. She stood among the crowd, taking in every sound rising around her. But her face didn't match those sounds. It wasn't relief, and it wasn't joy. It was some unfamiliar expression.

I walked toward her. It wasn't easy, cutting through the crowd. Everyone tried to catch hold of me. Reaching for my hand, calling my name, trying to clap my shoulder. I didn't avoid those hands. But I didn't stop, either.

When I reached her, I didn't know what to say.

"…Now." I said. "You don't have to leave anymore."

She looked at me.

"…Yes."

"…The reason you wanted to leave is gone now."

She looked at me for a long time, then shook her head.

"…The reason isn't gone."

I didn't understand what she meant.

"…The reason is exactly the same. My mother was pushed out, and I still inherited that weight. It was the city that the Scales called void. Not me."

*I didn't fully understand her words then. Now I do. She was keeping her own share of weight separate from the city's salvation. The city had been saved. But she herself still wasn't sure she had a place inside that salvation.*

---

On the platform, the Adjudicator was announcing something.

I didn't hear all of it. Disposition procedures, future policy for the Scales, records of the clause's invocation. No one else was listening fully either. Everyone was too busy weeping, or laughing, or holding on to one another.

I looked for the substitute herald. He stood alone at the edge of the square. No one had caught hold of him. To the people of this city, he was still a stranger's face.

I went to him.

"…Thank you." I said.

He looked at me.

"…For what."

"…For coming. In my place."

"I only did the job I was assigned." he said. He paused, then went on. "But I learned something today."

"…What."

"That even if I'd come, I could never have read that sentence." He lifted the paper slightly. "Because I didn't know this city."

*I had nothing to say to that. He held no resentment. And that very evenness made things heavier for me, not lighter.*

---

I stood in the middle of the square and looked at the people.

People, saved. A hundred twenty thousand of them. I had spoken that sentence for them. When I said it, I hadn't known what it meant. Now, in the middle of these sounds, I was only just beginning to understand what I had done.

This city lives.

This city, the Reckoning, the city that had raised the person who took my family from me — lives.

*When that thought first entered me, I didn't yet know what it meant. I received it simply as a fact. The feeling took time to attach itself.*

I didn't weep among those people. I didn't laugh either. I just stood there. While everyone around me was mixing tears and laughter together, I couldn't manage either one.

Trope looked at me. The Adjudicator looked at me. The substitute herald looked at me.

*What did they see in my face, that moment? I still wonder. I don't know what expression I was wearing. But I knew one thing myself. That something inside me hadn't even started yet.*

The sun rose fully over the square. Laughter spread outward through the whole city. Listening to that sound, I began, very slowly, walking out of the square.

---

The streets were no different from the square.

Doors stood open in every house. Someone had set out a table in an alley and was sharing food. Children ran shrieking through the streets. An old man sat in a doorway, catching every passerby to say the same thing. "Did you hear? Void. Void." Each time he said it, his voice cracked a little more.

I walked through them. No one seemed to recognize me. That felt, strangely, like relief. In the square, everyone had known my name. Here, I was just someone passing through.

*That afternoon, walking the streets, I had a strange thought. These people had no idea what I really was. Some knew me as a savior, some as just a stranger, a passing child. Neither knew the real me. Only two people in this city knew the real me. The Adjudicator, and Trope.*

---

When I reached the well, I stopped.

That was the place I'd first landed in this city. The well with moss on its stone rim. Forty days before, I had opened my eyes there and heard the words, "You're not from here, are you?"

People were at the well today too. No one drawing water. Everyone sat perched on the stone rim, laughing. Someone had begun to sing. It was a song I didn't know the tune to, but the person beside them joined in, and the person beside them too.

I set a hand against the stone rim. I found the spot Trope had pointed to. The groove her mother's hand had supposedly left there. I ran my fingertip over it.

*In that moment I thought of Trope. Was she, right now, singing along with this song? Or was she sitting somewhere alone, turning over again the words she'd said — "the reason isn't gone." I wanted to know which it was. But I had no strength left to go find out.*

A while later, I left the well. The singing grew fainter behind me.

Walking away, I turned back once. The silhouettes of the people sitting on the well's stone rim wavered in the evening light. Someone clapped, someone else laughed. That scene felt to me like a painting. A painting I wasn't inside of.

*I didn't know then why it looked like a painting. Now I know. I was the one who had made that scene. But the one who makes something usually doesn't get to live inside what they've made. The one who opens the door and the one who lives inside it are different people.*

---

Passing the bakery, the baker caught me again.

This time she pressed bread into my hands. Still warm.

"…Eat." she said. "No charge today. No one's paying today."

I took the bread. Warmth spread across my palm.

"…Thank you."

"I'm the one who's thankful." she said, her eyes reddening again. "If it hadn't been for you, today would have been the last day this bakery was ever open."

I felt like I should have smiled at that. I tried to. The corners of my mouth wouldn't move.

*I still wonder what my face looked like to her in that moment. What did she see in my expression? Sorrow? Being overwhelmed? She didn't ask anything after that — she just patted my shoulder once and turned away. Maybe she saw it exactly right. Maybe she simply thought that wasn't a moment to say so.*

I walked a long time with the bread still in my hand. I never took a single bite.

---

As the sun began to set, I walked out near the city wall.

Few people came out this far. Everyone had stayed in the square or the streets. I sat down with my back against the wall. The evening wind blew. The day's sounds reached me only faintly from here — laughter, singing, someone calling out a name.

*Listening to those sounds, I realized something strange. I wasn't sad. But I wasn't happy either. Neither of those two words could explain who I was, in that moment.*

I looked at my hands.

They were shaking.

*I looked at that hand for a long time. In the square, reading the clause, that hand hadn't shaken. Not when I read the disposition, not when I said "This city has turned." But now, doing nothing at all, sitting with my back against the wall, this hand was shaking.*

*I didn't have a name for that trembling then. I do now. It wasn't the trembling of fear. It was something long suppressed, leaking out of my body for the first time.*

I sat there a long while, the bread resting on my knees. The sky reddened, then slowly darkened.

---

It was full dark by the time the Adjudicator found me.

I knew it was him from his footsteps first. A familiar walk. He came and set his back against the wall beside me. He stood there a long while without a word.

"…Everyone's looking for you." he said. "No one knows you're here."

"…I wanted to be here."

He didn't press further.

"Today makes forty years." the Adjudicator said. His voice was low. "I spent forty years just reading that clause. I never thought I'd see it invoked."

I said nothing.

"It feels strange." he said. "I'm happy, but it doesn't feel like happiness. Do you know why."

*I couldn't answer that question. But hearing it, I felt for the first time that I wasn't alone. The Adjudicator, too, was standing there that evening carrying something he couldn't explain. Only his and mine weren't shaped the same.*

"…Adjudicator." I said. "What about that person. What about that person."

I couldn't finish the sentence. The Adjudicator didn't ask who I meant. He simply fell silent for a moment.

"If you mean your family." he said. "I don't know. Where that person is now, what weight they've gone on adding since — I don't know. The Scales would know."

"…I never asked the Scales."

"Why."

I couldn't answer.

*That was the first time I admitted it to myself. I was afraid to check that person's weight. Afraid it would come out low. Afraid that person, like this city, had turned somewhere too. That possibility frightened me more than the Reckoning's survival did.*

The Adjudicator rested a hand on my shoulder once. The same weight as before, in the square.

"Rest tonight." he said. "Think about tomorrow, tomorrow."

He left me with those words and walked into the dark. I watched his back until it was gone completely.

Far off, the singing still carried. Someone rang a bell. This time it wasn't the bell of proclamation, but simply a bell of joy. I found it strange that the same iron could make both those sounds.

*That night, for the first time, I admitted to myself that I was holding something back. I still couldn't name what it was. But I knew clearly, at least, that it could no longer be held back.*

*I couldn't sleep that night. The next morning, I found the name.*
