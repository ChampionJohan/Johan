# Chapter 27. The One Who Proclaimed

> _Previously_ — The Adjudicator showed Pheugon the original text of
> Article 4, Clause 11: "The confirmation shall be performed by the
> one who read this sentence aloud." There had been three city-wide
> cases, and all three cities fell. The third herald had been born
> and raised in that city, and fled the night before the
> proclamation. The Adjudicator said they'd talk about what
> "confirmation" actually meant the next day.

The next day I arrived earlier than the time we'd agreed.

I waited a little outside the door. No sound came from inside. Unlike
the day before, I knocked without hesitating.

"Come in."

The answer came quickly this time.

The Adjudicator sat in the same chair as yesterday. There was no
scroll on the desk. Instead he sat with nothing in front of him,
waiting for me. That absence felt heavier than the scroll had.

"Sit."

I sat.

---

"You know what the Scales does," the Adjudicator said.

"It measures weight. Without error."

"That's right. Precisely. Who stole what, who lied to whom, whether a
hand was raised to a terminal or not — all of it converts into
weight. The Scales does that perfectly."

"…Yes."

"But there's one thing the Scales can't do."

I waited.

"Direction," the Adjudicator said. "The Scales measures weight, not
direction. The posture you're sitting in right now, this weight — the
Scales stamps that precisely. But whether you're in the middle of
turning around, or just sitting still — the Scales doesn't see that.
It reads the same value either way."

"Why?"

"Don't know," the Adjudicator said. "That's simply the Scales' limit.
The Scales is a machine that measures what has already accumulated.
It doesn't measure which way you're facing right now, this instant.
There's no method for measuring that at all."

*At first, listening, I just took it as another story about the
Scales' flaws. Familiar territory. I'd spent two months combing
precedents for gaps in the Scales. I still didn't know, at that
point, that this particular story was headed straight for me.*

---

"So whoever wrote the clause," the Adjudicator went on, "handed that
judgment to a person. Because it's something the Scales can't do."

"To just anyone?"

"No." The Adjudicator looked straight at me. "Not just anyone. To the
one who read the sentence aloud."

I looked at him.

"Why that person, of all people."

The Adjudicator chose his words.

"Think about it," he said. "Who knows this city's sin most precisely.
The Scales. But the Scales can't see direction. Who's the next most
precise?"

I couldn't answer.

"The one who read the sentence," the Adjudicator said. "That person
read out loud — with his own mouth — exactly how much accumulated
weight this city carried, and exactly which sins had piled up to make
that number. More precisely than any citizen — more precisely even
than someone born and raised in this city — that person knows this
city's sin, sentence by sentence."

"Wouldn't someone from the city know better?" I asked. "If they've
lived here their whole life, wouldn't they know more than the
reader?"

"They'd know it differently," the Adjudicator said. "Someone from
this city knows it by living it. They know who wronged whom, by
rumor, in their body. But they don't know it as exact numbers. What
weight the Scales assigned to their neighbor's wrongdoing — they can
only guess, not know precisely. The reader is different. The reader
read that number, that item, that sentence aloud, without dropping a
single word. Knowledge no citizen of this city holds."

Listening, I understood it. And the understanding frightened me.
Knowing precisely and loving had nothing to do with each other. If
anything, they might run opposite ways.

"But what if that person hates this city," I asked. My voice cracked.
"What if the person who knows the sin most precisely also hates this
city the most? How can you trust that judgment then?"

The Adjudicator looked at me for a moment.

"You don't," he said. "The clause isn't trusting that person. The
clause is only saying that no one else has the standing to judge.
Not entrusted because trustworthy — entrusted because there's nowhere
else to entrust it."

"What's the difference."

"A great one," the Adjudicator said. "Standing doesn't choose the
person. Whether that person is good or bad, whether he loves this
city or hates it — the clause doesn't ask. It asks exactly one thing.
Did he read it."

I still had nothing to say.

"So the clause asks that person," the Adjudicator said. "Whether the
sin you knew and this city, as it stands now, are different. There's
exactly one person in the world with the standing to answer that. The
one who read this city's sin most precisely."

"How does the confirmation work," I asked. "Just… think it? Or does
he write something down?"

"Speech," the Adjudicator said. "Aloud. This clause was consistent
from beginning to end — not thought, not signature. A voice. One
sentence is enough. 'This city has turned.' But it can't be said just
anywhere."

"Where, then?"

"On the fortieth day," the Adjudicator said. "In the square. Right
before demolition executes, with the whole city watching. The same
place, the same hour as the proclamation. This time, the confirmation
has to be spoken there."

Hearing that, I felt something go cold through my body. It meant that
the man who had fled would have to go back to that very spot, and
this time with the whole city watching.

---

I heard that and stayed still.

The room was quiet. Faint voices drifted in from outside the window.

I suddenly remembered what Trope had said. *The face people make when
they're thinking about something that doesn't show up on the Scales.*
I hadn't been able to answer her then. Now I thought I understood.
What face I'd been wearing, all this time.

*I still remember that moment sentence by sentence. It's strange.
People usually remember big events in a blur. But that moment alone
comes to me one sentence at a time, each arriving separately. Maybe
that's exactly how it happened inside my head. One sentence at a
time, in order, everything came down.*

Without that man. No confirmation.

Without confirmation. The clause doesn't move.

If the clause doesn't move. The demolition never becomes void.

If I don't read. It means I am — permanently — the only person who
ever read this city's sin aloud, and now never will.

If I don't read. This city will certainly fall.

*I'd already run this arithmetic once before. Five months ago. That
day in the supervisor's office, when I received the papers. I'd
looked at those papers and felt relief. I hadn't known then what that
relief was. I know now.*

*I hadn't fled thinking this city would live.*

*I had fled knowing this city would certainly die.*

*That day in the supervisor's office, I heard the number a hundred
and twenty thousand. I told myself, at the time, it was a number I'd
never tried to count. But in truth I'd already started counting the
instant I heard it — not as people, but as a possibility. For a city
of a hundred and twenty thousand to fall, the clause would have to
stay silent. For the clause to stay silent, I would have to not read.
I had already finished that calculation in the supervisor's office, at
the very moment I took the papers in my hand.*

*My hand hadn't shaken. I called that composure. The steadiness of a
man who'd read a hundred and twelve cases. That was a lie. A composed
man is a man whose calculation is finished.*

---

I looked at my hands.

They were shaking.

Too late. Hands that had never once shaken through a hundred and
twelve readings were shaking now, here. And I knew this shaking
wasn't coming from fear. It was the shaking of a man caught.

*Caught at what. By myself.*

My weight had been the lowest in our whole cohort — low enough that
the supervisor checked twice. Everyone assumed it was diligence. So
had I.

*I know now that it wasn't diligence. I wasn't a child afraid of
weight. I thought, someday, there'd be a sentence I would have to
read, and I didn't want to lose my standing to read it. That the
sentence would turn out to be the Reckoning's — I hadn't thought that,
back then. Or maybe I already had. Since I was twelve.*

*Living with the lowest possible weight, and holding the most
precisely qualified standing — I'd lived three years not even knowing
those were the same thing. And the hands that had earned that
standing had fled, in the end, precisely so as not to read the
sentence. The person most qualified had chosen, most certainly, not
to use that qualification.*

For the first time I recalled the moment I'd received the papers
assigning me to the Reckoning. Seeing the city's name, I'd laughed.
Without a sound, just for an instant. *That was relief,* I'd told
myself then.

*I was wrong for three years. That wasn't relief. It was certainty.
The certainty that the Reckoning had come to me. The certainty that
if I read that document, a chance existed for the clause to fire.
And if I didn't read it—*

I recalled the night I first found the clause. That small print.
*Turns.* I read the word three times. That night, I told myself I
hadn't "quite grasped what it meant."

*A lie. I understood it precisely. I'd read the word three times not
because I didn't understand it. The calculation to eliminate that
possibility had already begun, right then.*

The supervisor had once asked. *You got something with the
Reckoning?* I'd said no.

*That lie was proof I'd already begun planning to flee. I already
knew, then, that if I didn't read this city, the one person who could
save it would cease to exist, permanently.*

*I hadn't fled. I had killed. A city where nothing had happened yet —
killed forty days before, in the hallway where I took the papers.*

---

The Adjudicator watched me for a long time.

He didn't seem to be waiting for me to say anything. He was just
watching. Waiting for me to come down that staircase on my own.

I couldn't say anything. There was nothing to say. No excuse, no
denial. The Adjudicator already knew. What I had only just come to
know, he seemed to have known all along.

He opened his mouth.

His voice was low.

"So it wasn't fear," he said, "that made you run."

I couldn't answer.

*I can still repeat that sentence word for word, not a syllable off.
It didn't scold me. He wasn't angry, wasn't surprised. He was simply
stating a fact. And that one fact brought down, all at once,
everything I had spent three years telling myself.*

*I was a boy who never did wrong. I wasn't a boy who didn't get
angry — I was a boy who withheld anger. I was a boy who took care not
to lose his standing. Every one of those sentences read differently
now. Not caution — preparation. Not restraint — aim.*

*The day of my first proclamation, I'd erased the shape of the
merchant's mouth. And a hundred and eleven more times after that. I
called it the fruit of training. I know now. I hadn't been practicing
how to erase. I hadn't been practicing how to endure, either. I had
been practicing how to wait — for the one day I would not read,
toward this city.*

The Adjudicator rose from his seat. I thought he would say something
more — comfort, or reproach. Instead he simply walked to the window
and opened it a crack. Evening air came into the room.

"I didn't tell you this to blame you," he said, looking out the
window. "I told you because I thought you needed to know. What
happens after that is yours."

I couldn't answer that either. He didn't wait for an answer. He just
stood, looking out the window. His back looked like the back of a man
who had, thirty-one years running, taken the same scroll out and put
it back in.

---

I don't know how long I sat in the Adjudicator's office.

He said nothing more. Neither did I. The sun was going down outside
the window. The number lit on the square's board quietly cast its
light over the whole city, behind my back.

*I walked out of that room that day a different person. To be
precise, I hadn't become a different person. I had, for the first
time, seen the original one. For three years I'd thought I was a good
boy. That goodness had been aimed, with precision, in one direction
the whole time — that, I learned for the first time that day.*

*I don't know what Jonah thought, walking toward Nineveh. I don't
know how he understood himself, aboard the ship to Tarshish. But I
know this now — that fleeing can, sometimes, be a more exact
calculation than arriving.*

*And I had already carried that calculation out.*

---

I went home that night and sat for a long time.

There was something I wanted to say. I didn't know exactly to whom,
or what. I only felt that if I didn't say something aloud, I wouldn't
survive it. For three years I had made a career of reading aloud, and
in all that time I had never once said my own story out loud.

I opened my mouth, then closed it again.

The next day, I knocked on the Adjudicator's door again.
