# Chapter 39. I've Been There

> _Previously_ — Mena came to the well and met Father. When she showed
> him the paper with his own name written on it, he read it aloud for
> the first time. Still unfamiliar, but something was slowly returning.

Even after Mena left again, our days at the well didn't change much.
Drawing water, walking, setting down the record each evening — it
repeated. Strangely, that repetition put my mind at ease. In Haven,
every day had felt like a new emergency. Here, every day resembled
yesterday. I already knew, by then, how precious that resemblance was.

Someone new came out at the well.

This time it was a young boy. About ten years old. He came out unable
even to cry, and just sat there, dazed.

His clothes were old and thin. His shoes didn't match. His look brought
back the way the children of Brick Hollow used to dress. I didn't know
which hollow he'd come from. But his eyes weren't unfamiliar to me at
all.

*Looking at him, I remembered the first time we met Father, months ago.
I remember how helpless we were then. Now it was different. We knew
what to do.*

Speon, as he always did, brought water first. The boy took it but
couldn't drink. His hands were trembling.

*I saw that trembling. And for the first time, I realized this place had
been holding more people than Speon alone could carry. Now, we were
with him.*

Speon set the jar down in front of the boy and stepped back without a
word. I understood that stepping back was a kindness. Speon had long
known how not to approach anyone first.

Father was watching the boy too, from a little distance. Something
familiar crossed his face. I didn't know exactly what expression it was.
But I felt that Father was recognizing something in that boy.

---

My brother sat quietly beside the boy.

"…I've been there," my brother said. "Our father came out of a well
like this too. At first, he knew nothing either."

The boy looked at my brother. For the first time, there was some
response in his eyes.

"…And now?" the boy asked. His voice cracked.

"…Now he can read his own name out loud," my brother said. "Not all of
it. But it's coming back, little by little."

Hearing that, the boy carefully took the water jar in both hands. And he
began drinking, very slowly. Watching, I only then realized I'd been
holding my breath.

"…I can't remember what my name was. Not any of it," the boy said. His
voice was thick.

"…That's all right," my brother said. "It doesn't have to come to you
right now. It took our father months."

"…Months?" the boy asked, his face startled.

"…Yes," my brother said. "But he wasn't alone for those months. I think
that was the most important part."

---

From that day on, the boy followed my brother everywhere. He tried to
copy everything my brother did.

When my brother mended the bucket, the boy watched and then quietly
offered his hand to help. When my brother walked the well with Father,
the boy trailed a few steps behind. I thought that looked like the old
my brother — how he too had once walked, wordless, in someone's
footsteps.

One day the boy picked up a stone and handed it to my brother. He didn't
explain why. My brother didn't ask why either, and just took it. Watching
that, I thought of Father. Father used to gather stones for no reason
too. I thought this resemblance might not be a coincidence.

Speon watched all this from a distance and said quietly to me:

"…Your brother's become a completely different person now."

"…How?"

"…He used to try to find answers," Speon said. "Now he's building a
place beside people. I think that's the far harder work."

"…Why are you helping me?" the boy asked one day.

My brother thought for a moment before answering.

"…Because I've had it done for me," my brother said. "Someone stayed
beside me. I've received that. So I can give it too."

The boy repeated the answer, as if turning it over.

"…Because you've had it done for you…" the boy murmured.

"…Yes," my brother said. "Someday you'll be that for someone too. You
probably can't believe it now."

The boy tilted his head. His face didn't look like he fully understood.
But something seemed to have stayed with him. From that day on, every
time he filled his water jar, he ladled one scoop more than his own share
and set it aside, not even knowing who it was for.

*Hearing that answer, I understood, for the first time, one whole
sentence: someone who has never been loved finds it harder to learn how
to love. My brother was passing on, unchanged, what he had received.*

I listened to that conversation from beside them. I knew the old my
brother couldn't have answered a question like that. The old him would
have said "just because." This brother knew the reason.

After a few days, the boy started smiling a little. He still didn't know
his own name. But he liked the temporary one my brother had given him.

"…Will I get to find my real name too, someday?" the boy asked.

"…Yes," my brother said. "Slowly. But it will come back."

"…How do you know it will come back?"

My brother glanced toward Father for a moment. Father was just then
drawing up the bucket at the well.

"…Because I've seen it," my brother said. "Our father didn't know
anything at first either. Now he reads his own name out loud. I saw
that with my own eyes. So I believe it."

The boy smiled for the first time at that. A short smile. But a clear
one. I wrote that smile briefly into my record that evening. "Today, the
nameless boy smiled for the first time."

Watching that smile, I felt something strange. Only months ago my
brother had been someone who couldn't speak with certainty to anyone
about anything. Now he was handing certainty to someone else, based on
what he himself had seen.

Toward evening, Speon came over and stroked the boy's head.

"…You can stay here until your name comes to you," Speon said. "No need
to hurry."

The boy nodded. And for the first time, he got up on his own to go fill
his water jar.

---

That night, my brother said to me:

"…I felt something strange today, talking to that boy."

"…What was it?"

"…I was saying, word for word, something I learned from that man.
'Even without a name, you're here now. That's enough.' I never once
memorized that line. It just came out."

I said nothing for a moment, hearing that. Only the sound of the night
wind brushing the trees by the well.

"…Doesn't that scare you?" I asked.

"…What?"

"…You becoming like that man."

My brother thought about that question for a long time.

"…I think it does scare me a little," he said at last. "But I don't
dislike it. That's strange."

I gave no answer to that. I just sat a little closer to my brother. He
seemed to notice, and offered his shoulder without a word.

*I set that down as the last line of this chapter. I knew then. My
brother still couldn't explain who that man was. But he was already
carrying that man's words into his own body. I was certain, for the
first time that day, how close that was to the beginning of faith.*
