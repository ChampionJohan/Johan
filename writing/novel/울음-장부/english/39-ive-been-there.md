# Chapter 39. I've Been There

> _Previously_ — Mena came to the well and met Father. When she showed him the name she'd kept, he read his own name aloud for the first time. Still unfamiliar, but something was slowly coming back.

Even after Mena left again, the days at the well didn't change much. Drawing water, walking, leaving a record in the evening — the repetition continued. Strangely, that repetition set my mind at ease. In Haven, every day had felt like a new incident. Here, every day resembled yesterday. I already knew now how precious that resemblance was.

Someone else came out of the well.

This time it was a young boy. He looked about ten. The moment he came out, he couldn't even cry — he just sat there, dazed.

The sky was unusually overcast that day. Under that overcast sky, I thought the boy's face was the same gray as the clouds. This wasn't the first time I'd seen a face like that at the well. But I never got used to it, no matter how many times.

His clothes were old and thin. His shoes didn't match. In that sight, the clothes of Brick Hollow's children overlapped in my mind. I didn't know which hollow he'd come from. But his eyes were not unfamiliar to me.

There was an old scar on his knee. I looked at that scar and started to guess whether it was recent or very old, then stopped myself. Either way, that wasn't what mattered right now.

*Watching him, I thought of when we first met Father, months ago. I remembered how helpless we had been then. It was different now. We already knew what we had to do.*

Speon, as always, brought water first. The boy couldn't even drink it once he had it. His hands were shaking.

The boy's reflected face wavered on the surface of the water jar. Watching that trembling reflection, I wondered if the boy still found even his own face unfamiliar. People just out of the well were often like that. They treated their own hands, their own faces, as if seeing them for the first time.

*I saw that trembling. And for the first time, I realized this place had been holding more people than Speon alone could carry. Now we were with him too.*

Speon set the water jar down in front of the boy and stepped back without a word. I knew that stepping back was a kindness. Speon had long known how to never approach anyone first.

Father was watching the boy from a little way off too. Something familiar crossed his face. I didn't know exactly what expression it was. But I felt that Father was recognizing something in that boy.

Father slowly walked toward him. I tensed for a moment. Father was still clumsy about growing close to strangers. But that day was different. Father quietly crouched down in front of the boy.

"…You're scared, aren't you?" Father asked. In a very small voice.

The boy looked up at Father. He didn't answer. But he nodded, just barely.

"…So was I," Father said. "Still am, sometimes."

I held my breath, hearing that. It was the first time I'd heard Father speak his own fear aloud like that.

"…Then when does it stop being scary?" the boy asked.

Father couldn't answer that question for a long moment. I knew he was trying hard not to pretend to know what he didn't.

"…I don't know," Father finally said. "But you can still drink the water even while you're scared. That much I know."

The boy looked down at the water jar at that. His hands still shook. But with that shaking hand, he pulled the jar a little closer to himself.

*Watching that scene, I thought — Father hadn't given the boy an answer. Father had no answer to give. But Father had given him the fact that he was still scared too. That stayed with the boy longer than any answer could have.*

---

Jeteo sat quietly beside the boy.

"…I've been there too," Jeteo said. "Our father came out here just like this. He didn't know anything at first, either."

The boy looked at Jeteo. For the first time, there was some response in his eyes.

"…And now?" the boy asked. His voice cracked.

"…Now he can read his own name out loud," Jeteo said. "Not all of it. But it's coming back, bit by bit."

Hearing that, the boy took the water jar carefully in both hands. And began drinking, very slowly. Watching that, I realized only then that I'd been holding my breath.

I thought, in that moment, of the day we'd first handed Father water, months before. We hadn't known anything then. There was nothing else we could do besides hand him water. Jeteo could do far more now than he could then. But in the end, what he was doing was still the same thing. Handing over water. Sitting beside him.

"…I don't remember what my name was. At all," the boy said. His voice was choked.

"…That's okay," Jeteo said. "It doesn't have to come to you right now. Our father took months."

"…Months?" the boy asked, startled.

"…Yeah," Jeteo said. "But he wasn't alone during those months. I think that was the most important part."

"…Is it not okay to be alone?" the boy asked.

"…It's not that it's not okay," Jeteo said. "But it takes longer, alone. I've seen it for myself."

The boy gripped the water jar a little tighter, as if turning that over in his mind.

"…Why are you being so nice to me?" the boy asked again. His voice still carried a trace of wariness.

Jeteo laughed a little at the question.

"…You already asked me that," Jeteo said.

"…I wanted to ask again," the boy said.

Jeteo really laughed at that. I thought it was the first bright laugh I'd seen from him in days.

"…You can ask," Jeteo said. "As many times as you want."

"…Even the same thing again?"

"…Yeah," Jeteo said. "I asked the same thing about a hundred times myself. No one ever told me to stop asking."

Hearing that, I thought of Haven's intake window. There, you couldn't file the same thing twice. It was already a resolved entry. Only then did I know, in a single word, what that city had lacked. A place to ask again.

---

From that day on, the boy followed Jeteo everywhere. He tried to copy everything Jeteo did.

When Jeteo mended the bucket, the boy watched from beside him, then slid his own hand in to help. When Jeteo walked around the well with Father, the boy followed a few steps behind. I thought that looked like the old Jeteo. He too had once followed someone's footsteps like that, wordlessly.

One day the boy picked up a stone and handed it to Jeteo. He didn't explain why. Jeteo didn't ask why either — he just took it. Watching that scene, I thought of Father. Father used to collect stones for no reason too. I thought this resemblance might not be an accident.

Jeteo carried that stone in his pocket. A few days later I saw him take it out and set it back down in front of the boy.

"…Here, take this back," Jeteo said.

"…Why? I gave it to you."

"…I already received it," Jeteo said. "Now you hold on to it, and later give it to someone else. I think that's better."

The boy tilted his head at that, but took the stone back and put it in his pocket. Watching that, I felt something flowing from Jeteo to this boy, and someday, to yet another person.

Speon watched that scene from a distance and said quietly to me,

"…Your brother's turned into a completely different person now."

"…How so?"

"…He used to try to find answers," Speon said. "Now he's building a place beside him. I think that's a much harder thing to do."

"…Why is it harder?" I asked.

"…An answer, once you find it, is over," Speon said. "Being beside someone has to be rebuilt every day. Just because you were there yesterday doesn't mean you're there today."

I heard that and looked toward our hut. Father sat in the shade, watching the boy. It looked like a spot where nothing was happening. I already knew now that it wasn't nothing at all.

"…Why are you helping me?" the boy asked one day.

Jeteo thought for a moment before answering.

"…Because I've had it done for me," Jeteo said. "Being sat beside — I've had that too. So I can do it too."

"…By who?" the boy asked.

Jeteo glanced toward Speon for a moment, and toward the shade of the tree.

"…Lots of people," Jeteo said. "My sister. Some man. I'm still receiving it, right here."

The boy repeated that back, as if turning it over.

"…Because you've had it done for you…" the boy murmured.

"…Yeah," Jeteo said. "You'll be that for someone else someday too. You probably can't believe that now."

The boy tilted his head. It wasn't a face that had fully understood the words. But something seemed to have stayed with him. From that day on, whenever he filled his water jar, he scooped out one extra ladleful and set it aside, even though it wasn't his to give. Not even knowing who it was for.

*Hearing that answer, I understood one complete sentence for the first time. A person who has never been loved has a harder time learning how to love. Jeteo was giving away, exactly as he'd received it.*

I copied that sentence straight into my evening record right then. My hand shook as I wrote it. I knew it was a sentence about Jeteo, and at the same time a sentence about me. I was giving away what I'd received too. Seeds. Waiting. How to sit beside someone.

I'd been listening to that conversation from the side. I knew that the old Jeteo couldn't have answered a question like that. The old Jeteo would have said "just because." This Jeteo knew the reason.

I thought that difference was truly huge. Between "just because" and "because I've had it done for me" lay the whole of the months Jeteo had passed through. Having watched those months beside him, I knew the weight of that sentence better than anyone.

Within a few days, the boy began to smile a little. He still didn't know his own name. But he liked the temporary name Jeteo had given him.

Jeteo named him "Little Shadow," because the boy always followed behind him like a shadow. At first the boy laughed at the name. But after a few days, he really did come to like being called that.

"…This isn't really my name, though," the boy said one day.

"…I know," Jeteo said. "But a name needs to hold the place until the real one comes."

The boy nodded, turning that over.

"…Will I get to find my real name too, someday?" the boy asked.

"…Yeah," Jeteo said. "Slowly. But it'll definitely come back."

"…How do you know it'll definitely come back?"

Jeteo glanced toward Father for a moment. He was just then pulling the bucket up at the well.

"…Because I've seen it," Jeteo said. "Our father didn't know anything at first either. Now he reads his own name out loud. I've seen it with my own eyes. So believe it."

The boy smiled for the first time at that. A short laugh. But a clear one. I recorded that laugh briefly in that evening's record. "Today, the nameless boy smiled for the first time."

Watching that laugh, I felt something strange. A few months ago, Jeteo had been someone who couldn't say anything to anyone with certainty. Now Jeteo was handing certainty to someone else, based on what he himself had seen.

Speon came over to us that evening and stroked the boy's head.

"…You can stay here until your name settles," Speon said. "No hurry."

"…Can I stay here even without a name?" the boy asked. His voice still carried some anxiety.

"…You're a person even without a name," Speon said. "There's no rule here that says people without names can't stay."

The boy smiled toward Speon for the first time at that. A small smile. But I noticed it was the second smile the boy had shown in a few days. I was glad that smile was coming a little more often.

The boy nodded. And for the first time, got up on his own to go fill a water jar.

I watched him from behind. His steps were still careful. But they were clearly steadier than the day before. I didn't miss that small difference — I held it carefully in my eyes.

---

That night, Jeteo said to me,

"…I felt something strange talking to that boy today."

"…What was it?"

"…I was saying, word for word, what I learned from that man. 'Even without a name, you're here right now. That's enough.' I never once memorized that. It just came out."

I said nothing for a moment, hearing that. Only the sound of the night wind brushing the trees by the well.

"…Doesn't that scare you?" I asked.

"…What?"

"…You becoming like that man."

Jeteo thought about that for a long time. In that silence, I saw starlight flicker in his eyes now and then. The sound of night insects filled the well-yard.

"…It feels a little scary," Jeteo finally said. "But it doesn't feel bad. That's the strange part."

I didn't say anything back to that. I just sat a little closer beside him. Jeteo seemed to notice, and without a word, let his shoulder be there for me.

Far off, the boy made some sound — talking in his sleep, maybe. I turned my head toward it for a moment, then back to Jeteo. The boy was sleeping soundly. His sleeping face looked much more at ease than the day we'd met him.

"…That boy will say that to someone else someday too," I said.

"…I hope so," Jeteo said.

"…Then what will he call you, when he does?"

Jeteo didn't laugh at that for a long moment.

"…He doesn't have to remember my name," Jeteo finally said. "He just has to remember that someone sat beside him. I don't even know that man's name either."

*I set down those words as the last line of this chapter. I knew, then. Jeteo still couldn't explain who that man was. But he was already carrying that man's words in his body. I was sure, for the first time that day, of how close that was to the beginning of faith.*
