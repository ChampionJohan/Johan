# Chapter 25. Following the Trail

> _Previously_ — After Mena brought news of Father, the siblings cried together for the first time since leaving Brick Hollow. They learned that tears don't erase something — they lighten a weight — and set out north, following Father's trail.

The road north was rougher than the Wilderness had been.

The grass thinned more each day. The ground grew harder. Jeteo walked anyway, every day. I followed behind him, and every night I wrote down what had happened that day.

*That was the first time I really used the writing kit Mena had given me. My hand wasn't used to it yet, and the letters kept slanting crooked. I kept writing anyway.*

There was no shade along the road. By day the sun beat straight down on the crowns of our heads; by night, a wind blew that cut clean to the bone instead. That swing in temperature caught me off guard every time. It was a cold we'd never known in Brick Hollow.

Within a few days, the sole of Jeteo's shoe had started to split. He tried to hide that from me too. I only found out from watching him limp, again and again.

"…What's wrong with your shoe?" I asked.

"…Nothing," Jeteo said. It was always his answer. I didn't press him further. No matter how much I asked, he was someone who would insist it was nothing, to the very end.

That evening, without telling him, I wrapped his shoe's sole with a strip of cloth. The next morning Jeteo saw it and said nothing. He just put it on and walked. I knew that silence meant thank you.

Water ran short again. We filtered muddy water the way Noa had taught us and drank it. Even that, Jeteo made me drink first, and drank after.

"…You drink first," I said, more than once. Every time, Jeteo pretended not to hear.

At night we slept back to back. It was our way of blocking the wind, even a little. I could feel Jeteo's back growing thinner by the day. He seemed to wait until he'd made sure I was asleep before he'd let his own eyes close. More than once, I pretended to sleep just to watch him fall asleep.

At night the stars came out unusually thick. Looking up at them, I tried to remember the names of the constellations Father had once taught us back in Brick Hollow. I couldn't remember a single one. That saddened me. On the road to find Father, the very things he'd taught us were the first things slipping away.

*That night, for the first time, I understood that keeping a record wasn't only for my own sake. I wasn't writing to keep from forgetting. I was writing to catch hold again of things I'd already forgotten.*

---

Food ran low too, little by little. We sometimes dug up dried fruit or roots to eat. We told edible from inedible the way Noa had taught us, but we were never quite sure of it, so Jeteo always tasted a little first.

"…If it tastes bitter, spit it out," Jeteo said. Every time I heard that, I knew he was swallowing the danger first, in my place.

On the fifth day, we found footprints we didn't recognize.

Jeteo knelt down and studied them closely.

"…One person's footprints," he said. "But it's strange. Whoever it was circled the same spot again and again."

"…Sounds like someone who was lost," I said.

"…Or," Jeteo went on, "someone who kept looking for something he couldn't find."

Jeteo's finger traced over each print, one by one. The depths were all different. Some prints were shallow; others were dug unusually deep.

Jeteo looked at me. We were both thinking the same thing at the same moment.

*My heart started pounding hard again. I couldn't be sure whose footprints these were. But I could see in Jeteo's eyes that he already was.*

We stayed at that spot a long while longer. Jeteo circled the footprints again and again, studying them. Watching him, it looked as though he were retracing Father's steps with his own body.

"…What was this person doing here?" I asked.

Jeteo touched the ground with his hand. The dirt there was packed unusually hard.

"…He kept circling this same spot," Jeteo said. "Like… someone looking for something he couldn't find."

*That thought hurt something in me. Father had forgotten even his own name, and still he kept searching for something. I felt like I both knew and didn't know what it was.*

"…Do you think," I asked carefully, "he might have been looking for us?"

Jeteo didn't answer. Instead, he stared for a long time in the direction the footprints pointed. I knew that silence was his answer, in its own way.

Jeteo stood up, brushing off the dirt. His hands were trembling. I pretended not to notice. He must have known I was pretending.

---

Following the footprints, we met a man. He wasn't the one who had left them — just another traveler passing through the area.

The man had a heavy pack on his back. His face was burned dark by the sun, and his gait was slow. He didn't seem surprised to see us. It seemed running into two children on this road wasn't such a rare thing.

"…Have you seen anyone strange around here?" Jeteo asked. "Someone who counts numbers out loud to himself."

The man nodded.

"…I saw him," the man said. "A good while back. He was walking along counting numbers, over and over. Didn't answer when I spoke to him."

"…How long ago was that?" Jeteo asked. Impatience colored his voice.

"…Let's see. A day? Two?" The man tilted his head. "I couldn't say for sure. I only saw him in passing myself."

"…Where did he go?" I asked, urgent.

"…I don't know," the man said. "But there's someone who's good at finding people like that. I heard his name is Temel."

"…Where can we find him?" Jeteo asked.

"…Keep heading north and you'll run into him," the man said. "He's always moving around out there. Finding people — that's his line of work, they say."

"…Does he take payment?" I asked. We had nothing to give.

The man shook his head.

"…Never heard of it," he said. "I heard he just does it."

*The moment I heard that name, something about it felt strangely familiar. I didn't know why, then. Now I do. It was another name that had been quietly finding people, here and there, across this world.*

With that, the man went on his way. I watched his back for a long time. One name, tossed off by a stranger, had become the most important piece of information in the world to us that day.

---

That night, we started walking again in the direction the man had pointed us.

"…This Temel person — do you think it's worth finding him?" I asked.

"…If these footprints belong to Father," Jeteo said, "I'll meet anyone. I'm not letting anything slip past me now."

That night we walked under the stars again, for the first time in a while. There were no clouds, so the road was easy to see. I wanted to believe it was a good sign.

As I walked, I watched Jeteo's back. His shoe was still split open. But his stride had quickened, faster than before. I couldn't tell whether that speed came from impatience or from hope. Probably it was both.

As we walked, I wrote down again what had happened that day — the footprints, the man, the name Temel. My handwriting still slanted crooked. Even so, I tried not to leave out a single letter. I believed a day would come when I could tell Father this whole road, from beginning to end.

*I set those words down as the last line of this chapter. I knew, that day. Jeteo was no longer someone who only gathered evidence, or raged alone. He was, now, a person who searched. And I was the one who recorded that searching.*
