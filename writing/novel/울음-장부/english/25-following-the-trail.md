# Chapter 25. Following the Trail

> _Previously_ — After Mena brought word of Father, the brother and sister wept together for the first time since leaving Brick Hollow. Elpis learned that tears erase nothing — they only lift the weight, by their own weight — and the two of them set out north, following Father's trail.

The road north was rougher than the Wilderness had ever been.

The land grew stranger with every mile. Brick Hollow's soil had been red and heavy. Here the dirt ran pale and light, so light that a single gust erased our footprints within minutes. That frightened me in a way I couldn't quite explain. If our own tracks disappeared that easily, then any tracks someone might follow toward us would disappear just as easily. But it also meant the opposite — the tracks we were following could vanish before we ever found them.

The grass thinned out, then stopped altogether. The ground hardened underfoot. My brother kept walking anyway, every day. I walked behind him, and every night I wrote down what had happened that day.

*That was the first time I really used the writing tool Mena had given me. My hand didn't know it yet — the letters kept slanting sideways. I kept writing anyway.*

There was no shade on that road. By day the sun sat straight over our heads and beat down without mercy; by night the wind cut clear to the bone in the opposite direction. I was startled every time by how far apart those two temperatures stood. It was a cold Brick Hollow had never taught us.

Within days, the sole of my brother's shoe began to split. He tried to hide that from me too. I only found out because I noticed him limping.

"…What's wrong with your shoe?" I asked.

"…Nothing," my brother said. His usual answer. I didn't push. Pushing would only have gotten me the same nothing, over and over, until I gave up.

That evening, while he wasn't looking, I wrapped a strip of cloth around the broken sole. The next morning he saw it and said nothing. He just put the shoe on and walked. I understood that silence as his way of saying thank you.

The cloth wore through again within three days. Each time, I wrapped it again in secret. Each time, he wore it in secret. We were passing something back and forth between us, wordlessly, over one shoe. I came to think it was the only language the road had left us.

Once, the cloth had frozen stiff overnight, soaked through with dew. My brother rubbed it between his palms for a long time before he could get it on. I lay there and listened to that sound — a dry, papery rubbing that went on and on. I felt both sorry and grateful, hearing it.

Water ran short again. We filtered muddy water the way Noa had taught us. Even that, my brother made me drink first, and drank his own share after.

"…You go first," I told him, more than once. He always pretended not to hear.

Once I wrestled the water bowl out of his hands and pressed it to his mouth before he could stop me. He laughed, just for a second. Then he took exactly one swallow and handed the bowl straight back. I understood that single swallow was the only concession he would ever allow himself.

At night we slept back to back, trying to block a little of the wind. I could feel my brother's back growing thinner by the week. He never seemed to close his eyes until he'd made sure I was already asleep. More than once I pretended to sleep just to watch him finally let go.

There were an unusual number of stars at night. I looked at them and tried to remember the constellation names Father used to point out to us in Brick Hollow. Not one came back to me. That grieved me. On the road to find Father, the first things I was losing were the things he had taught me.

*That night I understood for the first time that keeping a record wasn't only for my own sake. I wasn't writing to keep from forgetting. I was writing to catch hold, again, of things I had already forgotten.*

That night, instead of the constellations' names, I counted how many stars I could see. I got to thirty and stopped. There was no counting the whole sky anyway. I thought that was a little like this journey — instead of trying to count what couldn't be counted, you counted what was in front of you.

My brother had unusual trouble falling asleep that night. I lay still, pretending to sleep, and listened to his breathing. He turned over several times before finally settling with his back to the sky. That back looked, to me, like a back trying to avoid something.

---

Food ran low too. We dug up dry fruit and roots when we found them. Even after Noa's lessons in what was safe to eat and what wasn't, we were never quite sure, so my brother always tasted things first, in small bites.

"…Spit it out if it's bitter," he'd say. Every time he said it, I understood that he was swallowing the risk before it ever reached me.

Once he chewed a root and winced and spat it out fast. I asked, frightened, if he was all right. He said, like it was nothing, "…just a little bitter." That night I saw him quietly rubbing his stomach. I pretended not to see. He probably knew I was pretending.

On the fifth day, we found a stranger's footprints.

My brother knelt and studied them closely.

"…One person," he said. "But it's strange. They circled the same spot, over and over."

"…Maybe someone who got lost," I said.

"…Or," my brother went on, "someone who kept searching for something and never found it."

His finger traced print after print. The depths were all different. Some were shallow. Some were pressed unusually deep.

I crouched beside him and studied the prints too. Grains of dirt were still crumbling loose at the edges, not fully settled. They weren't old. That fact frightened me and gladdened me at once.

"…Look at this," my brother said, pointing to one print. "This one's much deeper than the others. Like someone stood here a long time."

I laid my palm on the spot. The soil was still cool. I thought that coolness held, pressed into it, the shape of however long someone had stood there.

"…Brother." I asked. "If a person stands in one place long enough, does the ground remember?"

He looked at me for a moment.

"…That's not remembering," he said. "It's just pressed down."

"…But it's still there," I said.

He didn't answer. Instead he laid his own hand over the mark once more. I noticed, then, that his hand was bigger than the print.

He looked up at me. We had the same thought at the same moment.

*My heart started pounding again right then. I couldn't be certain whose print it was. But I could see in my brother's eyes that he already was.*

We stayed there a long while. My brother circled the prints again and again, studying them. Watching him, it looked as though he were retracing Father's steps with his own body.

"…What was this footprint doing here?" I asked.

He touched the ground again. The soil there was packed unusually hard.

"…Going in circles," he said. "Like… like someone searching for something they couldn't find."

*That answer ached in my chest. Father had forgotten his own name, and still he'd kept searching for something. I felt like I both understood what it was and didn't.*

"…Do you think," I asked carefully, "he might have been looking for us?"

My brother didn't answer. Instead he stared for a long time in the direction the tracks pointed. I understood that silence as his own kind of answer.

He rose and brushed the dirt from his knees. His hands were shaking. I pretended not to see. He probably knew I was pretending.

We followed the tracks a little further. After a few steps they suddenly changed direction. Then changed again. That zigzag looked, to me, like the walk of someone who couldn't decide where to go.

"…Whoever this was, they wandered a lot here," I said.

My brother nodded and said nothing. I felt that he was understanding that wandering in his own way. He, too, had walked lost for years now.

---

Following those tracks, we met a man. Not the one who'd left them — a different traveler, wandering the same stretch of ground.

He carried a heavy pack on his back. His face was sun-darkened, his gait slow. He wasn't startled to see us. Two children on this road, it seemed, wasn't so rare a sight.

A smell of dried herbs rose from his pack. Catching it, I guessed he'd traveled this road a long time. It struck me as strange that I let my guard down so quickly around a stranger — and yet that single smell alone put me somewhat at ease.

"…Have you seen anyone strange near here?" my brother asked. "Someone who counts numbers to himself?"

The man nodded.

"…I saw someone," he said. "A while back. Walking along, counting the whole time. Didn't answer when I spoke to him."

"…How long ago?" my brother asked, impatience bleeding into his voice.

"…Let's see. A day? Two?" The man tilted his head. "I don't know exactly. I only saw him passing by."

"…Which way did he go?" I asked urgently.

"…Don't know," the man said. "But there's someone who's good at finding people like that. I heard his name's Temel."

"…Where would we find him?" my brother asked.

"…Keep heading north and you'll run into him," the man said. "He's always on the move. Finding people's what he does."

"…Does he take payment?" I asked. We had nothing to give.

The man shook his head.

"…Never heard of it," he said. "I hear he just does it."

"…That's a strange person," I said, without thinking.

The man laughed a little at that.

"…Strange," he agreed. "But you get a few strange ones like that on this road. People who lend a hand for nothing in return. I doubted it too, at first. But after you run into a few, you just start figuring that's how it is."

"…Why do they do it?" I asked.

The man shrugged.

"…I asked one of them that myself," he said. "He told me someone had done the same for him first. So now he does it too."

I wanted to write that answer down on paper. But my hands were too cold to write just then. So instead I recited it to myself, several times over.

*Hearing that name, I felt a strange familiarity, though I didn't know why at the time. I know now. That name, too, was one that had been quietly finding people all across this world.*

With that, the man went on his way. I watched his back for a long time. A name a stranger had thrown out in passing had, by that day's end, become the most important piece of information in the world to us.

My brother stood watching the direction the man had disappeared into, then turned back to me.

"…Temel," he said aloud, testing the name. He seemed to be pressing it into his mouth so he wouldn't lose it.

"…I'm memorizing it too," I said. "Temel, Temel."

That made him smile — the first smile I'd seen from him in days. I was so glad to see it that I looked at his face longer than I needed to.

---

That night we started walking again in the direction the man had pointed.

It was an unusually bright moon. I picked my way by its light, watching the ground. Our shadows stretched long ahead of us, shifting shape with every step. I tried to gauge, from those shadows, how far we'd come. I couldn't.

"…This Temel person — do you think he's worth meeting?" I asked.

"…If these footprints belong to Father," my brother said, "I'll meet anyone. I'm not letting anything slip past me now."

"…What if they're not Father's?" I asked again. Asking that question frightened me, but that night, for some reason, it came out on its own.

My brother stopped walking and looked at me. He was quiet for a moment.

"…I'll still meet him," he said finally. "Even if it's not Father — if whoever left those tracks is someone else lost and wandering without a name, like us. Then it's still good for someone to go find them."

*That answer surprised me a little. My brother had always been someone with a clear purpose. But that day, there was something in his answer that went beyond purpose. I didn't know exactly what it was, not then.*

That night we walked under the stars again for the first time in a while. There were no clouds, so the road showed clearly. I wanted to believe that was a good sign.

Walking, I watched my brother's back. His shoe was still coming apart. But his stride had picked up. I couldn't tell whether that quickness came from anxiety or from hope. Probably both.

He kept lifting his eyes to the stars as he walked. I couldn't tell if it was to find the way, or just because he wanted something to lean on. He had never been a person who prayed openly to the sky. But that night, I watched his eyes rest there, longer than usual.

I wrote down what had happened that day as we walked — the footprints, the man, the name Temel. My handwriting still slanted sideways. Still, I tried not to leave out a single letter. I believed a day would come when I'd read this whole road to Father, start to finish.

As night deepened, the wind turned cold again. I folded the paper and tucked it inside my clothes, and took my brother's arm instead. He lowered his arm without a word so I could hold on. I think that small kindness was the warmest moment of that whole day.

Somewhere far off, a wild animal cried once. I froze. My brother gripped my hand and said, "…It's all right, that's far away," and I believed him. Most things my brother said, I could believe.

The sound didn't come again. Instead, the wind kept combing through the dry grass. That sound felt louder to me than the animal's cry had. Even on a night when nothing happened, the world kept making some sound or other.

We walked quite late that night. Our legs ached, but neither of us suggested stopping. I don't think it was only impatience. The moment we stopped walking, it felt like the hope we'd been building would stop too, if only for a moment.

Finally my brother slowed and spoke first.

"…Let's stop here for today. I know your legs hurt."

"…Yours hurt too," I said.

He laughed instead of answering. We set down our packs and settled in. Before lying down, I looked up at the stars one more time. Still not one constellation name came back to me. But that night, strangely, it didn't feel sad.

*I set that thought down as this chapter's last line. I understood, that day, that my brother was no longer someone gathering evidence, or someone raging alone. My brother had become someone searching. And I had become the one who kept the record of his search.*
