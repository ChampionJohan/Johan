# Chapter 48. Our Ledger (Concluded)

> _Previously_ — On the day of the first snow, everyone gathered by
> the well. The man at the flowerbed only watched from a distance.
> "Not everything's gotten better, but right now, this moment — it's
> good." I learned that a moment could be wholly good, even
> unresolved.

Winter passed, and a sprout came up in the spot where I had planted.

It was small. A little bigger than a fingernail. Small, exactly like
the sprout I'd seen in the flowerbed at Haven. I sat in front of it
for a long time.

A spring wind passed over the well. I noticed its smell was unlike
any season I remembered from Brick Hollow. Soil, water, and something
I couldn't name, all mixed together. I decided to just call it "the
smell of this place."

Brother came and sat beside it too. We looked at that small sprout
together in silence for a long while.

"…It won't get pulled up this time, will it?" Brother asked.

"…No," I said. "No one stamps COMPLETE here."

Brother laughed at that. Then he carefully patted down the soil around
the sprout with his fingertips.

"…Should we name this sprout?" Brother asked.

"…Name a seed?" I asked back.

"…Why not?" Brother said. "We lived out our names too, didn't we?"

I laughed at that. We thought it over for a long while, and in the end
couldn't settle on a name. But strangely, that was all right too.

*I began this record on the last night in Brick Hollow. That night I
wrote that we were wrong. I wrote that this record would be a story
about that wrongness. I read that sentence again now.*

The girl who wrote that sentence feels like a stranger to me now. She
was terrified, then. Now I sit here by this well, warmed by spring
sunlight. The same person — and yet, somehow, an entirely different
one.

I sometimes open the first page of this record again. It still seems
to hold the smell of Brick Hollow's dust. The night Brother shouted up
at the sky. Mother's hand, pushing us out past the boundary stones. I
don't want to erase those pages. Without that night, this place
wouldn't exist either.

Speon still keeps the well. Mena still gathers names. The two of them
are together often now. Mena finally set down her pack and settled
here by the well for good. The record box now fills a whole wall of
Speon's hut. I still don't know all of what happened between them. But
now I know this: some stories can be stood beside without ever
knowing how they end.

Temel set out on the road again. He's out there somewhere, looking for
someone else, right now. Before he left, he handed me a small
notebook. "Someday I hope your record and mine get bound together," he
said. I still keep that notebook empty. Noa and the children went back
to their tent camp too. Even as they left, they promised to come
again. I've decided not to doubt that promise.

---

We are still wrong. I still don't have a full answer for why God
allows suffering. Neither does Brother. The man at the flowerbed never
gave us an answer. He only ever held out his hand.

I sometimes think it strange. We're still living without an answer,
and yet it doesn't frighten us the way it used to. I can't explain the
reason in a clean sentence. All I can say is that a place has opened
up where it's all right not to have an answer.

Father still doesn't remember all of our names. Some days he looks at
Brother's face like a stranger's. Every time it happens, Brother
doesn't get angry. He just tells him the name again. However many
times it takes, without wearing thin.

On some days, Father wakes in the morning and calls our names out
clear. On those days, all of us just look at each other and smile,
without a word. I've seen that smile more times than I can count. And
every single time, it fills me up as if it were the first.

Brother no longer fears that rise and fall. On the days Father looks
at him like a stranger, Brother simply calls his name again — the way
you water a seed, every single day. I've come to understand that this
steadiness is the very same thing I learned once, in the flowerbed.
Waiting was never a thing that finished in a single act. It was a
thing that began again, every day.

*I set down that repetition in this record exactly as it is. This
isn't a story of complete recovery. This is a story that keeps going.*

I think of Brick Hollow sometimes. Of the mother who had to leave us
behind, of the old man who fell, of every name we left there. I don't
know if I'll ever see them again. But I know this now: I have written
every one of their names down in this record. So I won't forget.

I sometimes try to call Mother's voice back to mind. It never comes
back whole. But its warmth, at least, is still alive — living on
inside that one line Father brought back to us. "You're a child who's
always going to be asking. I'm proud of that." I've already written
that sentence down somewhere in this record. However many times I
read it again, it never grows old.

---

The Ledger is, even now, probably still "resolving" people's grief in
Haven. The COMPLETE stamp is probably still coming down somewhere,
even now. I didn't bring that city down. We never had the power to.

I've sometimes felt ashamed of that. We never changed that city — we
only ran from it. But every time I think of what Temel told us, I set
that shame down a little more. More and more people are refusing
COMPLETE, he said. We never brought the city down. But maybe, just
maybe, we cracked it open, somewhere.

I think of the Administrator sometimes. That face that used to say,
"That isn't mine to answer." I believe that even now he's holding his
ground inside that city, resisting it in his own way. Not everyone can
run. Some people stay, and open a crack from the inside. I think that,
too, is a kind of courage.

And here I am, holding this stack of paper. Years' worth of record.
Names, dates, stories still unfinished. I know this record is
different from Haven's ledger. The ledger takes in a request and
resolves it. I only listen, and write it down. I resolve nothing. But
I forget no one.

I leaf through these pages with my fingertips. The night we fled Brick
Hollow. The flowerbed at Haven. The road that led us here to the well.
The night Father first called Brother's name. I know now that every
one of those pages has been pointing, all along, toward one thing.
Listening. And not forgetting.

Someday this record, too, will grow old and faded. Its paper will
tear; its ink will fade. But I'm not afraid of that. Even if the
record disappears, the habit it made will remain. The habit of
listening. The habit of sitting beside someone. The habit of trying
not to forget. I want to pass that habit on to Brother, and someday,
to someone else again.

*I write this now, carefully, as the answer to this whole book. This
is not a ledger that stops the weeping. This is a ledger that
remembers it.*

---

Brother still meets the man at the flowerbed now and then. He still
doesn't know who he is. He still doesn't declare, "I believe." But
now, when Brother sees him, he doesn't run. I think that's enough.

Just a few days ago, Brother went and sat a long while under that
same shade tree, and came back. I didn't ask what they'd talked about.
Brother didn't say. But the face he came back with always looked a
little lighter. I thought that was enough.

Father gathered a few stones again today. He still doesn't know who
he'll give them to. But I know now. Those stones will end up in our
hands, someday. Because the love Father carries in his body has always
been turned toward us, before his memory ever was.

Every time I look at those stones, I picture the three of us here
together, now, in this place. It isn't whole. But I've stopped
waiting for wholeness. I've simply learned to be grateful that we're
here together, today.

Sometimes, at night, when sleep won't come, I think of that man at
the flowerbed. I still don't know when he appears or when he vanishes.
I don't know his name either. But that not-knowing doesn't frighten
me the way it used to. I learned it here, by this well — that there
are things you can keep close beside you, even without knowing them.

Every evening Speon lights a fire by the well. Mena spreads open the
record box beside him. Brother tends the bucket. Father chooses his
stones. I set every scene of it down on this last page. It's an
ordinary evening. But now I know how precious an ordinary evening is.

*I stop this record here, for now. It isn't the end. I will keep
writing. We will keep walking. The answers still haven't all come.
But I know this now: even when the answers don't all come, having
someone to walk with is enough to keep going.*

*I still don't know that man's name. Someday I will. Until then, I
keep writing this ledger. Our ledger.*

*(The End.)*
