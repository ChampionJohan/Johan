# Chapter 48. Our Ledger

> _Previously_ — On the day of the first snow, everyone gathered at the well. The man at the flowerbed only watched from a distance. "Not everything's fine. But right now, this moment, is good." I learned that even without everything being resolved, a moment could be wholly good.

Winter passed, and a sprout came up in the spot where I'd planted.

It was small. A little bigger than a fingernail. The same tiny size as the sprout I'd once seen in that flowerbed in Haven. I sat in front of it for a long time.

It had two leaves. I still couldn't tell what kind of leaves they were. I never did find out, to the end, what kind of seed it had been. It was one of the seeds from the pouch Jeteo had pressed into my hand back in Brick Hollow, and I don't know where Mother got it either.

Sitting there, I thought about how I'd walked past this spot all winter long. I must have passed over that frozen ground dozens of times. Nothing had ever shown. There were times I thought it might never come up at all.

*I sat a long time writing this part down. I don't want to write that this sprout is proof of anything. It might never have come up at all. Even if it hadn't, the fact that we were together that winter would still have been true, just the same. I don't want to get that order wrong.*

I knelt in front of the sprout and breathed in the smell of the soil. Wet earth, mixed with the particular smell of something newly grown. I realized I'd never smelled that in Brick Hollow. The soil there was always soil being fired into brick — never soil for growing something.

Spring wind swept across the well-yard. I could feel that the wind's smell was nothing like any season in Brick Hollow. Soil, water, and some smell I couldn't name, all mixed together. I decided to just call it "the smell of this place."

Jeteo came and sat beside it too. We stared at the little sprout together for a long time, without speaking.

"…It won't get pulled up this time, right?" Jeteo asked.

"…Right," I said. "No one stamps COMPLETE here."

Jeteo laughed at that. Then, carefully, he patted down the soil around the sprout with his fingertips.

"…Should we give it a name?" he asked.

"…A name — for a seed?" I said.

"…Why not?" Jeteo said. "We lived by our names too."

I laughed at that. We thought about it for a long while and, in the end, never landed on a name. Strangely, that felt fine too.

"…What kind of flower do you think it'll be?" I asked.

"…No idea," Jeteo said. "We'll find out when it blooms."

"…What if it doesn't bloom?"

Jeteo looked at the soil for a moment.

"…Then it won't bloom," he said. "We'll still water it, though."

I didn't say anything to that. The old Jeteo would never have answered that way. The old Jeteo always wanted to know the outcome first.

I glanced sideways at his face. He wiped his dirt-smudged fingers on his pants and looked back down at the sprout. I thought that offhand gesture held years' worth of change inside it.

Father came up behind us just then. He had a bowl of water in his hand. No one had asked him to — he'd just brought it.

"…Is this the spot?" Father asked.

"…Yes," I said.

Father knelt down and poured the water very carefully. He wetted the edge of the soil first, so the sprout wouldn't tip over. Watching his hands move, I wondered where he'd learned to do that. He hadn't learned it anywhere. Father was just that kind of person.

*I began keeping this record on the last night in Brick Hollow. I wrote, that night, that we had been wronged. I wrote that this record would be the story of that wrong. I'm reading that sentence again now.*

The girl who wrote that sentence feels like a stranger to me now. That girl was terrified. Now I'm sitting in this well-yard, warming myself in the spring sun. Same person — and yet, somehow, a completely different one.

I still turn back sometimes to the first pages of this record. The dust of Brick Hollow still seems to cling to them. The night Jeteo shouted at the sky. Mother's hand, pushing us past the boundary stones. I don't want to erase those pages. If that night hadn't happened, this place wouldn't exist either.

I look at the date written on that first page. I was thirteen then. I'm not, now. This record has grown as thick as the time I've grown, in between.

*I had no idea what this would become, when I started keeping it. I just wrote because I was afraid of forgetting. Now I know. Writing because you're afraid of forgetting — that's the whole of what this book does.*

I sometimes compare the handwriting on that first page to my handwriting now. At first it was crooked, pressed deep into the paper. Now it's a little lighter. I think even the force in my hand has loosened, with time.

Speon still keeps the well. Mena still gathers names. The two of them are together often now. Mena finally set down her pack and settled here at the well-yard for good. Her record box now fills a whole wall of Speon's cabin. I still don't know everything that happened between the two of them. But I know this much now — some stories you don't need to know the ending of, to stay close to them.

Temel set out on the road again. He's probably out there somewhere, searching for someone else. Before he left, he handed me a small notebook. "Someday," he said, "I'd like your record and mine to be bound together." I still keep that notebook empty. Noa and the children went back to their camp too. They promised, as they left, that they'd come again. I've decided not to doubt that promise.

---

We are still wrong. I still don't have a full answer for why God allows suffering. Neither does Jeteo. The man at the flowerbed never gave us an answer. He only ever held out his hand.

For a long time, that was my complaint against him. If he could give an answer, I couldn't understand why he wouldn't. Jeteo felt it worse. He hated him, for a long time, because of it.

*I'll write it plainly now. The answer we received in Haven had no flaw in it. Truly. I've read that answer over many times since, and I still can't find where it goes wrong. But after reading it, Jeteo wept harder than ever.*

*I understand the reason for that a little now. What Jeteo had asked was "why." But what Jeteo actually wanted was someone who would hear that "why" out, all the way to the end. The Ledger answered the question. No one answered the person asking it.*

I spent a long time searching for another way to explain that difference. In the end, I settled on this. A question can have a correct answer. But the person asking the question doesn't need a correct answer — they need someone beside them. I think this whole book was written for that one sentence.

Sometimes I think it's strange. We're still living without an answer, and yet it doesn't frighten us the way it used to. I can't explain the reason in any precise sentence. All I can say is, somewhere along the way, a place appeared where it was all right not to have an answer.

Father still doesn't remember every name. Some days he looks at Jeteo's face and doesn't know him. Every time it happens, Jeteo doesn't get angry — he just says his name again. As many times as it takes, without tiring.

And some days, Father wakes up and calls our names clearly. On those days, all of us look at each other without a word and smile. I've seen that smile more times than I can count. And every single time, it still feels overwhelming, like the first time.

Last month, Father went three days straight without being able to say Jeteo's name. I wrote those three days down in this record exactly as they happened. I didn't want to be a record-keeper who only wrote down the good days.

On the third night, Jeteo said this to me.

"…I'm having a hard time today."

"…Yeah."

"…But I'll tell him again tomorrow."

I think whatever lives between those two sentences is everything we learned here. Saying it's hard, and doing it again tomorrow anyway. Haven never once passed through the space between those two things. That city erased the words "it's hard" the instant they were filed.

Jeteo no longer fears those ups and downs. Even on the days Father looks at him like a stranger, Jeteo simply says his name again — like watering a seed every day. I realized that steadiness was the same thing I'd learned at the flowerbed. Waiting wasn't something that finished once. It was something you started over again, every day.

*I set that repetition down here exactly as it is. This is not a story of complete recovery. This is a story that goes on.*

I learned from Jeteo how not to grow weary of that going-on. Every morning he gives the same greeting; every evening he says the same name. I've come to understand, living here at this well-yard, that repetition like that isn't tedious. It's faithful.

I think sometimes of Brick Hollow — of Mother, who had to leave us behind, of the old man who fell, of every name we left there. I don't know if I'll ever see them again. But I know this now: I've written their names down in this record, every one. So they wouldn't be forgotten.

There are some whose names I never knew. I wrote those people down as I remember seeing them. The man who braced his knee, carrying bricks. The woman who shared her water with us. The one taskmaster, near the boundary stones, who looked the other way for us that night.

*I hesitated a long time over that taskmaster. He wasn't on our side. But that night, he looked away and let us pass. I didn't know which column to file him under. So I just wrote down what happened, exactly as it happened.*

*I think, now, that's the difference between this ledger and that one. Haven's Ledger sorts everything into its place. I write down even what I can't sort.*

I sometimes try to bring back Mother's voice. It doesn't come back whole. But the warmth of it still lives on, in one thing Father told us she said. "You're a child who keeps asking. I'm proud of that." I've already written that sentence down somewhere in this record. I've read it over many times, and it has never once gone stale.

---

Somewhere in Haven, right now, the Ledger is probably still "resolving" people's grief. Somewhere, right now, the COMPLETE stamp is probably still coming down. I never brought that city down. We didn't have the strength for that.

I've felt ashamed of that, sometimes. We didn't change that city. We only ran from it. But every time I remember what Temel said, I set that shame down a little. He said more and more people were refusing COMPLETE. We didn't tear it down. But maybe, we made a crack in it.

I don't hate the Ledger. Nowhere in this record have I written that the Ledger is evil. The Ledger did what it knew how to do, and did it flawlessly. It fed the hungry, healed the sick, gave restitution to the wronged. I don't think that's a small thing.

*But there was one column the Ledger didn't have. I searched a long time for the name of that column. Now I call it this. The column for sitting beside someone.*

*The Ledger never knew how to sit beside someone. Not because it didn't know how — because that entry never existed for it. I don't think that's the Ledger's fault. That was a person's work to do. In that city, people simply handed that work over to the Ledger instead.*

I think, sometimes, of the Administrator — that face saying, "That isn't mine to answer." I believe he's still there, even now, holding his ground in his own way. Not everyone can run. Some people stay, and make their crack from the inside. I think that takes courage too.

But here I am, holding this bundle of paper. Years' worth of a record. Names, dates, stories still unfinished. I know this record is different from Haven's Ledger. The Ledger takes things in and resolves them. I only listen, and I write it down. I resolve nothing. But I forget no one.

I turn these pages over with my fingertips. The night we fled Brick Hollow, the flowerbed in Haven, the road to the well, the night Father first said Jeteo's name. I know every one of these pages leads, in the end, to one thing. Listening. And not forgetting.

I never wrote a resolved matter into this record. There was little to write. Most of what's in here is still unfinished. People not yet found. Answers not yet heard. Memories not yet returned.

*I write that this isn't a weakness in this ledger — it's this ledger's way. A ledger that only records what's finished erases people. I record what isn't finished. That way, that person stays here still.*

Someday this record, too, will grow old and faded. The paper will tear, the writing will fade. But I'm not afraid of that. Even if the record disappears, the habit it made will remain. The habit of listening. The habit of sitting beside someone. The habit of trying not to forget. I want to hand that habit down — to Jeteo, and someday, to someone else again.

*I write down now, carefully, that this difference is the answer to this whole book. This is not a ledger that stops the weeping. This is a ledger that remembers it.*

---

Jeteo still meets the man at the flowerbed sometimes, even now. He still doesn't know who he is. He still doesn't declare "I believe." But Jeteo doesn't run from him anymore, when he sees him. I think that's enough.

Just a few days ago, Jeteo went and sat under that tree's shade for a long while before coming back. I didn't ask what they'd talked about. Jeteo didn't say. But when he came back, his face always looked a little lighter. I thought that was enough.

Once, Jeteo told me himself.

"…Today I just kept getting angry, the whole time," Jeteo said.

"…What did he say?"

"…He didn't say anything," Jeteo said. "He just listened, the whole way through. To the end."

"…He didn't get angry?"

"…No," Jeteo said. "That's the strangest part. If it were me, I'd have told someone like me to give up a long time ago."

*I've reread that passage in this ledger many times. Jeteo still doesn't say that he believes. What he says is this: "That man doesn't cut off what I'm saying." I don't ask Jeteo to say more than that. I know that's already come from very far away, for him.*

Father still gathers a few stones every day. He still doesn't know who he'll give them to. But I know this now. Those stones will end up in our hands someday. The love Father remembers with his body was always reaching toward us, long before his names came back.

Every time I see those stones, I think of the three of us, together, in this place, right now. It isn't complete. But I've learned not to wait for completeness anymore. I've learned, instead, to be grateful just for being here together, today.

Yesterday Father held out a stone to me. And asked,

"…This one's Elpis's, isn't it?"

He'd said my name. Easily, without even checking.

I couldn't say anything, standing there. Father just kept holding the stone out, not understanding why I'd gone quiet.

*I set that moment down here, near the very last page of this ledger. It's nothing at all. Just a father, calling his daughter's name. The most ordinary thing in the world.*

I took the stone and closed it in my hand. Father was already choosing another stone, moving on to the next story. I loved that offhandedness. It meant that, for Father, calling my name was no longer some special event — it was becoming ordinary, everyday life.

*I hope whoever reads this book understands how enormous that ordinary thing is. I waited years for it.*

Sometimes, when I can't sleep at night, I think of the man at the flowerbed. I still don't know when he appeared, or when he disappeared. I don't know his name either. But I'm not afraid of not knowing, the way I used to be. I learned here, at this well-yard, that some things can be kept close even unknown.

Every evening Speon lights a fire at the well. Mena spreads out her record box beside it. Jeteo tends to the buckets. Father chooses his stones. I set down this whole scene, on this last page. An ordinary evening. But I know now how precious an ordinary evening is.

There's the sound of water being drawn. The sound of wood burning. Someone calls a name, and someone else answers. That's all there is.

*Haven never had this sound. That city was quiet. For a long time I thought that quiet was peace. Now I know. It was a quiet made because no one called anyone by name.*

*I write down sound, on this last page. I write down the noisy things. That's the reason I started writing this ledger in the first place.*

I'm setting this record down now, for a while. Not the end. I'll keep writing. We'll keep walking. The answers still haven't all come. But I know this now. Even without every answer arriving, having someone to walk beside is enough to keep walking.

*I still don't know that man's name. Someday I'll learn it. Until then, I'll keep writing this ledger. Our ledger.*

*(The End.)*
