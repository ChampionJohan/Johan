# Chapter 37. Days by the Well

> _Previously_ — Feeling that what they needed was not an answer but a
> place, the siblings returned to Speon's well and settled there. Elpis
> planted the seed again, and Father said, "I know it's planted."

That was how we began to live by the well.

A low stone wall ran around the well. Grass with no name I knew grew in the
cracks between the stones. Dew gathered on its leaves every morning. I
formed a habit of touching that dew with my fingertip once, every time I
went to draw water.

There was a small hut by the well. Speon had used it for a long time. We
built a small place beside it and lived there. It was smaller than our
house in Brick Hollow. Strangely, I didn't mind the smallness at all.

We had almost no belongings. What we'd carried out of Haven, and a few
things Father owned — that was all. My brother never once complained
about how little that was. I knew that the old him would have called even
that unfair. Not anymore.

Day to day, little changed. In the morning we drew water, walked with
Father, and in the evening I set down the record. At first the sameness
felt strange to me. Haven, and the road we'd fled along — those had always
been time spent running from something.

*I learned then, for the first time, how precious an ordinary day is. I
hadn't known in Brick Hollow how rare a gift a day with nothing happening
in it could be.*

The morning routine was simple. Draw water, take Father's hand and walk
once around the well, and in the evening write a short account of what had
happened that day. At first, that writing felt like homework to me. But
after a few days, it stopped being homework and became a way of holding
on to the day.

Speon taught us the rules of the well, one at a time. Always hang the
bucket in the same place. Don't question someone newly arrived — give
them water first. I felt that these rules were nothing like the rules of
Haven. Haven's rules had been made for intake. These rules were made for
people.

Father got a little better. He still didn't know my brother's name. But
during the day he began, in small bursts, trying to do things himself.
Drawing water, moving stones. No one had to tell him to.

Father didn't sleep well at night. He woke often, and when he woke he sat
without speaking for a long while. I went and sat beside him every time.
I said nothing. We simply looked at the dark together. I thought that
was all I could do. Later I learned it wasn't just all I could do — it
was the most important thing.

"…It's easier when I'm doing something," Father said. "When I'm moving."

*I heard that and thought of the Father of Brick Hollow. Those hands had
always been moving too. I felt, in that moment, that something inside
Father was returning before his name did.*

---

My brother began helping Speon look after the well. He mended the
bucket, repaired the stone wall, and took turns with Speon keeping watch
at night.

"…Why are you helping with this?" I asked.

"…Speon's been doing this alone a long time," my brother said. "I think
I understand now. Why waiting becomes a kind of work."

*Hearing that, I knew my brother had begun to understand Speon's life. It
wasn't an understanding he'd arrived at by getting an answer. It was an
understanding he'd gotten by doing the work himself.*

My brother's hands had gone rough in those few weeks. Calluses were
starting to form where the bucket rope wore against his palms. Every time
I saw those hands I thought of his hands carrying bricks in Brick Hollow.
Back then those hands had moved to dodge a taskmaster's whip. Now these
hands moved without anyone telling them to. I kept that difference close.

While teaching my brother how to care for the bucket, Speon sometimes let
slip pieces of old stories — how he'd once been too afraid, and had fled
leaving someone behind. My brother didn't press him for more. He just
listened.

"…Why don't you ask?" Speon asked.

"…I've learned lately that it's better to talk about things when you
want to," my brother said.

Speon looked at him for a long time after that.

"…You seem like a different person than you were a few months ago,"
Speon said.

"…I feel that way sometimes too," my brother said. "But I couldn't tell
you exactly what's changed."

*I listened to that conversation beside them and looked at my brother
again. I couldn't put my finger on the exact day this had started. Some
changes come that way. Not overnight, but the way water soaks in.*

That evening, Speon taught my brother how to measure the depth of the
well — tying knots one by one into a rope, and counting how many knots
down the water began. My brother practiced it over and over until he had
it. In that repetition, I felt that something in my brother's whole
posture toward learning had changed. Before, he would have asked why he
had to learn it first. Now he just learned.

---

One night, there was a sound at the well.

It was an unusually quiet night. No wind. In that stillness, the sound
made me think, for a moment, of our last night in Brick Hollow. I
realized then that memory was still living somewhere in my body.

The sound woke Father too. He walked toward the well a little after us,
but with a steady step. Watching that step, I felt that Father no longer
ran from unfamiliar sounds.

The four of us ran toward it at the same time. A woman was trembling,
having just climbed out of the bottom of the well. Her eyes held nothing
in them.

My brother reached her first.

"…It's all right," my brother said. It was the very voice we had once
heard from Father. "Breathe slowly. You're safe here."

*Watching that, I stopped breathing for a moment. Only months ago, this
had been the place my brother feared most. Now he stood there, the
calmest person in it.*

I sat quietly beside the woman and held out the water jar. Her hands
were shaking too badly to take it. I held it for her instead and let the
water trickle in a little at a time. This was what we had done, when
Father first found us.

Speon only watched from a distance. He didn't step forward. I understood
then why he had been able to keep this well for so long. He wasn't a man
who stepped forward every time — he was a man who knew how to step
forward only when it was needed.

---

The woman stayed by the well for several days, and then remembered part
of her name. Not all of it. But she wept and laughed at once, over even
that much.

For days she barely spoke. But every morning she came out on her own to
fill her water jar. I already knew, from watching Father, that this
small movement was the beginning of recovery.

Speon called her simply "our guest," instead of a name. At first I
thought that was impolite. Later I understood. Speon was refusing to
define her by any name until she found her own.

The night before she left, she pressed a small stone into my hand. She
didn't explain why. I didn't ask and just took it. I still have that
stone.

*Before I fell asleep that night, I thought for a moment of the day we
first arrived at the well. Everything had been unfamiliar then. Now the
sound of the bucket, the sound of Speon's footsteps, had all become
familiar. I didn't know when that familiarity had begun. But that it
had begun — that much was certain.*

That night, my brother said to me:

"…I still don't know who that man is. I can't even say I believe in
God. But watching that woman today, I knew something. I know how to be
beside someone now. That much, I'm sure of."

*I set that down as the last line of this chapter. I knew that my
brother still couldn't use the word faith. But he was already doing what
faith does. I decided not to think that order was strange.*
