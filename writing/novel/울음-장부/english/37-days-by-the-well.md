# Chapter 37. Days by the Well

> _Previously_ — Sensing they needed a place more than an answer, the siblings went back to Speon's well and put down roots. Elpis planted seeds again, and Father said, "I know something's been planted."

That was how we began to live at the well.

For the first few nights, sleep wouldn't come, strangely. It took me a long time to understand why. Ever since we'd left Haven, our bodies had always tensed first, not knowing what the next day would bring. Here, our bodies still hadn't learned to believe that tomorrow wouldn't look very different from today.

A low stone wall ran around the well. Nameless weeds grew in the gaps between the stones. Every morning, dew gathered on their leaves. I got into the habit of touching that dew with a fingertip, every time I went to draw water.

There was a small hut by the well. Speon had used it for a long time. We made a small place for ourselves beside it. It was smaller than our house in Brick Hollow. Strangely, I didn't mind the smallness at all.

We had almost no belongings. Just what we'd carried out of Haven, and the few things Father had. Jeteo never once complained about how little we had. I knew that the old Jeteo would have called even that unfair. Not any more.

Among our things was the seed pouch. It was nearly empty now. I kept carrying that empty pouch instead of throwing it away. Speon once asked why I kept an empty pouch. I only smiled, not knowing how to answer. I couldn't have put it into words then — that emptiness is also the mark of something that was once full.

The days weren't very different from one another. Draw water in the morning, walk with Father, leave a record in the evening. That sameness felt strange to me at first. Haven, and the road we'd fled along — both had always been time spent being chased by something.

*That was when I first understood how precious an ordinary day was. I hadn't known, in Brick Hollow, what a rare gift a day with nothing happening in it could be.*

I gradually settled on a spot for writing my evening record — beside the hut door, where the stone wall supported my back. From that spot I could see the whole well. I liked the angle. It was a place where I'd know at once if anything happened. Lately, though, not much happened. I didn't mind that at all.

The morning routine was simple. Draw water, take Father's hand and walk once around the well, and in the evening, write a short note about the day. Writing felt like homework at first. But after a few days, it stopped being homework and became a way of holding on to the day.

Speon taught us the well's rules, one at a time. Always hang the bucket in the same spot. Give water to anyone newly out before you ask them anything. I could feel that these rules were nothing like Haven's. Haven's rules existed for processing requests. These rules existed for people.

"…How many rules are there?" I asked.

"…Never counted," Speon said. "One got added whenever it was needed. There weren't any at first."

"…Which one came first?"

Speon paused, hand still, and thought.

"…Not asking," Speon said. "Not asking why someone came out of the well, or what they'd lost before. You wait until they say it themselves. That one came first, for me."

Hearing that, I remembered how the intake window in Haven had always asked first. What is your name. What is the issue. I felt that difference clearly, for the first time, right then.

"…So what do you do with your curiosity?" I asked. "Just hold it in?"

"…Draw them another cup of water," Speon said. "As much as you're curious."

I thought about that for a long time. A person who turned curiosity into water instead of questions. From that day on, I got into the habit of filling a water jar first, whenever someone came out of the well. And strangely, that was how — later — the person would come to speak first.

Father improved little by little. He still didn't know Jeteo's name. But during the day, he began trying to do things on his own for a few minutes at a time — drawing water, moving a stone. No one had to ask him.

Father seemed to especially like drawing water. That repetition of lowering and pulling the bucket seemed to steady him. I saw him a few times just standing there, gripping the bucket rope, still. Even when he wasn't moving, he never let go of that rope.

Father slept badly at night. He woke often, and when he did, he sat for a long while without speaking. Every time, I went and sat beside him. I said nothing. We just watched the dark together. I thought that was all I could do. I learned later that it wasn't just all I could do — it was the most important thing.

"…It's easier when I'm doing something," Father said. "When my body moves."

"…What are you most at ease doing?" I asked.

Father thought about the question a moment, rubbing the dirt floor with his fingertips.

"…Moving stones," Father said. "I don't know why. I just like having something in my hands."

Hearing that, I thought of the bricks of Brick Hollow. Even then, Father's hands had always been holding something. Something heavy. Something hard. It struck me, strangely painfully, that the habit had stayed with him this way.

*Hearing that, I thought of Father in Brick Hollow. Those hands had always been moving too. I felt, then, that something inside Father was coming back before his name did.*

---

Jeteo began helping Speon look after the well. He mended the bucket, tended the stone wall, and at night took turns keeping watch with Speon.

"…Why are you helping with this?" I asked.

"…Speon did this alone for so long," Jeteo said. "I think I understand it now. Why waiting becomes a kind of work."

"…What do you mean?" I asked.

"…I used to think waiting was doing nothing," Jeteo said. "But mend a bucket every day and you find out how much work goes into it. Waiting was like that too. Work that takes a lot of hands."

*Hearing that, I understood that Jeteo had begun to understand Speon's life. It wasn't an understanding he'd arrived at from an answer. It was an understanding earned by doing it alongside him.*

Jeteo's hands had grown much rougher in those few weeks. Calluses were starting to form where the bucket rope rubbed. Every time I saw those hands, I remembered his hands carrying bricks in Brick Hollow. Back then those hands had moved to dodge a taskmaster's rod. Now these hands moved with no one making them. I kept that difference close to my heart.

As Speon taught Jeteo how to mend the bucket, he let a little of his old story slip out now and then — how he'd once been so afraid that he'd fled and left someone behind. Jeteo didn't press him for more. He just listened.

"…Why don't you ask?" Speon asked.

"…I've learned lately that it's better for people to talk when they want to," Jeteo said.

Speon looked at him a long time at that.

"…When you first came here, you did press me," Speon said. "Asked why I'd waste my time keeping some well out here like this."

Jeteo looked a little embarrassed at that.

"…Sorry, about back then," Jeteo said.

"…Nothing to be sorry for," Speon said. "That question made me think it over again too. Why I'm here."

"…Did you find an answer?"

"…Still looking," Speon said, smiling. "But I'm not in a hurry any more."

"…What happens to something you're not in a hurry about?" Jeteo asked.

Speon thought a moment, winding the bucket rope.

"…You just have to go through a few days where the answer doesn't come and the day passes anyway," Speon said. "Then you find out. That the answer was never the thing holding your day up."

Jeteo didn't answer that. But I saw his face take on the look of someone filing a thing away rather than discarding it.

"…You seem like a different person than a few months ago," Speon said.

"…I feel that way sometimes too," Jeteo said. "But I can't really explain what's changed."

*Listening to that conversation from the side, I looked at Jeteo again. I couldn't point to an exact day when this had started in him. Some change comes that way — not overnight, but seeping in like water.*

That evening, Speon taught Jeteo how to measure the depth of the well — tying a knot in the rope every so often and counting which knot the water reached. Jeteo practiced it over and over until he had it. In that repetition, I felt that the very way Jeteo learned things had changed. He used to ask first why he had to learn something. Now he simply learned.

"…Why measure this depth?" Jeteo asked, counting the knots one by one.

"…You need to know when a drought comes," Speon said. "If you know the water's dropping ahead of time, you can warn people ahead of time."

"…It's not dropping now, though."

"…No," Speon said. "But you measure every day. If you only measure on the day it drops, it's already too late."

Jeteo's hand stopped at that. I saw something pass over his face. It was the exact opposite of Haven's logic — a city that only ever took in a request after the problem had already happened. Here, there was someone keeping watch before the problem ever came.

---

One night, there was a sound of movement at the well.

It was an unusually quiet night. No wind. In that stillness, hearing that sound, I suddenly thought of our last night in Brick Hollow. I realized, in that instant, that the memory was still lodged somewhere in my body.

Moonlight lay scattered in pieces on the water. I watched that trembling light and, for a moment, held my breath. From a well that had made not a single sound of water, a splashing sound suddenly rose. I strained my ears not to lose it.

Father woke to the sound too. He walked to the well a little later than we did, but with a steady stride. Watching that walk, I felt that Father no longer fled from unfamiliar sounds.

The four of us ran there together. A woman was crouched trembling, just out of the bottom of the well. Her eyes held nothing in them.

Jeteo went to her first.

"…It's all right," Jeteo said. It was the very voice we had heard from Father. "Breathe slowly. You're safe here."

*Watching that scene, my breath caught. A few months ago, Jeteo had stood in the exact spot he was most afraid of. Now he stood there most calmly of all.*

I sat quietly beside the woman and held out a water jar. Her hands shook too badly to take it. I took her hand instead and let the water trickle in, little by little. That was exactly what we had done for Father, when we first met him.

Speon only watched from a distance. He didn't step in. I felt, then, that I understood how he'd been able to keep this well for so long. He wasn't a man who stepped in every time — he was a man who knew when to step in only when it was needed.

Father stood a little apart too. Neither stepping in nor stepping back. I felt that Father was studying the woman's trembling shoulders closely. He murmured something, low.

"…Was I like that too?" Father asked me.

For a moment, the question stopped my words.

"…Yes," I finally answered. "But now you can ask a question like that."

Father nodded at that. And looked at the woman again. It was the first time I'd seen him recognize himself in a stranger.

After a long while, Father lifted one of the water jars and pushed it a little toward the woman. He said nothing. He simply set it within reach of her hand and stepped back.

*Watching that small gesture, my throat went hot. Father still didn't know who he was. But who he had once been — his hands remembered that first.*

---

The woman stayed by the well for a few days, and then remembered part of her name. Not the whole thing. But even that much was enough to make her laugh through tears.

The day the woman first spoke a fragment of her name aloud, I happened to be carrying a water jar. I stopped in my tracks at the sound. "…Mi…" the woman said. That was all. But she held on to that one syllable and wept for a long time. I couldn't tell if that weeping was sorrow or joy. Probably both.

The woman barely spoke for days. But every morning, she came out on her own to fill her water jar. I had already learned from watching Father that this small movement was the beginning of recovery.

Speon didn't call her by a name. He just called her "the guest." At first I thought that was rude. Later I understood. Speon was refusing to define her by any name at all until she found her own.

The day before she left, she pressed a small stone into my hand. She didn't explain why. I didn't ask, and took it. I still have that stone.

"…When I remember my name, all of it, I'll come back," the woman said. Her voice still trembled a little. But it was a different trembling than the one she'd arrived with.

"…Come back whenever you're ready," I said. "This place isn't going anywhere."

The woman laughed at that. A laugh I hadn't seen before. Short, but clear.

The morning she left, Speon carved a small mark into the well's stone wall. I asked what it was for.

"…It's a count of everyone who's passed through," Speon said. "Since we can't write their names, we mark it, at least."

I looked at that stone wall closely for the first time. There were a great many marks. I began thinking that every one of those marks was someone's day, and then stopped counting partway through.

*Before I fell asleep that night, I thought for a moment of the day we'd first arrived at the well. Everything had been unfamiliar then. Now the sound of the bucket, the sound of Speon's footsteps — all of it sounded familiar. I didn't know when that familiarity had come. But that it had come — that much was certain.*

That night, Jeteo said to me,

"…I still don't know who that man is. I can't say I believe in God either. But watching that woman today, I knew something. I know how to sit beside someone now. That much is certain."

"…When did you learn that?" I asked.

"…I don't know," Jeteo said. "I just came to and realized I already knew. No one taught me."

"…Maybe you learned it from Father," I said. "Just being beside us."

Jeteo was silent at that for a moment.

"…Maybe so," Jeteo finally said.

*I set down those words as the last line of this chapter. I knew that Jeteo still couldn't use the word faith. But he was already doing the work faith does. I decided not to think that order strange.*
