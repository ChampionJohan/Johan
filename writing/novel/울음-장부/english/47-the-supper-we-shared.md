# Chapter 47. The Supper We Shared

> _Previously_ — Father, unable to explain why, took off his coat and wrapped it around Elpis. That night, he asked to hear stories of his own past, and listening to what the siblings told him, said, "What a good person he was, that man."

On the day of the first snow, Mena came again. This time, she wasn't alone.

The snow fell without a sound. I was watching it pile white on the stone wall around the well when I heard several voices, far off. At first I thought it was the wind.

It almost never snowed in Brick Hollow. When it did, the moment it touched the ground it mixed with dirt and turned gray. I saw snow pile up white for the first time here, at this well.

There had been no snow at all in Haven. That city's sky was always the same brightness. I'd thought, back then, that this was convenient. Now I'm not even sure that city had a sky at all.

*I've marked that day, in this ledger, as the loudest day in it. I wondered if it was strange to write down a loud day in a weeping ledger. But looking back on it now, this is the most important chapter in the whole book.*

Noa and the children had come too. They said they'd heard the news and walked a long way.

The children ran around screaming as if seeing snow for the first time. Listening to that laughter fill the well-yard, my throat tightened, strangely.

One child balled up snow and threw it at Jeteo. He didn't dodge — he just took the hit. Then he balled up his own snow and threw it back. In an instant, a snowball fight had broken out. Watching that, I tried to work out how long it had been since I'd seen Jeteo laugh like that.

I gave up counting. It had been too long.

Father stood right in the middle of that chaos too. The children threw snow at Father too. He didn't know what to do at first, then clumsily scooped up a handful and threw it. It didn't hit anyone. The children laughed even harder at that.

Standing there in that laughter, I wondered if the three of us had ever laughed like this, for no reason at all, since leaving Brick Hollow. We hadn't. We'd always been walking toward something.

*It felt strange to see so many faces gathered in one place. A good kind of strange.*

I took in each face, one by one. Noa, the children, and Father. I never could have imagined, back in Brick Hollow, that all these people would gather in one place. I kept finding it hard to believe that this unimaginable thing was actually happening now.

Temel arrived the next day too, however he'd heard the news. He said it was the first time this many people had gathered at Speon's well.

"…Was this place always this quiet?" I asked.

"…Yeah," Speon said, laughing. "Not any more, though."

There wasn't the slightest hint of annoyance on Speon's face. If anything, he looked like he was enjoying the noise. I found that unfamiliar, and welcome.

Mena came and linked her arm through Speon's. It was a natural gesture. I tried to guess how much time had built up inside that naturalness.

"…I'm thinking about settling down here for good now," Mena said.

Speon's eyes went wide at that.

"…Really?"

"…Yeah," Mena said, smiling. "I'm tired of walking around everywhere. And here — I think I have a place of my own here now too."

I listened to that conversation from the side and laughed to myself.

Speon couldn't say anything, and just kept fidgeting with the bucket rope. I thought he was working up to some grand statement. In the end, what he said was this:

"…The water's cold in winter. You have to be careful."

Mena burst out laughing.

"…That's your answer?"

"…Yeah," Speon said. "That's the most important thing, if you're going to live here."

*I copy that answer down exactly, into this ledger. I think that was the most affectionate thing Speon knew how to say. This was a man who told you how to get through the winter instead of saying he loved you.*

I told Jeteo about that exchange word for word. He laughed for a long while, then said, "…I bet I'll say something like that too, someday." I found it strange, hearing that, that Jeteo had somehow come to understand love that way.

---

We lit a fire at the well and all sat around it together. Speon laughed loudly, for the first time in a long while. The children ran through the snow, shrieking.

Two pots hung over the fire. Barley in one, dried vegetables and beans in the other. Noa added in a little of the salt she'd brought. There weren't enough bowls, so the children shared, two to a bowl.

I can't say that supper was especially delicious. The barley was undercooked and the soup was bland. But it's the supper I still think of most often, even now.

*In Haven, this supper would have been fixed the moment it was filed. Not enough bowls, improper cooking time, insufficient salt. All of it resolved within minutes. And no one would have remembered that day at all.*

*I know the difference now. When you share something insufficient, it becomes an event. Something adequately filled becomes no event at all.*

Mena took out her record box again and showed it around. Noa and Temel flipped through the papers together, searching for names they knew. Watching that, I realized this well-yard had somehow become a place where records gathered.

Some of the papers had names half worn away; some had a blank space where a name should be. Mena didn't throw those blank ones away either — she kept them as they were.

"…Why don't you get rid of these?" I asked.

"…Not knowing a name is a record too," Mena said. "It means someone was there, and we just never learned who. That needs to be kept."

I thought about that for a long time. Haven's Ledger had no blank spaces. An unknown entry was never even created in the first place.

*I hold that as my own standard, even now, writing this ledger. What I don't know, I write down as not known. I don't fill a blank space with something plausible-sounding.*

I looked for a long time at those blank name-fields. Those blanks didn't make me sad for being empty — the very fact that such a space existed at all was, itself, a comfort. In Haven, a field like that would never even have been made.

Jeteo was sitting among the children, showing them how to roast potatoes in the fire. The children laughed with delight at every word he said. I thought that Jeteo was giving those children something now, the way he had once given it to us.

One child asked Jeteo why the potatoes had to be buried in the ashes. Jeteo answered patiently. The child asked again, why that kept them from burning. Jeteo answered that too. When the child asked a third time, Jeteo laughed.

"…You're just like me," Jeteo said.

I laughed to myself at that. Jeteo no longer got angry at a child who kept asking questions. He welcomed it now.

Noa spoke quietly to Father.

"…Elpis and Jeteo talked about you so much."

Father looked toward us at that. There was moisture in his eyes.

"…I'm starting to remember bits of it too," Father said. "Not all of it, but."

Noa quietly took Father's hand at that.

"…It's all right if it doesn't all come back," Noa said. "What matters is that you're here now."

Father nodded at that. His eyes were damp.

"…You were one of the people who found me too, weren't you?" Father asked.

"…That's right," Noa said. "The very first."

Father took Noa's hand in both of his at that.

"…Thank you," Father said. "For not giving up on me, that day."

Noa shook her head.

"…There was no reason to give up," Noa said. "That's just what we do. We keep looking until we find them."

"…And if you don't find them?" Father asked.

Noa looked at the fire for a moment.

"…There are plenty of people like that too," Noa said. "Then we remember that person's name instead. That's the last thing we can do."

Father lowered his head at that. I think, in that moment, Father realized he had come close to being on that very list himself.

Noa smiled at that, holding back tears.

---

As the meal was winding down, I happened to look toward the tree's shade.

He was standing there. Quietly, though no one had called him.

Snow was gathering little by little on his shoulders too. I thought that sight would stay with me a long time, strangely.

He didn't brush the snow off. He didn't look cold either. I found myself, for the first time, not wondering why he never came near the fire. I thought it was fine to have at least one person you didn't need a reason for.

I waved my hand. He gave a small nod. And this time, he only watched from a distance. He didn't come closer.

*I knew, then. He was always with us, and yet he never once took our place. This evening had to be our evening. He knew that before we did.*

Jeteo saw him too. This time, he didn't run over, and he didn't argue with him either. He just raised a hand a little, in greeting. He nodded back at that greeting.

*I remember that short greeting for a long time. I saw, that day, for the first time, that there can be a relationship that needs no words at all.*

Speon spotted him too and quietly raised a hand. So did Mena, and Temel. I felt that each of those greetings carried a different weight. Each of them had come to know him on their own time.

Noa alone tried to get up from her seat. But Jeteo quietly caught her arm.

"…Just leave him be," Jeteo said. "I think he's more comfortable that way."

Noa sat back down at that. I felt that the way Jeteo understood that man now went deeper than any of us.

I looked toward the tree's shade again. He was still standing there, watching us, snow falling on him. I thought that sight didn't look lonely at all. If anything, it looked the most at ease of anyone there.

I tried to hold that sight in my eyes for a long time. I felt that a day would come when I'd want to remember this scene again.

Father looked that way too. He looked for a long time.

"…Who is that?" Father asked.

I didn't know what to answer. Neither did Jeteo.

"…I don't know," Jeteo finally said. "But he's been in that spot every time things were hardest for us."

Father nodded at that. He didn't ask further.

*I've reread that answer many times in this ledger. Jeteo never did manage to explain him, to the end. Neither can I. That was all either of us could say. That he was there. That he was there, every single time.*

---

As the night deepened, people fell asleep one by one. The children curled up together under blankets. Mena leaned against Speon's shoulder, eyes closed. Noa and Temel were talking in low voices by the fire.

Father had fallen asleep leaning against the wall. A stone was still held in his hand. I reached to take it out, then stopped myself.

I sat side by side with Jeteo, watching the fire.

Embers crackled and popped. Listening to that sound, I slowly went back over the day. Snow, laughter, familiar faces. All of it felt like a dream.

"…Are you happy?" Jeteo asked.

I thought about that question for a long time.

"…Not everything's fine," I said. "Father still doesn't remember everything, Haven's still out there, our names still aren't fully known."

"…But?"

"…But right now, this moment, is good," I said.

"…Me too," Jeteo said. "I used to think everything had to be resolved before I could be happy. Turns out that's not true."

"…When did your thinking start to change?" I asked.

Jeteo thought for a moment before answering.

"…I don't know," Jeteo said. "Just, at some point, I started to feel like having someone beside me was enough."

"…Are you still angry?" I asked.

"…Yeah," Jeteo said. Without hesitation. "No one's giving Father back the years he lost. And I never got to see Mother again either."

I liked that answer. Even on a day like this, Jeteo didn't lie.

"…But tonight, that anger's the furthest back," Jeteo said. "Not gone. Just, tonight, it's furthest back."

*I think that sentence is the most honest sentence in this whole book. Jeteo didn't say his anger had disappeared. He said it was furthest back. I think that's how people endure.*

I thought, in that moment, of the COMPLETE stamp the Ledger of Haven had always issued. That stamp always erased a problem so it could never come forward again. Jeteo's anger hadn't been erased. It had simply moved its place. I thought that difference was one of the most important differences in this whole book.

I leaned my head on Jeteo's shoulder at that. He didn't push me away either.

The embers dimmed slowly. But it didn't feel cold.

Noa and Temel's low voices went on. The two of them were exchanging certain names back and forth. I couldn't make out all those names. I didn't need to.

*I set that sound down as the one I like best in this whole book. The sound of people's names passed back and forth in low voices. It resolves nothing at all. But in a place where that sound is heard, no one disappears.*

I listened to that sound and closed my eyes. Names scattered into the dark, one by one, and were born again from someone's mouth. I thought that repetition was the whole work of this well-yard.

"…Will everyone still be here tomorrow?" I asked.

"…No, everyone will go back to their own place," Jeteo said. "But it's all right. We'll meet again."

I heard that and looked up at the sky. The snow had stopped, and stars were coming out one by one. I started to count them, then gave up. I liked just looking at them better than counting.

I looked once more toward the tree's shade. He was already gone. I didn't check whether footprints remained in the snow. I think, maybe, I didn't want to check.

I sat there a little longer. Jeteo's breathing slowly grew even. I sat by the fire a while longer even after I was sure he was asleep. I wanted to hold on to that night a little longer.

*I set down those words as the last line of this chapter. That night, for the first time, I learned that even without everything being resolved, this present moment could be wholly good.*
