# Chapter 26. The Man Who Finds People

> _Previously_ — The brother and sister found a stranger's footprints. A traveler confirmed that the man had been walking along counting numbers, and told them the name of someone who finds people: "Temel."

Two days later, we really did meet Temel.

He wasn't a big man. He carried his pack light. His eyes never stopped moving over his surroundings.

His pack was far more modest than ours. That struck me as odd. I'd expected someone who traveled the country finding people to carry more, not less — but he looked instead like a man who carried only what he strictly needed. I didn't know the reason then.

His walk was unusual. Not hurried, not slow. It was the walk of someone who already knew exactly where he was going. Watching it put me strangely at ease. I felt certain this man wouldn't get lost.

"…Are you the one who finds people?" my brother asked.

Temel stopped and looked at us. For just a moment, something flickered in his eyes.

"…Something like that," Temel said. "Who are you looking for?"

*In that moment I felt something strange in Temel's eyes. The look of someone who had repeated the same work for a very long time. Not tired — worn familiar.*

His hands drew my attention too. Dirt was packed into his fingernails in layer upon layer — not dirt that would wash off in one washing. They were the hands of someone who had walked a long way.

There were small scars scattered across the backs of his hands as well, like marks from pushing through thornbrush. Looking at them, I understood that for this man, finding people wasn't simply walking a road — it was a thing his body took blows for.

"…Don't your hands hurt?" I asked without thinking.

Temel looked down at his own hands.

"…They hurt when they hurt," he said. "But this isn't pain. This is just old."

That distinction stayed with me. I'd never before met someone who separated *old* from *painful* like that.

"…Our father," my brother said, his voice trembling slightly. "He walks counting numbers. He's forgotten his name too."

Temel closed his eyes briefly at that, as if remembering something.

"…Finding someone like that can take a long time," Temel said. "Are you all right with that?"

"…Yes," my brother said immediately. No hesitation at all.

"…We met Speon and Mena," my brother added. "They didn't mention you, but we found our way to you on our own."

Something shifted, faintly, in Temel's expression at those two names.

"…Are they well?" Temel asked. His voice held both gladness and caution at once.

"…Yes," I said. "Each of them is doing well, where they are."

Temel nodded and didn't ask further. In that silence, I felt there was an old story between the three of them that we knew nothing about.

A faint shadow crossed Temel's eyes for a moment. I couldn't tell if it was grief or longing. Maybe both. What it might feel like to hear news of people you hadn't seen in a long time, delivered by two strange children's mouths — I couldn't have imagined it, then.

"…Does Speon still keep the well?" Temel asked suddenly.

"…Yes," I said. "People keep coming to it, I hear."

Temel smiled faintly at that. It looked like a smile he hadn't worn in a long time.

"…That's him," Temel said. And didn't go on.

He gazed off into the distance for a moment. I felt that his eyes weren't turned toward Speon's well, but toward somewhere much further away. Probably toward some era we didn't yet know.

"…And Mena?" I asked carefully. "I heard she became a Recorder."

Temel's eyes softened at that name too.

"…She's probably doing it well, being Mena," Temel said. "She was always careful about everything. The one of the three of us who forgot the least."

*Listening to that, I thought of the writing tool Mena had given me. It was still in my hand even then. That was the first time I truly felt how far the things these three people had left with each other still reached.*

---

My brother told Temel about Father. The brick-counting habit, the footprints, the numbers.

Temel listened and nodded.

"…I've seen people like that a few times," Temel said. "People who've lost their names usually repeat something with their hands or their mouths. Something they used to do stays in the body."

"…Why is that?" I asked.

"…I don't know exactly either," Temel said. "But the body seems to remember longer than the mind. The mind loses hold of a name. The hands keep counting bricks."

"…Then can you tell what someone used to do, just by looking at their body?" my brother asked, leaning forward slightly.

"…To some degree," Temel said. "If there's a callus on the hand, it's someone who held something for a long time. If the walk leans to one side, it's someone who carried something for a long time. The body doesn't lie much."

My brother glanced down at his own palm. There was still a callus there from Brick Hollow. I saw him slide that hand quickly into his pocket.

"…Can you tell about us too?" my brother asked.

"…I can. A lot," Temel said. "Both of you have hands that carried something for a long time."

*Hearing that, I thought of Father's hands. I tried to imagine what they might be doing right now. Just imagining it hurt.*

"…If he finds his name," I asked, "does that mean he'll go back to how he was?"

Temel shook his head.

"…Not always," Temel said. "Even finding a name doesn't mean going back to exactly who you were before. But it's better than not having one."

*That answer didn't comfort me much. But strangely, I was grateful for the honesty. Temel didn't give us false hope.*

"…Then why do you keep going out to find people?" I asked. "You just said they don't come back exactly the same."

Temel stopped walking for a moment and looked at me.

"…Even if they don't come back exactly the same, it's still better than not looking," Temel said. "Finding someone's name doesn't bring everything back about them. But at least, you don't leave them wandering alone."

Hearing that, something in me ached. Temel wasn't promising a perfect outcome, and yet he had a reason not to give up. I didn't understand, then, how that was possible.

"…So can you find him?" my brother asked urgently.

"…I'm someone who finds people," Temel said. "But I'm not someone who carries answers around. I'm someone who goes looking."

*I didn't really understand the difference in those words at the time. I do now. Temel wasn't someone who gave you certainty. He was someone who walked alongside you.*

"…What if we find him and he's not Father?" I asked. It frightened me to ask, but it had to be asked.

My brother turned to look at me. His face said it was unexpected, that I'd been the one to raise it. I didn't look away from him. I thought someone had to name that possibility out loud.

"…Then that person still deserves to be found, on their own terms," Temel said. "There's no wasted step in the search. Whoever you're looking for, the walking still means something."

"…Has that happened to you? Finding someone and it wasn't the person you wanted?" my brother asked.

Temel nodded.

"…It has," Temel said shortly. He said nothing further. I felt a long story hiding inside that shortness.

*That answer comforted me in a strange way. For the first time, I understood that this journey might not be a complete failure even if we never found Father.*

"…What happened to that person, then?" I asked again. "The one you found who wasn't who you wanted."

Temel stopped and looked up at the sky for a moment.

"…That person was also looking for their own family," Temel said. "So I helped find them too. In the end, we found them. I stood beside them and watched that reunion. It wasn't the person I'd been looking for. But I cried, that day."

*Hearing that story, my chest filled strangely full. For the first time, I sensed, dimly, that searching might be something larger than simply getting what you'd hoped for.*

My brother didn't answer that. But I saw his expression loosen a little. He had always measured a journey only by its outcome. That day, I felt something like a different standard beginning to grow in him.

---

"…Why do you do this work?" I asked.

Temel looked off into the distance for a moment.

"…A long time ago, I ran from a place where I was supposed to be guiding someone," Temel said. "I was afraid, back then. Now I'm still afraid sometimes. But I don't run anymore. That's the only way I know how to pay it back."

*Hearing that, I thought it sounded like something Speon had once said. I didn't know it then — that these two had truly come out of the same story.*

"…Where did you run from?" my brother asked, his voice careful.

Temel hesitated before answering.

"…I had to guide some people across a certain place," Temel said. "But I got scared and stopped partway. Some of them made it across without me. Some didn't."

Temel's voice cracked slightly there. I didn't press further. Some stories, I learned in that silence, it's a courtesy not to hear to the end.

"…Weren't you scared? The moment you wanted to run again?" I asked.

"…Every time," Temel admitted honestly. "Even now, sometimes. But if you run because you're afraid, you find yourself running from then on. So I just walk, still afraid."

*I held onto that sentence for a long time afterward. I didn't fully understand its weight then. I do now. It was a sentence we would need to learn again and again for the rest of this journey.*

"…Can't you wait until you're not afraid anymore?" I asked.

Temel laughed shortly at the question.

"…I tried waiting like that too," Temel said. "But the day of not-being-afraid never came. So I decided to just start while still afraid. It was the only way there was."

My brother listened to this quietly. I could feel his eyes turning something over. He, too, had always been someone who waited for certainty before he acted.

A gust of wind blew hard, once. Temel shouldered his pack and started walking again. I noticed that he didn't stop walking even right after saying he was afraid. I'd never before met someone whose words and steps stayed that closely joined.

"…Do you still see that person now?" I asked.

Temel laughed briefly. There was a trace of loneliness in the laugh.

"…Sometimes," Temel said. "Not as often as you'd think. But every time I do, it feels exactly the same as before."

---

Temel led us north for several more days, watching the ground the whole way — the direction dry grass had been trampled, scratches on stones, even the depth of footprints.

Temel's own steps looked far more at ease than ours. But watch closely, and his eyes never rested for a moment. Following his pace, I felt for the first time how exhausting searching really was. It wore out the eyes before it wore out the body.

On the second day, Temel suddenly stopped and crouched to the ground. We stopped too. He studied one very small mark in the dirt for a long time, then finally shook his head and stood back up.

"…Not it," Temel said briefly.

I understood, only then, that this small disappointment was something Temel lived through several times a day. A searcher's day was mostly filled with confirming *not this one*.

At night, Temel always kept the fire small. "Too big and you can't find anyone," he said. "Someone searching shouldn't stand too bright. The one you're looking for won't be able to see you." I understood that and didn't understand it, both at once.

I sat by that small fire that night and tried to measure with my eyes how narrow its reach was. Only as far as our three sets of knees. Beyond that, all darkness.

*I understood, then. A person who searches doesn't light up the whole world. They light only the ground at their own feet, and keep walking by that light.*

Through those four days, my brother slept fitfully every night. I heard him turning and turned restlessly too, alongside him. Temel never tried to stop us. He simply, wordlessly, packed up each morning and walked on.

On the second night, half-asleep, I heard Temel humming low to himself. It wasn't really a song, more like breath given shape. I listened without opening my eyes. There was, strangely, an old grief in that sound, and beneath it, some rhythm that refused to stop.

On the third morning I saw Temel's shoes. As worn as ours. Layers of patched sole overlapped several times. I thought those shoes showed, plainly, all the time this man had walked.

On the third day, the path narrowed and trees began to appear here and there. Temel's steps slowed bit by bit — steps that were watching something more carefully.

"…We're getting close," Temel said. Not a voice full of certainty. But my heart began pounding at those words anyway.

"…Found it," Temel said quietly on the fourth night. Far off, a small light was visible.

That light was very small. So small that I checked, several times, whether it was really fire or only something my eyes had made up. A single small light had never felt so enormous.

Temel pointed at the light and lowered his voice.

"…Don't rush," Temel said. "Confirm first, then approach. If gladness gets ahead of you, you could end up hurt."

At that, I let some of the strength go out of my legs. I'd wanted nothing but to run. My brother looked the same. His hand was gripping mine hard enough to hurt.

"…This is the closest we've ever gotten," my brother said quietly. His voice shook. "I… I don't know what to do."

"…Me neither," I said. We stood there and listened to each other's breathing for a moment.

*The moment I saw that light, I set down this chapter's last line here. I gripped my brother's hand tight again. I couldn't know whether that light was Father or not. But in that moment, for the first time, I felt with my whole body that you could walk forward without knowing the answer.*
