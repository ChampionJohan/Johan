# Chapter 40. Going to Him First

> _Previously_ — A nameless boy newly came out at the well. Jeteo passed on to him, exactly as he'd received it, how to sit beside someone. And realized for himself that he was carrying out the words he'd learned from the man at the flowerbed in his body, without ever having memorized them.

The man appeared and vanished with no fixed time.

For the first few days, I tried marking the days he came on a calendar. But I soon gave up. I couldn't find any pattern at all. And yet, he had never once come on a day he wasn't needed.

Some days he'd be touching the soil by the well. Some days he'd sit quietly beside someone newly out of it. No one ever knew when he'd come.

Some days I only realized afterward that he'd been there. The bucket sitting a little differently than usual, a stone the boy had been playing with moved to another spot. Every time I saw those small traces, I knew he'd passed through. He never left much of a mark of himself.

His clothes were always similar — plain, the kind you'd see anywhere. At first I thought that was strange. Later, I came to think it suited him instead. A man who didn't want to stand out.

His shoes were always worn too. I couldn't tell if that wear came from long use or from long walking. Probably both. Every time I looked at those shoes, I felt that wherever he came from, he was never a being who simply stood still.

I'd once tried to trace back the places he appeared. When someone newly out couldn't lift a water jar, when the boy woke alone at night crying, when Speon, rarely, looked worn out. He'd be standing quietly in those spots. I thought that couldn't be coincidence. But he never made a show of having come knowing in advance, either.

*That was when I began to notice that irregularity was, itself, a kind of rule. He was always there, at the moment he was most needed.*

I'd once even tried to work out a relationship between when he came and the weather. Did he come more often on rainy days, less on clear ones? In the end I found no correlation at all. Strangely, I liked even that failed observation. Some beings couldn't be caught by calculation.

Speon, and Mena too, never asked him certain things. When he'd come. Why now. I came to understand, living at the well, that this silence wasn't resignation. It was a kind of ease that only someone who had watched a long time could have.

I envied that ease at first. But living there, I came to understand it wasn't a natural-born ease. Speon must have been impatient too, at first, like me. I came to understand, little by little, at the well, that this ease had been made by time.

I once asked Speon about it.

The sound of him mending the bucket creaked a few times in the pause. I asked, carefully, with that as my backdrop.

"…Where does that man stay?"

Speon answered without stopping his work.

"…I don't know either," Speon said. "Haven't known for decades. But I've never once felt shorted. He came when he was needed."

"…Have you never once wondered?"

"…I've wondered," Speon said. "But at some point, that wondering stopped turning into fretting. There are things you can leave as just wondering."

"…Elpis, aren't you curious too?" Speon asked.

"…Yes," I said. "But lately that curiosity doesn't scare me."

"…Why?"

"…I learned it's all right to be without knowing," I said.

"…Where'd you learn that?" Speon asked.

"…From a seed," I said. "You don't know when a seed will sprout. But you keep giving it water anyway. I know now that that's what faith is."

Speon looked at me a long time at that. His face had something startled in it.

"…How old did you say you were?" Speon asked.

"…Thirteen," I said, laughing.

"…Sometimes you seem like you've lived longer than me," Speon said.

"…But I get impatient too, sometimes," I said. "Not every day. Some nights."

"…What do you do then?" Speon asked.

"…I water something," I said. "Even though you're not supposed to water at night. I just need to do something, is all."

Speon didn't laugh at that. Instead he nodded slowly.

"…I wipe down the bucket at night too, for no reason," Speon said. "Even though I already wiped it down during the day."

I held on to that for a long time. I didn't know, then, that it would someday reach Jeteo too.

The boy — the nameless one too — flinched the first time he saw the man. I understood that reaction. A body tenses first when a stranger suddenly appears. But after a few days, the boy grew easy with him too. Whenever the man appeared, the boy would slip over and sit beside him. He said nothing. Just sat there. Watching that, I understood that the boy was already learning how to be beside someone without speaking to them.

The man never tried to teach the boy anything. He simply touched the dirt alongside him when the boy fiddled with it. When the boy stacked stones, he watched without knocking them down. In that, I felt something like what Father had shown, the first time he came to us.

Once, a stone tower the boy was building collapsed. He looked like he was about to burst into tears right there. The man, beside him, picked the stones up one by one without a word and handed them back. The boy took them and started stacking again. Watching that scene from a distance, I understood that this was always how he rebuilt what had fallen. Not building it back up for you — handing you what you needed to build it back up yourself.

That evening the boy came and asked me,

"…Why doesn't that man help? It'd be faster if he just built it for me."

I couldn't answer that question right away. In Haven, the answer would have come instantly. File a request, and the tower would have arrived already built.

"…I guess fast isn't always best," I managed to say.

The boy tilted his head. But the next day, too, he stacked that tower alone. And the day he finished it, without anyone telling him to, he called me over to show me. I knew, then, that a tower built for you has no one to call.

I thought back to when I'd first met him. In an alley in Haven, he'd seemed like just someone passing by. I hadn't known, then, that he'd be a person who stayed with us this long. Now, on a day he didn't come, it left a strange emptiness. I didn't know exactly when that emptiness had started.

I wrote that emptiness into my evening record once. "That man didn't come today. The day felt strangely long." Reading that sentence back, I laughed. I'd somehow started measuring the length of my days by whether he came or not.

---

One afternoon, he sat alone in the shade of the tree by the well.

Jeteo walked over. I didn't follow. I knew that was his place to go.

It was late afternoon. Sunlight slanted in through the shade of the tree. Jeteo's shadow stretched long, moving toward the man. I watched that shadow gradually shorten. Jeteo's steps were slow. But they didn't stop.

I watched Jeteo's back. His shoulders weren't as tense as they used to be. Watching that back, I felt for the first time that you could tell what someone was walking toward just from the shape of their walking away.

"…Can we talk?" Jeteo asked. Not in his old accusing tone.

*I watched from a distance. I saw, for the first time, Jeteo going to find him. It had always been the man appearing first. This time Jeteo approached first.*

Watching that back, I thought of our last night in Brick Hollow. The night Jeteo shouted at the sky. Back then Jeteo had approached in anger. Now he was approaching by walking. I knew that difference was enormous.

---

The man gestured with his hand to the spot beside him. Jeteo sat.

I watched the way Jeteo sat beside him. In the old days he would have crossed his arms, or turned his body away. That day he simply sat side by side. Legs stretched out, hands resting on his knees. I understood, then, how much a single small posture could say.

The smell of earth in the tree's shade drifted on the wind to where I stood. Breathing it in, I fixed the sight of the two of them sitting there in my eyes. From a distance it was just an ordinary sight — two people sitting under a tree, nothing at all. But I knew how long it had taken to make that sight possible.

Neither spoke for a long while. Even from a distance, I could feel that the silence wasn't awkward.

I sat by the bucket and watched the two of them. Speon quietly came and sat beside me. He said nothing. We sat there together, side by side, watching the two of them from afar.

Every time the wind stirred the tree's leaves, the shadow shifted a little across the two of them. I tried to gauge how much time had passed by watching that shadow. But I couldn't quite manage it. That silence didn't seem to be something you could measure with time.

*I knew, then, that some conversations have already begun, even in silence. Jeteo and the man hadn't said a word yet. But I felt that something was already passing between them.*

I leaned toward Speon and asked in a very small voice,

"…What do you think those two are going to talk about?"

Speon shook his head.

"…I don't know either," Speon said. "But whatever comes out after a silence like that usually stays with you a long time."

I heard that and looked toward the two of them again. Still no words. But I wasn't afraid of that silence any more.

The bucket rope swayed lightly in the wind, making a small sound. A bird landed on the well's stone wall, then flew off again. I listened to those small sounds as if counting each one. If I didn't do at least that, the silence felt too heavy to bear.

"…I still don't really know," Jeteo finally said. "Who you are. Why you keep showing up. Whether I'm supposed to believe in God."

He didn't rush to answer, hearing that. In that silence, I felt Jeteo was preparing his next words.

"…I've come this far not knowing," Jeteo added. "But somehow, that not-knowing doesn't make me as angry as it used to. That's the stranger part."

"…I know," he said.

"…Don't just say you know — tell me what you know," Jeteo said. There was a trace of old irritation mixed into his voice.

He laughed a little at that. Not an angry laugh.

"…I know you still want an answer," he said. "And I know you're sitting here even though it hasn't come. That's a beginning."

"…But there's one thing I do know," Jeteo said. "I know how to sit beside someone now. But when I think about where I learned it… it comes down to you, in the end."

"…Do you remember how you learned it?" he asked.

Jeteo thought about that question for a moment.

"…You never once taught me," Jeteo said. "I just kept watching you do it. And somewhere along the way, I found myself doing it too."

"…That's what learning is," he said. "Even without words."

"…So did I learn you, or did I believe in you?" Jeteo asked.

"…The two don't always come in that order," he said.

Jeteo laughed a little at that. Closer to an exasperated laugh.

"…Talk like that and I'll never get an answer."

"…Right," he said. "But you keep coming."

I heard that from a distance and my chest went hot. That man had never once tried to win an argument against Jeteo's questions. He had simply recognized, before Jeteo did, the fact that Jeteo kept coming back.

He didn't answer that. But even from a distance, I saw his shoulder move, ever so slightly. I didn't know what that movement meant. But I could tell it wasn't a bad one.

---

He turned and faced Jeteo directly for the first time at that.

I felt that his eyes were different from usual. Eyes that were always gentle were, in that moment, looking straight at Jeteo.

"…Let me ask you one thing," he said. "Why can't you still trust me?"

The well-yard suddenly seemed to go very quiet. Even the bucket, even the birds, seemed to pause for a moment. In that stillness, I held my breath.

Far off, I felt Speon's hands go still too. Both of us knew, in our bodies, that this moment mattered.

Jeteo was silent for a long time. In that silence, I saw his hand tighten into a fist on his knee.

I watched Jeteo's profile. His jaw trembled faintly. I realized, for the first time, that trembling wasn't anger, but something else. Jeteo was fighting with something now — not with him, but with something old inside himself.

He didn't rush that silence. Sunlight filtering through the leaves drew small patterns on the dirt between them. I watched those patterns shift, trying to gauge how much time had passed.

"…Because I'm scared," Jeteo finally said. "If I trust you and it ends up like it did with Father again… I really don't think I could bear it this time."

Jeteo's voice was very small. But I felt the whole well-yard had gone quiet as if it too had heard those words. I knew, in that moment, that Jeteo was showing the deepest bottom of his heart for the first time in years.

He didn't argue with that at all. I found that strange. The Ledger of Haven would have had eight grounds of comfort ready on the spot. He didn't do that. He simply shifted his knee a little, turning his body further toward Jeteo.

"…That fear — I can't make it go away," he finally said.

"…Then what can you do?" Jeteo asked.

"…I'll be scared alongside you," he said.

*I set down that answer as the last line of this chapter. I heard Jeteo say, for the first time, the real reason behind his doubt. Not logic. Fear. I knew, in that moment, that Jeteo had come just a little bit closer.*
