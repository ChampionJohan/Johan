# Chapter 33. A Night on the Run

> _Previously_ — The searching light appeared again. It passed, but relief left guilt behind it. When Father asked, "Am I a burden to you?" Jeteo answered, "We're going together."

Two nights later, it wasn't the light that came — it was people.

That day had been unusually peaceful. Father had even picked a small wildflower along the road and handed it to me. Looking back, that peace hadn't given the slightest warning of what the evening held, which only made it more frightening.

The wildflower was yellow and very small. Handing it over, Father said, "…this is for you." The tone of someone for whom giving something to another person felt awkward, even to himself.

I pressed that flower between two sheets of paper. Later it dried flat, leaving only a faded stain on the page. I still have that stain.

My brother had been unusually on edge since evening. He stopped several times and looked back over his shoulder. I'd thought it was just habit. Looking back now, I think he'd already sensed something.

Toward evening the wind changed direction. It carried, not the scent of dry grass, but something like burning. I didn't know what that smell was, then. I know now. It was the smell of a fire people had lit.

Father, too, had been unusually quiet that day. I still wonder whether that was coincidence, or whether the three of us had already been sensing something, without knowing it.

At dinner, Father suddenly asked.

"…Are we being followed?"

My brother and I both looked at Father at once.

"…Why do you think that?" my brother asked.

"…I don't know," Father said. "I just keep wanting to look back. Both of you keep doing it too."

*I felt ashamed at that. We hadn't explained anything to Father. And yet he had already known, just from watching our bodies. There are things people know even without being told.*

At first I thought it was the wind. But there was a rhythm to it. Wind has no rhythm.

Footsteps. Several of them. We ducked quickly behind a stand of dry brush.

*In that moment I gripped Father's hand with everything I had. I wanted him to understand that we needed to stay quiet. But Father, frightened by the unfamiliar sound, began trembling instead.*

The brush's dry branches rustled against our clothes. Even that faint sound felt far too loud, so I made myself breathe with the greatest care.

The ground still held a little of the day's heat. I felt that faint warmth against my back and had the strange thought that this earth knew we were here.

Voices. Low, businesslike voices.

No anger, no urgency in that tone. I had heard that tone every day in Haven — behind the intake counters, in the corridors, handing over receipt slips. That night, the most frightening thing was that this same tone had followed us all the way out here to this field.

Their shadows stretched long under the moonlight. I counted them. Three.

"…You said there was a signal near here?"

"…Two Reassignment candidates. And a report that a completion record broke away too."

"…A completion broke away?" another voice asked. "Can that even happen?"

"…Rare," said the first voice. "That's why recovery's a top priority."

*I heard the word "recovery" for the first time that night. A word used for objects. From the moment I heard it, I began to think of Haven differently. That city had never once hated us. To hate someone, you have to see them as a person first.*

*The instant I heard that, my whole body went cold. I understood the phrase "a completion record broke away" meant Father. It was the first time I truly grasped that even Father, to this city, was not a person but an entry.*

I glanced sideways at Father's face. He had no idea what that conversation meant, and just gripped our hands tight. I thought his ignorance was, right then, a mercy. If he had known he was being called an "escaped record," I don't think he could have borne it.

Far off, insects called in fits and starts. I thought that irregularity resembled our own heartbeats. Even the world, it seemed, was frightened alongside us in that moment.

---

"…Shh." My brother pressed his hand gently over Father's mouth, as softly as he could. "We have to be quiet. Please."

Father nodded, eyes wide. He didn't know why, but he seemed to sense the danger.

*That moment stayed with me a long time. Father didn't know who my brother was. And yet he trusted the hand covering his mouth. I thought, for the first time then, that trust lives somewhere beneath memory.*

Father's hand kept gripping mine tightly. I felt a faint tremor through it. A body that had forgotten both name and face had, all the same, kept perfect hold of fear.

I wanted to calm that trembling. But I was shaking myself, so I couldn't tell who was calming whom.

The footsteps drew closer. Three people in Haven uniforms passed by. Bright tools in their hands.

Their clothes were clean. Their shoes were clean. Not the clothes of someone who'd walked a dirt road for days. I couldn't even imagine how or from where they had come this far.

One of them looked down at his wrist, reading something. Then shook his head. I understood our whole night hung on that single shake of the head.

Their pace didn't hurry at all. That very leisureliness frightened me more. It was the walk of people certain they would find us in the end.

*I didn't know what that device was. But I knew it was for finding us.*

"…The signal's weak around here," one said. "Why is that."

"…That's the boundary," another said. "The Ledger doesn't reach well out here either."

*I held onto that line the longest that night. That there was a place in the world the Ledger couldn't reach. It should have sounded frightening. It sounded, instead, strangely welcome.*

My brother pushed me gently behind him. Even in that moment, the gesture of protecting me first. I found that gesture familiar, and yet it hurt more than usual that night.

I gripped Father's hand tighter still, from behind my brother's back. Three of us were joined, that way, in a single line. I understood, in that moment, that there was nothing left we could do but hold onto each other.

*I knew my brother was afraid too, in that moment. But he was trying to carry all of that fear alone. As he always had.*

I watched my brother's back. His shoulders trembled faintly, holding his breath. I reached out and gently caught the hem of his shirt. I said nothing. I only wanted him to know I was here too.

My brother reached back without turning and took my hand. His eyes stayed forward the whole time. That hand trembled a little less than before, I thought. I learned that night: holding on makes the trembling smaller.

*I understood, then, for the first time, how much sturdier a family of three could be. Alone, I'd have collapsed right there. Even the two of us together might have been much the same. But three of us — we became the strength that held each other up.*

---

One of them stopped directly in front of the brush where we were hiding.

I could see the tips of his shoes. Through the leaves, only that much showed. I calculated that his shoe was one hand's-width away from touching Father's knee. I don't know why I calculated something like that.

*I remember hearing no sound at all in that moment. But that can't be true. The wind was blowing, insects were calling. I think a person, truly frightened, loses the sounds of the world for a moment.*

Time seemed to stop right there. I was terrified my heartbeat might carry outside. I could feel sweat breaking out on my brother's hand too.

I shut my eyes tight in that moment and thought of the last night in Brick Hollow. Holding our breath, hiding from the taskmasters' footsteps that night, felt exactly the same as this moment now. We were still surviving the same way.

I thought I would stop breathing. My brother pulled Father in tighter still.

Then Father tried to draw a deep breath. My heart plunged in that instant. If Father coughed, it was over.

But Father swallowed that breath back down. No one had told him to. I saw his throat move once, hard.

*Even now, thinking of that moment, my throat tightens. Father hadn't forgotten how to protect us. He had lost his name, and still he hadn't lost that.*

The man swept his light toward the brush. The beam passed right beside us.

I nearly imagined that light brushing my skin. Neither hot nor cold, and still every hair on my body stood on end.

The light swept through the inside of the brush. The dry branches went briefly white under it. I saw, in that instant, the back of my own hand go white too. I quickly pulled it inside my clothes.

*I shut my eyes tight in that moment. For the first time in my life, I asked for something — not in words, just in my heart. I don't know if that counted as a prayer. I just asked.*

In that brief moment, all the nights we'd run — Brick Hollow's night, Haven's alleys, every one of those fleeing nights — came flooding back at once. We had always been hiding from something. In that moment, I couldn't imagine when it would ever end.

*I understood something strange, then. We had always been running from something. And yet inside that running, we had never once stopped holding onto each other. Maybe, I thought for the first time in that moment, that wasn't only running. Maybe it was something else too.*

"…Nothing here," the man said, back toward the others.

"…You sure?"

"…Sure. Just brush."

I couldn't relax even hearing that. Because I alone knew the words weren't true.

The man soon turned and walked off in another direction.

I still couldn't open my eyes in that moment. Only once the footsteps had faded completely did I dare open them, carefully. My fingertips tingled and stung. I'd been gripping far too hard, for far too long.

There were my nail-marks left in Father's palm. I let go the instant I saw them. Father never once said it hurt.

"…I'm sorry," I whispered.

"…It's all right," Father said. "I liked being held."

Father said that while looking down for a moment at his own palm. He touched the marks there like something curious. He never once let on that they'd hurt.

---

Even after the footsteps had disappeared completely, we couldn't move from that spot for a long while.

My legs had gone numb and I couldn't stand. My brother seemed the same — he stayed sitting there, still buried in the brush. Father was the first to gather himself and help us both up.

As he lifted us, Father brushed the dirt from our knees. My brother's knee first, then mine. I thought, somehow, that order felt like a very old one.

Father's hand felt unusually steady in that moment. Memory was gone, but the body still knew how to raise its children back onto their feet.

Feeling that touch, I suddenly thought of some morning back in Brick Hollow. Back then, too, Father had brushed the dust from our clothes to start the day.

My brother finally let out a breath and looked up at the sky.

"…Where are you," my brother said, low, but clearly. I knew who it was addressed to. "You don't show up when it matters, and yet that time — that time you did."

My brother's voice wasn't loud. But the field was wide enough that the words traveled far, and came back bringing nothing at all.

"…You came the night Father was screaming," my brother said. "Nobody died that night. Today all three of us nearly died. And today — where were you."

*I set that question down here now, exactly as it was, without softening it. It was the most honest sentence in this whole story. I have no intention of making it gentler.*

No answer came at all. The night sky was just a night sky.

There were so many stars. So many. I found myself angry at how many there were, that night. All that many, and not one had answered.

Father watched my brother quietly. He clearly understood nothing. And still Father went and stood beside him, and laid a hand on his shoulder without a word. My brother didn't shake it off.

"…Hard, isn't it," Father said. That was all. Not an explanation, not comfort. Just the words of someone who had recognized that another person was in pain.

My brother bowed his head at that. I saw his shoulders heave once, hard.

I looked at that hand and felt a strange comfort. Someone who had lost both name and memory still knew how to lay a single hand on the shoulder of someone breaking down over an answer he'd never received. I thought that might be the deepest thing still left in Father.

I watched the two of them's silhouette for a long while. A son who'd gotten no answer, and a father who couldn't even understand the question, standing side by side. And yet the picture, strangely, didn't feel only sad.

After a while I went and stood beside them too. The three of us stood in a row and looked up at the same sky. No one said a word.

"…I'm hungry," my brother said, after a long while.

I burst out laughing at that. Just moments earlier he'd been shouting up at the sky, and this was his very next line.

"…Me too," Father said.

That night we split what bread was left, three ways, in tiny pieces. I think that was the only answer we received that night.

Chewing his bread, Father said suddenly.

"…It's good." Such an ordinary thing to say. But somehow, in that moment, it made the three of us smile, just a little.

Even after we'd finished the bread, we stayed sitting there a long while. The night wind was gradually dying down. I felt it fading and held onto one single fact — that this night, too, would eventually pass.

Father pushed the last piece of bread toward my brother. My brother refused it a moment, then finally took it. I think that small back-and-forth was the most ordinary moment of that whole night.

*I set that silence down as this chapter's last line. I understood, then, how justified my brother's question was. I couldn't give him an answer. No one could. And yet, in that silence, I held onto one single fact — that we were still alive.*
