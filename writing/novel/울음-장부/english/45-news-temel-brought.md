# Chapter 45. News Temel Brought

> _Previously_ — Father revealed the meaning of our names. Jeteo meant "to seek, to search"; Elpis meant "hope." It came out that everything about how the siblings had lived had already been inside their names.

Around the time autumn arrived, Temel stopped by the well again.

Leaves were just beginning to color. I thought the color was different from the autumn I'd known in Brick Hollow. Brick Hollow's autumn had been the color of dust. Here, autumn was red and gold.

The trees around the well were shedding their leaves, one by one. Every morning I gathered a few of the fallen ones. There wasn't really a reason. I just felt it would be a waste to leave them.

I pressed the leaves I'd gathered between the pages of my record. After a few days, the leaves flattened out and darkened in color. I found that both a shame and something I liked. The leaves didn't lose their shape even as they dried.

*I still have those leaves. Some have crumbled, some have survived. I think that's a lot like what a record is. You can't keep everything, and what you do keep isn't exactly as it first was. But it survives.*

On every page where I'd pressed a leaf, I wrote a short line about what had happened that day. Nothing big, mostly. Jeteo fixing the bucket, Father humming a new tune. I liked pressing those small things flat, together with the leaves.

Father was the first to see Temel coming that day. He looked toward the well, then raised a hand and pointed toward the hill. He said nothing. I thought about when Father had started being the first to notice someone coming.

"…It's been a while," Jeteo said, greeting him warmly. His face was far more at ease than it had been months earlier.

Temel's walk looked lighter than before, too. I wondered where that lightness came from. His pack looked lighter too.

Temel saw Father and smiled.

"…You've gotten a lot better," Temel said.

Father looked at Temel, curious.

"…You were the one who first came looking for me, weren't you?" Father asked. Not an exact memory. But something seemed to remain.

"…That's right," Temel said, his face full of surprise.

Temel bent his knees to meet Father at eye level.

"…You didn't even have a name back then," Temel said quietly. "And now you recognize me like this."

Father smiled a little at that. A smile like he found it surprising himself.

"…What was I like, when you first found me?" Father asked.

Temel chose his words for a moment. The firelight cast shadows across Temel's face.

"…There was nothing there," Temel said honestly. "No name, no expression. But your eyes were looking for something. That was why I didn't give up on you."

Father was silent for a long time, hearing that. I felt he was carving those words somewhere in his heart.

"…Why didn't you give up?" Father asked, after a while.

Temel looked a little surprised at the question. No one had probably ever asked him that before.

"…I lost my own name once too," Temel said. "Someone didn't let go of me, then. I'm just doing what I received."

*I remember that answer for a long time. Temel didn't say he was some great person. He said he was doing what he'd received. I later learned that every adult I met at this well spoke that same way.*

I thought, in that moment, of the Administrator in Haven. The Administrator had been proud of the work he did. Temel spoke of his work more like the paying off of a debt. I thought that difference in attitude mattered more than the work itself.

Father nodded. And briefly took Temel's hand, then let it go. It was a strange gesture — not quite a greeting, not quite a handshake. Temel took it without any awkwardness.

---

That evening, we sat together around the fire. Speon threw a few more logs on. The flames grew, casting a red light over our faces.

The dry wood crackled as it burned. A few sparks rose and vanished. I followed those sparks with my eyes. As one went out, another rose.

There had been no fire in Haven. To put it precisely, there hadn't needed to be. That city was always just the right warmth, just the right brightness. I'd thought, back then, that was a good thing.

*Even now, I don't write that the city was a bad place. Only that in that city, there had been no reason for people to sit gathered around anything. Without a fire, no one gathers. Without gathering, no stories come out. I think that may have been the real reason that city was so quiet.*

Temel passed on the news of the world outside.

"…Curious about Haven?" Temel asked.

Jeteo nodded. There was no anger in it like before. Just curiosity. I felt that difference again, watching from the side.

"…Still quiet," Temel said. "But I've heard there's been a crack in that quiet lately. More and more people are refusing COMPLETE, refusing to file at all."

*Hearing that, I felt strangely moved. We hadn't gone there to change that city. But something seemed to have taken root in the place we'd left behind.*

"…Aren't those people in danger?" I asked.

"…They are," Temel said honestly. "But it doesn't seem to stop. Once someone starts, I guess it gets a little easier for the next person."

"…Who started it first?" Jeteo asked.

"…Don't know," Temel said. "Apparently they started without leaving a name. Just refused to file, that's all."

Jeteo nodded quietly at that. I felt he was holding that nameless person somewhere in his heart.

"…So do they just stay in pain, then?" I asked.

"…Yeah," Temel said. "If you don't file, nothing gets resolved."

"…Then why do they do it?"

Temel stared into the fire for a while before answering.

"…They said if you take the pain away, the person seems to disappear with it."

A chill ran down my spine at that. I thought of the backs of the people I'd seen walking away in Haven, holding papers stamped COMPLETE. They'd all looked at ease. At ease, and yet somehow thin.

Jeteo leaned a little toward the fire.

"…I think I know that feeling," Jeteo said. "When they told me to file about Father, I was afraid. I was afraid that if I filed it, Father would really disappear."

Temel looked at Jeteo. He didn't add anything. He just nodded.

Father was listening to that too. His expression was complicated. I felt Father react, faintly, every time he heard the name Haven. Not a complete memory. But his body seemed to sense it first.

"…Are you all right?" I asked Father.

"…Yes," Father said. "It's just… a name that's unfamiliar, but not unfamiliar."

Jeteo moved closer to Father's side.

"…You don't have to talk about it, if you don't want to," Jeteo said.

"…No," Father said. "I want to know. I want to know more about my own story too."

Hearing that, I thought Father had grown far braver than before. That willingness to face his own past without fear — I found it admirable.

I thought about where that courage of Father's had come from. It wasn't that he wasn't afraid of living without an answer. It was, I think, because he'd come to know there was someone who would stay beside him even without the answer. I thought that was the same thing Jeteo had learned.

---

"…What about the Administrator?" I asked.

"…Still there," Temel said. "But not long ago, I heard someone secretly made a room where people keep remembering the names of those who've been COMPLETEd. They say the Administrator knows about that room and looks the other way."

*I thought of the Administrator's face in that moment. That face saying "That isn't mine to answer." I thought that maybe he was still resisting, in his own way, from inside that city.*

"…Does that room have a name?" I asked.

"…Not one, they say," Temel said. "Just a room, apparently. But people call it 'the remembering room.'"

I repeated that name to myself, several times, in my mind. The remembering room. I thought that name resembled the thing we were doing here. Right in the middle of Haven, someone was secretly doing the very same thing we'd learned here.

"…What do they do there?" Jeteo asked.

"…Not much, apparently," Temel said. "They say the names of people who've been COMPLETEd out loud. That's all."

"…That's it?"

"…Yeah. That's it."

I thought that answer stayed with me longer for being so small. Calling names aloud. That was the biggest act of resistance that city could hold.

*I've never seen that room. I still can't be sure, even now, that it really exists. But I set that rumor down in this ledger anyway. Not because I wish it were true, but because the fact that such a rumor could spread at all means there were still people left inside that city.*

Father listened to this and said quietly,

"…Did someone say my name there too? In that place?"

No one could answer. Jeteo reached his hand toward Father and laid it over his.

"…It's been said here," Jeteo said. "Every day."

Speon spoke up then, joining the conversation for the first time.

"…Any news of Mena?" Speon asked. His voice made a poor effort at sounding unbothered.

Temel answered, holding back a laugh.

"…She's doing fine. Still walking around out there. Though I hear she's setting her direction this way more often than she used to."

Speon said nothing to that. But I saw the tips of his ears turn faintly red.

Watching that, I couldn't help laughing. It struck me, oddly tenderly, that even someone who'd lived through so many years turned out just the same as anyone else, in a moment like this.

Speon poked at the firewood needlessly. It didn't need poking. The fire dimmed for a moment and then flared back up.

"…Drink some water," Speon said, in a tone aimed at no one in particular.

Temel burst out laughing. I laughed along too. Even Jeteo laughed. Father laughed along too, not knowing what the joke was about. I thought that was the best part of the whole evening.

---

"…What about Noa?" Jeteo asked.

"…She's well. Her tent camp's grown even bigger. More and more people out there remembering names, they say."

I thought of Noa's face. I thought, too, of the lights of that tent camp where she'd first taken us in. I thought of the song the children had sung under those lights. I could still hum it, even now.

I hummed a little of that song right there. Father heard the sound and lifted his head.

"…Where did you hear that?" Father asked.

"…At the tent camp," I said.

Father listened for a moment, then began humming a few phrases along. His pitch was a little off. But he followed it through to the end. I couldn't tell, then, whether Father had heard that song somewhere before, or was simply following the sound of my voice. I still don't know now.

"…The children must have grown too," I said.

"…Grown a lot," Temel said. "Some of them are looking after younger children now, themselves. That's how time flows."

Jeteo laughed quietly at that.

"…It's strange," Jeteo said. "We were just running away, and something's growing wherever we ran through."

Temel looked at Jeteo quietly at that.

"…That's just how seeds are," Temel said. "They only really start growing once they've left the hand that planted them."

Hearing that, I thought of my flowerbed. That sprout, smaller than a fingernail — it would be growing, bit by bit, even now, while I wasn't watching.

*I've never seen that flowerbed again. That was a long ache for me. It's a little less of one now. Not seeing it again doesn't mean it isn't growing.*

I thought, in that moment, of the seed pouch Jeteo had given me. Jeteo had offered no explanation at all, handing it to me. I felt now that I understood what that silence had meant. Some things, you don't need a reason to plant.

"…Temel," Jeteo said. "Can I ask you something?"

"…Sure."

"…Doesn't it wear you out? Finding people."

Temel thought for a moment.

"…It wears me out," Temel said. "But being worn out and wanting to quit turn out to be different things."

Jeteo nodded at that. I saw him tuck that sentence away somewhere. Jeteo had taken to keeping people's words like that, lately.

Temel stayed with us late into the night. At dawn the next day, he set off again. We saw him off as far as the edge of the well.

"…You'll come back?" I asked.

I noticed my own voice growing a little urgent as I asked. I was still bad at sending people off. Ever since Brick Hollow, a departing back had always been, for me, a little frightening.

"…Yeah," Temel said. "This place has become important to me too now."

Father looked like he wanted to say something too, then. But the words wouldn't come, so he just handed Temel a stone instead. The one with the white stripe, picked up a few days earlier.

Temel took the stone and looked at it for a long time.

"…Thank you," Temel said. "I'll carry this in my pocket."

Father smiled at that. I thought Father had finally come to fully understand why he'd been collecting stones.

The dawn air was cold. A light frost lay over the grass. I heard the footsteps crunching over it, crisper than usual.

Temel's figure grew smaller and smaller, then disappeared over the hill. I waved my hand until that figure was gone.

Speon watched that departing back for a long time too. I saw both longing and hope held together in his eyes.

Jeteo walked over to Speon and nudged his shoulder.

"…Next time, grab hold of her first," Jeteo said, teasing.

Speon cleared his throat at that and looked away, pretending not to hear. I couldn't help laughing at that.

I looked for a long time down that road that had disappeared over the hill. Temel lived on the road; we had put down roots at the well. I thought those two lives, though different, were headed toward the same place. Someone searches, someone stays. Both were ways of refusing to forget people.

*I set down those words as the last line of this chapter. I knew, then. A seed grows even when the one who planted it can't see it the whole way through. We were learning that fact with our own bodies.*
