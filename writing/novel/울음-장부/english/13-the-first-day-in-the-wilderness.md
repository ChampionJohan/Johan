# Chapter 13. The First Day in the Wilderness

> _Previously_ — Marked as candidates for Reassignment, the brother and sister left Haven and crossed the boundary stones. Realizing that Haven, too, had been only another kind of fence, they set out walking toward the unshaped fields beyond. "This fear is mine. No one else gets to resolve it for me."

From that day on, we had to learn all over again what hunger was.

In Haven, the moment you thought you were hungry, bread was already waiting for you. Not here. Here your stomach just went on being hungry.

*That going-on was strange to me. In Haven, no sensation ever stayed long. Hunger, thirst, even sorrow — they would rise for a moment, be filed, and vanish. Here a sensation didn't vanish. It simply went on being there. I found that frightening, and strangely, it also felt real.*

Less than a full day had passed since we crossed the boundary stones, and already Haven's time felt impossibly far away. When I looked back there was no longer any sign of the city's spires. Nothing but flat ground and low hills.

The ground underfoot was different too. Haven's streets had always been even stone. Here it was rough and uneven. I stumbled more than once, and every time, my brother reached out without a word and caught me.

Small stones were pressed into the ground. Some were sharp, some rolled round and loose under my heel. I felt every one of those unevennesses come up through the soles of my shoes as I walked. Haven's streets had never given my feet anything to feel — you set one foot down and the next step simply followed. Here, every single step belonged to me alone.

A low wind blew, carrying the smell of dry earth. I wrinkled my nose. But soon I grew used to it. Haven had almost no smell at all. Everything there was so clean that the absence of any smell had, once or twice, struck me as strange in itself. Here the smell shifted a little with every step — dry grass, a damp shade, and now and then the trace of some animal I couldn't name.

The sun was already high. The shadows were shortening. That day I learned, for the first time, how to judge the hour by the length of a shadow. In Haven I had never had to count time for myself — the Ledger always told us.

*I noticed something strange then. The hunger itself was exactly like Brick Hollow's. But the way we had to bear it was different. In Brick Hollow, hunger came bound together with the whip. Here, hunger was only hunger. No one was handing it to us as a punishment.*

As I walked I touched the back of my hand. The scar from Brick Hollow was still there. While we were in Haven I had forgotten it entirely — too caught up in all the Ledger kept giving us. Only out here in the Wilderness did the scar come back into my sight.

*Looking at it, I had a strange thought. This body remembered Brick Hollow. However perfect Haven was, it had never managed to erase even this much.*

Even as he walked, my brother kept scanning every direction. I could tell it had already become a habit. In Haven no one ever looked around like that. Here, not looking around would have been the strange thing.

"…Scared?" my brother asked.

"…Yes," I said. "But strangely, I don't hate it."

He looked at me for a moment at that. His face said he understood something.

"…Me too," he said after a while. "Scared, but — maybe it's because this fear is mine."

Hearing that, I looked at his face again. In Haven his face had always looked like it was holding something back. Now it was simply his face.

I wanted to look at it a little longer, so I slowed my steps on purpose. He noticed and turned back to me.

"…Why are you looking at me like that?" he asked.

"…No reason," I said. "It just feels like the first time in a long while I'm really seeing your face."

At that he let out a small laugh. Even the sound of it was unfamiliar. I couldn't remember hearing it much, not in Brick Hollow, not in Haven.

A gust of wind blew hard once. Dry dust rose a little at our feet, then settled. I watched quietly as it settled onto the tops of my feet. Haven's ground had always been clean. There had never been room for dust to gather. Here, the trace of this ground stayed on our bodies exactly as it fell.

*I didn't brush the dust off. Strangely, I wanted to keep it there. It felt like proof that we had really walked this road.*

Walking ahead of me, my brother suddenly stopped and looked back. He stood like that a long while.

"…See something?" I asked.

"…No," he said. "I'm looking because there's nothing to see."

I thought about what he meant for a moment. That was when I learned that even making sure no one was following took time. In Haven there had never been reason to look back. Whatever came from ahead or behind, the Ledger always knew first.

*I understood then that even confirming, with your own eyes, that nothing was there took time. And that even that time belonged to us.*

---

We went looking through the field for anything we could eat. My brother dug up some kind of root and chewed it.

"…Bitter," he said, wincing. "But not so bad you can't eat it."

I chewed some too. It really was bitter. But we laughed. We hadn't laughed much, back in Haven.

*I didn't spit the bitterness out. I let the taste spread all the way to the back of my tongue and simply endured it. Haven's food had always been just right — never too sweet, never too bitter. That was the first time I understood that "just right" was, in truth, nothing at all. This bitterness, at least, was something.*

"…Why is this funny?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he said. "Just — we found this ourselves. Maybe that's why."

*Hearing that, I understood something for the first time. The bread the Ledger gave us had been perfect, but it had never been ours. This wretched, bitter root was ours.*

He went on pulling up a few more kinds of plants and smelling each one. Some he tossed aside at once; others he tucked carefully into the fold of his shirt.

I crouched beside him and watched his fingers. His hands looked a little like Father's — thick-knuckled, at ease with soil. The hands that had once carried bricks in Brick Hollow were pulling weeds now. Strangely, that change felt like something to be grateful for.

"…Can't eat this one," he said, tossing away a plant with pointed leaves. "Smells wrong."

"…How do you know?" I asked again.

"…My nose just knows first," he said. "When your body says no, you have to believe it. In Haven our bodies never even got the chance to say no to anything."

Listening, I wondered whether my own body had a sense like that. I still didn't know, not yet. But I thought that someday I too might learn to understand what my nose, what my body, was telling me.

"…How do you tell them apart?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he admitted. "I'm just going on instinct. I could be wrong."

Strangely, that answer felt reassuring rather than frightening. In Haven no one ever said "I could be wrong." The Ledger was always right.

"…And if you're wrong?" I asked.

"…My stomach will hurt, I guess," he said, shrugging. "Then next time I just won't eat that one."

I laughed because it sounded funny. But once I stopped laughing and thought about it again, it wasn't a funny thing at all. Learning by being wrong first. Haven had never had such a thing — there had never even been a chance to be wrong.

He kept rubbing each leaf between his fingers and smelling it. Some made me wrinkle my nose; some were faintly sweet. I tried a few myself. The green smell it left on my fingertips lingered a long time.

"…Look at this," he said, pointing to a small yellow flower. "There's this too. I don't think I ever saw this color in Haven."

I leaned in to look closer. He was right. Haven's flowerbeds had always been filled with flowers of the same color, the same size. This one was small and crooked. But that crookedness felt more real, somehow, not less.

We sat for a while, sharing the root between us. My stomach was still hollow. But strangely I liked that moment. My brother and I, chewing the same thing, wearing the same face.

Far off, I heard a bird call. I was hearing it for the first time. Haven had no birds. Or maybe it did — I couldn't say. But I had no memory of ever hearing birdsong there.

"…What kind of bird is that?" I asked.

"…I don't know either," he said. "But you don't need to know its name. If it sounds good, that's enough."

I liked that. That you could love something without knowing its name. That was a thought Haven had never taught me.

"…Do you think Father ever ate something like this?" I asked.

He was quiet a moment before he answered.

"…I don't know. But if he's alive somewhere, I think he's probably about as hungry as I am right now."

*Hearing that, I imagined Father through hunger for the first time. Strangely, even without an answer, it felt like it brought me a little closer to him.*

"…Father never let it show, even when he was hungry," I said. "Remember? Whenever he split up dinner, he always left a little of his own share behind."

"…I remember," he said quietly. "I didn't understand why back then. I think I do now."

I didn't press him further. Too much was folded into that "I think I do now," and I didn't want to pry it open.

We sat a good while longer. Every time the wind blew, the dry grass rustled. I sat listening to it quietly. In Haven, even when the wind blew, it made no sound. Every wall there seemed to hold something that swallowed sound whole.

*That was the first time I understood that sound came in kinds. The kind that got swallowed, and the kind that simply rang out. I liked the ringing kind better.*

My brother stood and stretched. His joints made a small cracking sound.

"…Let's go," he said. "We should walk a little more before the sun's down."

Standing, I felt an ache behind my legs. I had never walked this long in my life. But even that ache, strangely, wasn't unwelcome. In Haven, however far we walked, our legs had never felt like this. Only now did I realize it had always felt as though something else had been walking for us.

We started walking again. The shadows were growing longer.

---

Toward evening, we saw smoke far off in the distance. A sign that someone lived there.

"…Should we go look?" I asked.

He hesitated a moment.

"…Carefully," he said. "It could be another place like Haven."

We crept toward the smoke carefully. There were a few small tents. People sat around a fire. There were children among them, and old people too.

*Seeing that, I felt strangely at ease. Every one of their faces was different. Some were laughing, some were crying. It was a sight I had never once seen in Haven.*

Something was boiling in a small pot hung over the fire. The smell rode the wind all the way to us. The moment it reached me, my stomach lurched hard. Haven's food had had no smell — or maybe it had, but I couldn't remember it. This smell was thick, smoky, a little scorched. And yet, strangely, it smelled like people living.

My brother slowed and stepped in front of me.

"…Stay behind me," he said.

I fell in obediently behind him. But his own steps, I noticed, were trembling a little too. I understood that he was afraid, and leading the way anyway.

---

Some of the tents were worn and patched in places. Looking at the mended seams, I understood these people had held on here a long time. Haven's buildings had always looked new. There, nothing was ever given the chance to wear before it was fixed.

A woman noticed us first.

"…Who are you?" she asked. Her eyes were wary, but not hostile.

"…We're lost," my brother said. "Could you spare us something to eat?"

The woman studied us a moment, then waved us toward the fire.

"…Come and sit. There isn't much, but we can share it."

The people around the fire saw us and shifted, each making a little more room. No one said much of anything. They simply moved themselves aside and made a space.

*I still haven't forgotten that small movement. In Haven no one ever had to make room for anyone — there was always space enough. These people, whose space was not enough, narrowed it anyway to make some of it ours.*

Settling into that narrow gap, I felt a stranger's shoulder brush lightly against mine. A stranger's warmth. I almost drew back, but I didn't. I found I didn't mind it.

*Hearing her say that, I felt something strange. That phrase — "there isn't much." I had never once heard the word not enough in Haven. The Ledger was always sufficient. But in these people's not-enoughness there was something the Ledger's sufficiency had never had.*

What the woman gave us was one piece of dry bread and a mouthful of water. Truly not much at all. But my brother's hand trembled a little as he took it. I understood that wasn't only from hunger.

"…Thank you," he said. His voice was low.

"…It's nothing," the woman said. "Everyone comes to us like that, at first."

The firelight wavered over their faces. I looked at each one in turn. Some looked tired; some, like mine, looked like they were studying something unfamiliar. But no one pushed us away.

Even after I'd finished the bread, I kept the last small piece in my hand a long while. I hated to swallow it. In Haven I had never once felt the wish to hold onto something. That day I learned, for the first time, that longing had nothing to do with being full. My brother, too, finished his share and carefully brought the crumbs left on his fingertips to his mouth.

The woman looked our way once more and asked,

"…What are your names?"

"…Jeteo," my brother said. "This is Elpis."

"…Jeteo. Elpis." The woman said each name once, aloud. Then she nodded. That was all. Nothing was filed. No answer came back.

*I remembered that nothing-of-a-moment for a long time after. It was the first night our names were spoken not to be resolved, but simply to be spoken.*

Somewhere off, a child laughed. I turned my head toward the sound. A small child was chasing another around and around the fire. A few of the adults watched and laughed quietly.

*Listening to that laughter, I felt that these people, who had nothing, had not lost something. I didn't know exactly what, not then. Now I do. It was each other.*

What exactly that something was, I did not yet know, that night.
