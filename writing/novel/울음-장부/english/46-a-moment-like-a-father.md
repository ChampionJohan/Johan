# Chapter 46. A Moment Like a Father

> _Previously_ — Temel brought news from outside. Haven is still quiet, but more and more people are refusing COMPLETE. "Wherever we ran through, something's growing."

Autumn faded fully away. As winter drew near, a cold wind began to blow through the well-yard.

Nearly all the leaves had fallen. A thin frost began settling on the stone wall around the well. I picked up a new habit of rubbing that frost with a fingertip, every morning.

Wherever my fingertip touched, the frost melted away and the dark stone showed through. I'd leave a few of those marks in a row and step back. Once the sun rose, those marks vanished along with the rest of the frost. I didn't mind that. I could always make new ones the next morning.

*I never felt the seasons change in Haven. That city was always just right. I thought, back then, that being just right was a good thing. Now I like a morning when my hands are cold. Because when they're cold, you can lend someone your coat.*

Every time I made those frost marks, I called for Jeteo. He was annoyed by it at first, but later crouched down beside me too, making marks of his own. We'd leave our handprints side by side in the frost and watch together as the sun erased them.

The well water grew colder as winter came on. But strangely, the water we drew up in the morning would give off a faint steam. Speon said it was because the ground underneath was warmer than the air above. I thought that sounded like something you'd say about people.

Father seemed to love winter especially. I didn't know why. He just breathed the cold air in deep and made a face like he loved it. I thought that expression looked familiar. In Brick Hollow, too, Father's face had looked like that whenever winter came.

I was making do with just one thin coat. One morning, Father took off his own coat and wrapped it around my shoulders.

"…You looked cold," Father said. As if it were nothing.

I could feel Father's warmth still lingering in that coat. It was warm.

The coat smelled of earth and smoke. The smell of someone who spent the day picking up stones, then sat beside the hearth each evening. I realized, then, that the smell had turned into Father's smell. In Brick Hollow, Father had always smelled of dry clay.

After giving me the coat, Father immediately looked away, as if not wanting to give me a chance to say thank you. I found that rushing away familiar.

Speon watched that from beside us and smiled quietly. I thought I understood why. Speon must have seen countless small gestures like that here at the well.

"…Your father's gotten a lot better," Speon said.

"…I think so," I said. "But lately I'm not sure what 'better' even means. He can do things like that even without all his memory back."

Speon nodded at that.

"…Maybe that's the real recovery," Speon said. "Not memory — love left in the body."

I couldn't answer for a long moment, hearing that. I held those words close for a long time. I didn't know, then, that they would stay with me the whole winter through.

"…But what," I finally managed to ask, "if the memory never comes back at all?"

Speon's hand paused where it had been coiling the bucket rope.

"…Then it just doesn't come back," he said. "I can't call that a good thing. That's a loss. A real loss."

I felt oddly at ease at that honesty. Speon never invented a comfort that wasn't there.

"…But just because a loss happened doesn't mean what's left becomes fake," Speon said. "That's a different matter."

*I copied those words into that night's record. I understood, then, a little, why Haven had been so good at comfort and yet never comforting at all. That city always tried to make loss into something that had never happened. Speon named a loss a loss, and then sat down beside it.*

I set those two sentences side by side and studied them for a long time. "A loss happened." And "I'm still beside you, even so." I learned, only here at this well, that the two could come from the same mouth, at the same time.

---

I couldn't say a word in that moment. It was such a small gesture. But inside that gesture was something that had come before name or memory.

*I knew, then. Being a father wasn't about remembering — it was about noticing the cold first. Father didn't know that. But he was already doing it.*

Wrapping that coat around myself, I thought of Brick Hollow. Every winter, Father had always given us his coat first. Back then I'd just thought it was natural. Now I know how far from natural that was.

"…Are you cold?" I asked.

"…I'm fine," Father said, smiling, even though he looked cold himself. I found that lie strangely touching.

I secretly studied Father while wearing that coat. There were goosebumps faintly rising on his arms. I pretended not to see.

*I still think that pretending-not-to-see was the greatest gift I could give Father. Some kindness is only complete when you pretend not to have noticed it at all.*

A little while later, I pulled one side of the coat back and draped it over Father too. It was too small a coat for two people to share. Father laughed.

"…This way we'll both be cold," Father said.

"…Then let's both just be a little cold," I said.

Father looked at me for a long moment at that. And said nothing, just stayed like that. We stood there, sharing one coat, beside the frost-covered stone wall.

I felt, right there, Father's arm wrap lightly around my shoulder. An awkward strength. I thought that awkwardness made it feel more real, somehow. I felt that clumsy arm would stay with me longer than any practiced embrace ever could.

---

The wind was fierce all that day. Jeteo stayed out late with Speon, mending the bucket rope. I watched them through the window.

Even after the sun went down, the two of them stayed out a while longer. Once it grew dark, Father took a lantern outside and stood beside them. Father didn't do anything. He just stood there, holding the light.

Through the window, I watched the three of them — the two mending the rope, and the one lighting them from above. I knew, right there, that this picture would stay with me a long time.

Jeteo saw that scene too. That evening, he said to me,

"…I still don't know if Father remembers me completely. But today, seeing that, I understood. Even without full memory, Father is already Father."

"…How do you know?"

"…This morning he tried to pick up the water jar before I could," Jeteo said. "He used to do that too. Whenever we carried something heavy, he'd always take it away from us."

I nodded, hearing that. I'd seen similar moments myself, more than once.

"…You can just tell, from what he does without thinking," Jeteo said. "Action came before words, with Father."

Hearing that, I remembered that the old Jeteo never used to talk this way. He always used to want proof. Now Jeteo was saying that a single gesture was enough.

"…I feel the same way," I said. "Lately there are things I believe even without proof."

Jeteo glanced at me at that. He said nothing. But something like agreement showed on his face.

"…That doesn't mean I've come to believe everything," Jeteo said, after a moment. "I still don't know why Father had to go through that. No one can explain that."

"…Yeah," I said.

"…But it's strange," Jeteo said. "You can still eat dinner even without an explanation. That used to be impossible for me."

*I copy that down exactly, into this ledger. Jeteo never did get his answer, to the end. I write it down, not hiding it. Only, Jeteo learned how to live without an answer. I think that was harder than getting an answer.*

The night wind seeped in through the window frame. I pulled the blanket a little closer and sat right up against Jeteo. He didn't push me away, either.

---

Jeteo brought the firewood inside. It had been left outside a long while, so its surface was damp. Father took it and stood the pieces up beside the hearth to dry. No one had told him to.

Once the fire caught, the room grew warm. That night, Father sat us down on either side of him and asked,

"…Would you tell me some old stories? About me. What kind of person I was."

Jeteo hesitated for a moment at that, then began slowly. The days in Brick Hollow, the stories Father used to tell us every evening, the calluses on Father's hands.

I added a few things too. The tune Father used to hum while hauling bricks; the way his hand stroked our hair every evening; his face that never once grew angry, no matter how hard things got.

Speaking of it, I realized I remembered far more about Father than I'd thought. Nothing big, mostly. Small things, all of it. How Father took off his shoes, which side of his jaw he chewed with when he ate, how his eyes crinkled when he laughed.

*That was when I first saw what memory is made of. Memory wasn't made of events — it was made of habits. What Father had lost was an event. What remained in Father was a habit. That was why Father was still Father, even not knowing who he had been.*

"…And you always went to bed after us," I said. "You'd check we were asleep before you closed your own eyes."

"…That's right," Jeteo added. "You never once slept first."

Father smiled a little, hearing that. A shy sort of smile.

"…I think that's still true," Father said. "Even now, my eyes only close after I've seen you two asleep."

Father closed his eyes several times, listening to that story.

"…Did I ever get angry?" Father asked.

Jeteo thought for a moment.

"…There was one time," Jeteo said. "When I talked back to a taskmaster. That was the first time you ever shouted."

"…Why?"

"…You must have been scared," Jeteo said. "Scared I'd get beaten."

Father sat quietly with that for a long time.

"…That's a good kind of anger," Father said.

I found that phrase staying with me for a long time, strangely. A good kind of anger. I'd never heard, until that day, that such a thing could exist.

No one spoke for a while. In that silence, I felt Father piecing together the fragments of his own life, one at a time.

"…Tell me more?" Father asked. Eyes still closed.

Jeteo picked the story back up again. The days we'd had to hide from the taskmasters; and even so, Father's voice in the evenings, telling us old stories. Listening, I felt I could smell Brick Hollow again.

"…Weren't you scared of them? The taskmasters," Father asked.

"…We were," Jeteo said. "But you being beside us made it less scary."

"…I don't remember any of it, but hearing that, strangely, it puts my mind at ease," Father said.

"…Why?" I asked.

"…It means I was of use," Father said. "Even then, too."

Something stung at the tip of my nose at that. Father still didn't fully know how great a presence he had been to us.

"…What a good person he was, that man," Father said. He said it as if speaking of someone else, and yet there was something stirring in his voice.

"…That man is you, Father," Jeteo said quietly.

I knew how long it had taken Jeteo to say those words. For months, he'd called Father "sir." That hadn't been dislike — it had been carefulness. Jeteo hadn't wanted to force the name of "Father" onto him.

Father nodded at that. Slowly, but clearly.

I thought that nod carried more weight than any words could have. Father accepted that title with no conditions attached at all. Even without his memory fully returned, he was ready to fill that place.

Father wrapped both arms around the two of us. An awkward gesture. But its strength was certain.

"…Thank you," Father said. "For giving my own story back to me."

Tears welled in my eyes at that. Jeteo must have felt the same, because he buried his face in Father's shoulder.

I thought, right then, of the last night in Brick Hollow, the moment Father had held us for the last time. That moment and this one overlapped. A different place, a different season — but the same warmth.

The time between those two moments hadn't erased anything. The day Father had been taken away, the night we crossed the boundary stones, the day we'd stood before the intake window in Haven — it was all still there, unchanged. I no longer wished for that to disappear.

*I hesitated a long time before writing this part. I felt the temptation to write "everything was restored." That isn't true. Father never remembered a great many things, to the end. We never got back the years we lost.*

*But what was in that room that night wasn't recovery — it was something else. I still haven't found a name for it. So I'll just set that night down exactly as it was.*

The fire in the hearth slowly died down. We stayed like that a while, holding each other. No one moved first.

Outside, the wind kept blowing. Cold air seeped in through the gap in the door. But no one got up to fix it.

I didn't write that night's record. I couldn't pull my arms free. I ended up writing it the next morning, and because a day had passed by the time I wrote it, a few details had already blurred.

*I don't mind that. Some nights are better lived in than written down. I've learned that too, in my years as a recorder.*

A few days later, I added a few more sentences, thinking back on that night again. A record doesn't get finished all at once, that way. I decided to think of that, too, as this ledger's own way — that some pages are allowed to fill in slowly.

Whenever I think of that night, I remember the ember that glowed red the longest inside the hearth. The flames had all died down, but that ember alone kept its warmth for a long time. I thought Father was like that ember too. On the outside, he looked entirely gone out. But somewhere inside, there was always warmth left.

*I set down those words as the last line of this chapter. I knew, that day. Father was learning his own story all over again. And the two people who knew that story best were sitting right beside him now.*
