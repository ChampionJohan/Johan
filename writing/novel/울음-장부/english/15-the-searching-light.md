# Chapter 15. The Searching Light

> _Previously_ — At a tent camp in the Wilderness, the brother and sister met Noa and her children. Noa was someone who had left Haven of her own will rather than have her sorrow "resolved." When they showed her the paper stamped COMPLETE, it came out that the resistance of remembering names was already underway all across the Wilderness.

At dawn, one of the children woke us.

A small hand shook my shoulder. Half-asleep, I thought at first it was only a dream. But the shaking grew more urgent. I forced my eyes open.

"…Look, there's a light," the child said, pointing at the sky.

I opened my eyes. Low in the sky, a small light moved slowly. It looked like a star, but it wasn't. It hung too low, and it moved too evenly.

The night air was cold. I sat wrapped in my blanket, watching that light. I couldn't tell whether my fingertips were trembling from the cold or from fear.

*The instant I saw it, a chill ran down my back. I didn't know what it was. But I knew, from the look on Noa's face, that it was nothing good.*

I couldn't tear my eyes from that light. It was very small, but strangely distinct — as if the whole darkness had made itself into a backdrop for that one light alone. I found that distinctness frightening. That something distinct could be more frightening than something blurred — that was a thing I understood for the first time in that moment.

A small rustling stirred inside the tents. The sound of children waking. I heard the adults soothing them, low. I found it strange that even in such urgency, no voice ever rose.

My brother saw it too. I felt his whole body go rigid, all at once. Without a word, he took my hand and pulled me low. His hand was cold as ice.

Around us, one by one, the other adults were already getting up, gathering the children in. No one shouted. Everyone moved quietly, practiced, as if this weren't the first time.

A man quickly covered the embers with dirt. Smoke billowed up for a moment, then died away. I could tell at a glance how many times that motion had been repeated. There was no hesitation in it at all.

Somewhere far off a dog barked once, then cut off sharply. It sounded as if someone had clapped a hand over its mouth in a hurry. That total stillness felt, if anything, more suffocating than the bark had.

*That quiet frightened me more. A danger that came with shouting wasn't unfamiliar to me — that had been Brick Hollow, every day. But a danger this quiet, that no one raised their voice against, was something entirely new.*

Noa called the children's names quietly, one by one. Not loudly. Just a calling to check where each of them was. Even in that brief, frightened moment, hearing their names called put me strangely at ease.

*I understood then. Being called by name in a moment of fear, and having your case received in a moment of comfort, were two entirely different kinds of care.*

Noa knelt in front of one child, level with her eyes, and cupped the girl's cheeks in both hands, whispering something low. I couldn't hear all of it. But I saw the strength go out of the child's shoulders. I kept that scene in my mind. That was how one person soothed another, standing in front of fear.

Rachel came up quietly beside me and gripped my arm. I could feel the small tremor of her hand against mine. I gripped it back. I said nothing at all. But holding that hand, somehow, I was a little less afraid.

I looked up at the sky once more. The stars went on twinkling around that small light as if nothing were happening. I found that indifference strange. The stars seemed to have no idea at all what that light was searching for. Or else they knew, and simply went on twinkling as they always had, regardless. I couldn't say which of the two was sadder.

*That was the first time I ever thought the sky might not be on our side. In Brick Hollow I had believed the night sky was the one place even a taskmaster's hand couldn't reach. But this city, it turned out, knew how to float its own light in that same sky.*

---

"…It's a searching light," Noa said in a low voice, quietly pushing the children into the tent. "Haven sends those out sometimes. When it's looking for Reassignment candidates."

"…Is it because of us?" my brother asked.

"…I don't know," Noa said. "But there's no need to find out. Put out the fire."

We doused the fire quickly and crouched behind the tent. I gripped my brother's hand tight. He gripped mine just as tight.

*In that instant, the night we crossed the boundary stones of Brick Hollow came back to me exactly as it had been. In place of the crack of whips, there was that silent light. I found it more frightening. Because it made no sound.*

I lifted my head just slightly and looked at the light again. It moved without a sound, slowly crossing the sky. The motion of it looked as if something were being combed through, very carefully, inch by inch.

*Watching that motion, I felt something strange. There was no hurry in that light at all. And the fact that it wasn't hurrying was somehow more chilling than if it had been. Not hurrying was the ease of something already certain it could find whatever it was looking for.*

I tried to breathe as low and slow as I could. Every time my heart beat, I felt sure the sound of it would carry out into the open. I could feel my brother holding his breath the same way beside me. We stayed curled up like that, each of us hiding our own breathing from the other as best we could, waiting only for that light to pass.

*In that moment I remembered the nights in Brick Hollow we'd spent waiting for a taskmaster's footsteps. We had held our breath the very same way, back then too. That familiarity made me sad — that even in the place we'd run to, we still had to curl ourselves into the same shape.*

---

The light passed slowly over our heads once. It didn't stop. It went on past and vanished into the dark.

Only after a long while did Noa let out her breath.

"…It's gone," she said.

Only then did I let out the breath I'd been holding, long and slow. When I opened my hand, there were little half-moon marks where my nails had dug into my palm. Only then did I realize how hard I'd been gripping my brother's hand.

Around us, people rose to their feet one by one. No one asked anyone else if they were all right. But everyone checked each other's faces, once, all the same. I felt that wordless checking was a truer kind of asking after someone than words could have been.

Rachel still hadn't let go of my arm. I gently worked her hand loose and looked at her face. There were tears standing in her eyes, but she wasn't crying. That held-back look, on a face so young, hurt something in me — it looked far too practiced for her age.

"…Does this happen often?" I asked.

"…Sometimes," Noa said. "Not a few of the people living out here came from that city. So we're always careful of it."

*Hearing that, I understood that the Wilderness was not, as Haven seemed to believe, empty. This was a place where the people Haven had missed had gathered to live.*

Listening, I imagined other tents somewhere out in that darkness, curled low just as we were. I thought there must be people we couldn't see, hiding from the same light, holding the same breath, somewhere beyond our sight. Just imagining that made me feel, strangely, a little less alone.

My brother asked quietly.

"…So does that light keep circling this area?"

"…There's no pattern to it," Noa said. "Sometimes it won't come for months. Sometimes it comes several days running. So we just stay ready, always."

"…Ready how?"

"…Putting the fire out fast. Keeping the children quiet. That's about it," Noa said. "It isn't much. But it's all we can do."

"…Did you see it too, your first time out here?" my brother asked.

"…I didn't know what it was, at first," Noa said. "I thought it was just a star. But people out here told me. That light never moves like a star. A star drifts, or twinkles. It doesn't move steadily in one direction like that."

"…So everyone who first comes out here doesn't know, and has to learn?" I asked.

"…That's right," Noa said. "I learned it the same way. Someone told me. Now I'm the one telling you."

*Hearing that, I sensed another kind of ledger, running through this Wilderness. A ledger with no paper, no filing. A ledger of how to survive, handed down from person to person.*

"…Does it make a sound at all?" I asked. That, strangely, was what I wanted to know most.

"…I've never once heard it," Noa said. "Some people say it just doesn't have the part that makes sound. Others say it doesn't need to, so no one bothered building it."

*Hearing that, I felt a chill run over me. Not being able to make a sound, and having no need to make one. I thought the second was far more frightening. Having no need meant it could already find out everything some other way.*

"…What happens if it catches you?" I asked.

Noa's face went thoughtful, choosing her words.

"…It might be better not to know," she said. "But not asking might be more frightening, so I'll tell you. If it catches you, you're just taken back to Haven. And after that… no one has ever heard about that person again."

At that, my brother's hand closed into a fist.

"…So going back means becoming a Reassignment candidate all over again?" my brother asked.

"…Probably," Noa said. "I don't know exactly, myself. But the fact that word of the people who go back stops completely — that can't mean anything good."

Listening to that, I thought of the day the phrase "candidate for Reassignment" had first been spoken to us. That phrase had always worn a gentle face, then and now, hiding something terrible underneath it.

I thought again of the Administrator's face. Always smiling. I never once imagined, while I was still inside Haven, that behind that smile was hiding a light this quiet.

*I thought of every gentle thing about Haven all over again. The gentle voice, the gentle lighting, the gentle bed. I began to think that all that gentleness might, in truth, have been the very same kind of thing as this quiet light. Never hurrying, never making a sound, and yet, in the end, always finding the person it was looking for.*

*Hearing that, I thought of the COMPLETE stamp again. I understood then that the people of the Wilderness lived carrying the same fear we did, every single day.*

I looked at my brother's face. He was still watching the spot where the light had disappeared. I didn't ask him what he was thinking. I already knew, by then, that some thoughts only grow heavier once you put them into words.

A gust of wind scattered a little of the dead fire's ash. Watching it scatter into the night air, I felt an odd sadness — that the thing which had been warming us only a moment before could vanish this easily, without a trace. I couldn't tell whether that sadness was for the fire, or for something else entirely.

---

That night, my brother asked Noa something for the first time.

"…If we stay here, will we be putting the rest of you in danger?"

Noa looked at him for a long moment.

"…A little," she said honestly. "But sharing danger is just how it works out here. We all came here the same way."

"…What do you mean, you all came here the same way?" I asked.

"…The first time I stepped into someone else's tent, I was a danger to them too," Noa said. "But they didn't drive me out. So that's what I learned to do too."

*Hearing that, I felt that this tent camp was like one long chain. Someone had taken on the danger and let it in, and because of that, the next person could take on the danger too, and let the next person in after that.*

Noa looked off into the distance for a moment. Her face looked like she was remembering something.

"…The person who took me in, back then — she's not here anymore," Noa said quietly. "I'll tell that story some other time. Your story comes first, right now."

I felt, from those words, that Noa was carrying some heavy story of her own that we didn't yet know. I didn't press her. I felt certain I'd hear it, in time.

"…Still, wouldn't it be better if we left?"

"…That's for you to decide," Noa said. "But as for me, I'd choose to carry the danger together over driving away someone dangerous. That's what's different between us and Haven."

My brother said nothing to that. But I saw something like a decision cross his face.

Smoke from the fire shifted with the wind and drifted toward us for a moment. My eyes stung, and I shut them briefly, then opened them again. Even in that brief moment, my brother's eyes never left Noa's face.

Watching his profile, I felt he was laying Noa's words over some memory from Brick Hollow. It was because someone had shared a danger with us, I thought, that we were even here now. Someone in Brick Hollow might have been that kind of person to us too, without our ever knowing it.

"…Thank you," my brother finally said. "We don't want to make you carry more danger because of us. But… let us stay a little longer, for now."

I saw his hand resting over his chest as he said it. The paper stamped COMPLETE was there, inside. I thought that guarding that paper, and not wanting to be a burden to these people, must come from somewhere in the same root. My brother was afraid of laying a weight on anyone. And yet, little by little, he was also learning how to let a weight be shared.

Noa smiled a little at that.

"…That's all I need to hear," she said. "Out here, even the words 'let us stay' are a good thing to say."

"…Haven doesn't have that phrase at all," I said. "There, whenever you're given something, it feels like you're meant to pay it back right away. But they never even tell you how. They just tell you to move on to the next matter."

"…That's the strangest part of it," Noa said. "It leaves you feeling indebted, but it never lets a real relationship grow out of that debt. Because a relationship means you'll come back again. Out here it's the opposite. You owe someone, and you come back, and before long, that's just what living is."

The fire was being built up again. People drifted back to their places, one by one, trading low words of reassurance. I was glad to hear that noise return. That small commotion felt far more alive to me than the silence had.

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. Haven was a place that got rid of danger. This Wilderness was a place that shared it. For the first time, I understood that which place was safer might matter less than which place was more alive.*
