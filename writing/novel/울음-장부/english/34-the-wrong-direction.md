# Chapter 34. The Wrong Direction

> _Previously_ — A Haven search party truly did come after the siblings. Once the danger had passed, Jeteo asked the night sky, "You don't show up when it matters, and yet that time you did?" — and got only silence in return.

The next day, we set out again, as quietly as we could.

None of us had slept properly. Everyone's eyes were hollow. I flinched all day, overreacting to every footstep. Even the crack of a breaking twig made me jump more than once.

My brother changed our course twice that morning. Not the direction we'd meant to go, but a wide, deliberate loop around it. "…We're not going the way those people went yesterday," was all he said.

Because of that we walked far slower than usual. I found that slowness frustrating, and also a relief. Staying alive mattered more than being fast.

The sky was as clear as it had been the day before. I found that clarity strangely alien. The world seemed entirely untroubled by what had happened to us the night before.

*I thought, for the first time then, about the world's indifference. The night we held our breath, so close to death, had been, to the sky, just one more night passing by. I found that indifference bitter, and at the same time, strangely comforting. It meant the world wasn't singling us out specifically.*

I thought about mentioning that to Father and decided against it. Father had all but forgotten the night before already. Bringing that night back up in front of someone who'd forgotten it seemed cruel.

My brother stayed almost entirely silent all that day. I didn't want to disturb him, so I simply walked beside him.

*I understood, then, that my brother's silence was connected to the question from the night before. A question left unanswered, I learned that day, stays on afterward, in silence, in place of an answer.*

Now and then I saw my brother glance up at the sky. It looked like searching for something, and also like resentment. I didn't ask.

Once, he stopped walking entirely and looked up. He looked for quite a while. I looked up too. Nothing there but a few clouds.

"…What are you looking at?" I asked.

"…Nothing," my brother said. And started walking again.

*I turned that word — "nothing" — over for a long time. No one stares that long at a sky with nothing in it. My brother hadn't been looking at something. He'd been watching for something to appear.*

Father, unusually, was in high spirits that day. I didn't know why. He simply said walking felt good. I envied that lightness. Father, having no memory, seemed to have all but forgotten last night's terror by that morning.

Father even crouched down beside the road for a long while, watching a small insect. I found the sight strange, and somewhere in it, endearing too. Father looked like a man fully present in this one moment.

Father mimed counting the centipede's legs with his finger. Three, four, five — and every time the centipede moved, he'd start over from the beginning. I saw, in that gesture, the old habit of Father counting numbers on his fingers. But this time it didn't frighten me.

"…Look at this," Father called us over. "So many legs."

My brother came over reluctantly and looked.

"…It's a centipede," my brother said.

"…It has a name," Father said, marveling. "Even something this small has a name."

My brother went quiet for a moment at that. I understood exactly why he couldn't answer.

My brother watched Father from a little way off, then said quietly to me.

"…Father seems a lot freer than we are," my brother said. "Because he has no memory."

I didn't know how to answer that. It seemed both right and wrong at once.

*I thought, for the first time then, that forgetting might not be only a curse. It was a strange thought. I didn't hold onto it long.*

Father walked along, kicking small stones now and then with the toe of his shoe. A childlike gesture. Watching it, I laughed, and then, right after, my chest ached, somewhere.

I only understood the shape of that ache late that evening. Father wasn't only turning into more and more of a stranger — he was, at the same time, growing up all over again, alongside us. I found it strange that both things were happening at once.

*Even now I don't know how to settle that thought. Father was a person losing something, and at the same time, a person learning something new. I didn't know which side to root for. In the end, I decided to root for both.*

---

That afternoon, we met a merchant coming from the opposite direction. He spoke to us first.

He carried a large pack on his back. He looked like someone who traded goods between towns. I saw the fatigue of a long-traveling man in his clothes.

Pots and bowls hung from his pack, clattering with every step. I'd been hearing that sound from a long way off. But none of the three of us hid. That wasn't the sound of someone who traveled in hiding.

"…How far are you going?" the merchant asked.

"…South," my brother answered shortly.

"…All three of you?" the merchant asked, glancing at Father. "Is that your father?"

My brother paused a moment before answering.

"…Yes," my brother said. "He's our father."

*I nearly cried, hearing that answer beside him. It was the first time my brother had introduced Father to a stranger that way.*

"…There was quite a stir around here last night," the merchant said. "Heard a search party chased someone clear off in the wrong direction."

"…Wrong direction?" my brother asked, ears pricking up.

His voice cracked slightly. I understood he was reliving the night before, hearing that.

I nearly dropped the water jug I was holding. Only then did I remember — the direction those people had gone past our brush, the night before, was exactly opposite the direction we'd come from.

"…Yeah. Some old man apparently gave them the wrong directions. Then when they went looking for him afterward, he'd vanished too. People are telling all kinds of strange stories about it now."

*I looked at my brother in that instant. He looked at me too. Neither of us said a word. But we both knew we were thinking the same thing.*

The merchant, not noticing our changed expressions, kept talking.

"…Funny thing is, there wasn't a single trace where the old man disappeared from. Not even a footprint." The merchant shrugged. "People are saying all sorts of things about it."

"…What did the old man look like?" my brother asked, unable to hold back.

The merchant shrugged again.

"…I only heard it secondhand, so I don't know. Just that he was wearing worn-out clothes, that's all I heard."

I found that description so simple that it only made me more certain, not less. Nothing flashy, nothing distinctive. That was exactly the man we knew.

The merchant's pack clattered again. That ordinary sound felt, in that moment, oddly loud. For a second I even had the illusion that the world was listening in on us.

"…Why did the old man do that?" I asked. "Why would he lie to the search party?"

"…Who knows," the merchant said. "Nobody knows the answer, that's why the strange stories keep going around."

"…Is it wrong to tell a lie?" Father suddenly cut in.

The merchant looked at Father, a little startled.

"…Well, I suppose that depends on who it's for," the merchant said.

Father nodded slowly at that. I thought Father seemed satisfied with that answer.

*That answer was enough for me too. I could see, in my brother's expression, that he felt the same. For the first time, I thought of the man in the worn clothes who had moved for our sake, and felt something warm settle in my chest. I learned, that day, that fear and gratitude can live in the same place at once.*

Before leaving, the merchant added one more thing to us.

"…You be careful too. People around here are on edge these days."

We nodded. That parting word didn't feel like a passing remark to me. It felt like real concern.

The merchant walked a few steps, then turned back to add one more thing.

"…And don't build your fire too big at night. It shows for miles."

My brother bowed his head at that. I remember it as the most formal bow my brother ever gave a stranger.

---

After the merchant left, my brother was silent a long while before he spoke.

"…Was it him."

Having said it, my brother rubbed his own mouth once with his hand. Like someone not quite trusting the words that had just come out of it.

There was both certainty and resistance in my brother's voice at once. I felt he wanted to accept the fact, and at the same time was afraid that accepting it would change something.

"…I don't know," I said. But in my heart, I was already almost certain.

I didn't tell my brother that certainty out loud. I thought if I showed it too plainly, he'd push back against it even harder.

*I learned something that day. When a person is coming to understand something on their own, pushing them from the side only makes them step back. From that day on, I stopped trying to convince my brother of anything.*

"…Then why doesn't he just say so from the start?" my brother asked, frustration bleeding into his voice. "Then I wouldn't have to get angry like this every single time."

"…Would you not be angry, if he did?" I asked.

My brother was quiet a moment.

"…No," my brother said finally. "I'd probably still get angry. Nothing about Father makes sense anyway."

"…Then it wouldn't change anything, even if he told us," I said.

My brother looked at me. He seemed about to say something back, then let it go.

*I didn't have an answer to that question either. But a strange thought crossed my mind, then. That maybe being told in advance is worse than walking on together, still afraid.*

"…And," my brother added, low, "being angry is exhausting too. But if I stop being angry, then I don't know what I'm supposed to do."

I thought that was the most frightening thing my brother had said on this whole journey. Anger was proof that my brother still expected something. Without any expectation left, there'd be no anger either.

*I stopped being afraid of my brother's anger from that moment on. I understood that anger was proof something was still alive inside him.*

*I'd been afraid, before, that my brother would break down. Looking back now, I don't think he was breaking down at all. My brother was searching for a place other than anger.*

I couldn't say anything back to that. I just walked quietly beside him. I learned again, that day, that silence is sometimes better than any answer.

---

Father had been listening quietly to that whole exchange, and then asked.

"…Who is that person?"

Only then did I realize Father had been listening to every word. I'd thought he mostly let our conversations pass right through him. Not that day.

My brother couldn't answer right away. After a long pause, he finally said,

"…I don't really know either. But… he seems to keep being near us. Without ever explaining."

My brother himself looked surprised at his own words as he said them. I heard him admit that for the first time that day.

"…Why doesn't he explain?" Father asked.

"…I don't know," my brother said. "That's the part that frustrates me most, too."

"…I'm one of the ones who can't explain," Father said. "Not explaining, and being unable to explain — I suppose they must look the same, from outside."

My brother stopped walking at that.

Father smiled a little at what he'd said.

"…Then I guess I'm a bit like him," Father said. "I can't explain who I am either. And yet here I still am."

My brother stopped a moment, standing still. I saw him swallow something back before he could say it.

"…Yeah," my brother finally said. His voice was low. "A bit like him."

"…So are you angry at me too, then?" Father asked. It came out like a joke, but it didn't quite sound like one.

"…No," my brother said quickly. "I'm not angry at you."

"…Why not?"

My brother took a while to answer.

"…Because you're here," my brother finally said.

Father tilted his head at that. Not the face of someone who'd fully understood. But the face of someone who'd received something warm.

*I still count that answer, even now, as one of the most important answers in this whole story. My brother hadn't wanted an answer. He'd wanted someone to stay beside him. I don't think my brother himself knew that, until the words came out of his own mouth.*

Father heard that and reached out to pat my brother's shoulder, lightly. He didn't say anything more. But that single gesture was, in that moment, enough.

I saw my brother's breath go out of him in one long rush the moment that hand touched his shoulder. It sounded like several days' worth of held breath being let out all at once.

*I understood, from that day on, that people can be undone by words, but also undone by a wordless hand. What undid my brother wasn't the silence of the night before. It was that hand, that afternoon.*

I felt, in that short answer, that my brother had set something down, just a little. Not all of the anger — but a new place seemed to have opened up right beside it.

We walked late into that evening. It wasn't until the sun had fully set that we made camp. I noticed, that night, that my brother spent a little longer than usual looking up at the sky.

We kept the fire small, exactly as the merchant had advised. Small enough that the three of us had to press close together to feel any warmth at all. Father sat in the middle. No one had suggested it. It simply became his place, naturally.

The small firelight lit each of our three faces in turn. In that flickering light, I felt our three expressions growing, little by little, to resemble each other.

That night I wrote in my paper: "Today, for the first time, my brother called Father 'my father' to a stranger." I wrote that one line and, for a long while, couldn't bring myself to fold the paper closed again.

Father glanced at that paper and said nothing. Instead he laid a gentle hand on my shoulder. I understood from that touch that whether or not he understood the sentence, he sensed that something precious had been written down.

*I set that line down as this chapter's last line. I saw an expression on my brother's face in that moment that I'd never seen before. It wasn't anger. It wasn't certainty either. It was simply the face of someone who had opened his heart, just a very little.*
