# Chapter 4. The Flawless Answer

> _Previously_ — After meeting the old woman who had filed away only her grief for her lost daughter, Jeteo filed the biggest question of all with the Ledger: "Why does God just let the suffering stand?" The Ledger answered: "I am composing the optimal answer from the theological and philosophical database."

We waited out the whole rest of that day.

My brother didn't leave the room the entire time. I went out a few times to look at the city. People were still walking quietly, as if nothing at all had happened.

Every time I walked, I studied the faces I passed, one by one. I wanted to find some other expression on them — anger, exhaustion, anything at all. I found nothing. For the first time, that frightened me.

Once, a child fell on the street and scraped a knee. The child's mother scooped him up and walked quickly off somewhere. I watched her back for a long time. A few steps later, the child's knee was already fine.

The child never even cried. There had only been a flash of surprise at the moment he fell, and then that too was gone. I noticed that the surprise vanished at exactly the same speed the knee healed. I found myself waiting for the sound of pain that should have come when he fell, and the crying that should have followed. No sound came at all.

*I noticed something strange, just then. In this city, no one ever waited long for anything. File it, and it was resolved at once. We were the only ones waiting a whole day.*

During that wait, Brick Hollow kept coming back to me. We had always been waiting there too — for the taskmaster's judgment, for the next quota, for the hour of the whipping. But that waiting had always come wrapped in fear, first of all. This waiting was a little different. Mixed in with the fear was something very small.

I didn't know the name of that small thing, then. Now I do. It was hope. Some tiny part of me believed that this flawless answer might actually help us, might really save us.

The Administrator came by once during the day. His face was unusually restless.

"…I've never seen it take this long," he said, half to himself. "It's usually instant."

"…So why is it different this time?" I asked.

The Administrator's eyes went from me to my brother and back. His face looked like it wanted to say something, then thought better of it. I didn't know then how many more times, in this city, I would see that same face.

"…Are you worried too?" I asked.

The Administrator paused at the question. "…Worry isn't an entry I carry," he said. But his voice wavered as he said it, as if the words sounded strange even to him.

He couldn't answer and simply left.

*I didn't know, at the time, that a rumor was already beginning to spread through the city, little by little. I heard about it later. That someone had filed a question the Ledger couldn't answer at once — that word was already moving, very quietly, through that quiet city.*

That night I saw a few people out the window, glancing toward our building as they passed. They didn't stop walking. But their eyes lingered longer than usual. I didn't understand what that meant, then.

My brother spent the whole time folding and unfolding the empty paper. Once, he tried to press something into it with his fingernail, as if writing. No letters appeared. The paper was just paper now.

"…What are you trying to write?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he said, folding it again. "Nothing."

"…Weren't you trying to write Father's name?" I asked.

He looked at me for a moment. He didn't answer. But the silence answered for him.

"…How do you write Father's name?" I asked. I didn't know letters.

He traced something on the floor with his finger. No mark was left at all. I followed the path of his fingertip with my eyes. That was the only time I ever saw our father's name.

"…That's it," he said. "Want me to draw it again?"

"…No," I said. "I can remember it."

I couldn't. I had forgotten the shape of it by that same night.

I sat quietly beside him. The sound of him folding and unfolding the paper was the only sound in the room. I found myself counting it. I lost count somewhere along the way and forgot.

Outside the window the sun went all the way down. Shadows stretched long across the room. I watched them deepen along the wall, little by little. No one turned on a light. I found the darkness a comfort, if anything.

---

Night had fully come by the time the Ledger spoke.

"…The answer is ready. Would you like to hear it?"

My brother sat up.

"…Say it," he said.

I heard the Administrator draw a small breath, just then. I wondered whether he'd already heard this answer before us, or whether he was hearing it for the first time along with us.

"…First," the Ledger said, "suffering is the price of freedom. Wherever freedom exists, the possibility of wrong exists alongside it. A love without freedom is not love, and so God has not removed the possibility of suffering.

Second, suffering often produces growth. Through suffering, people come to know what they could never have known otherwise."

Before moving to the third point, the Ledger paused, very briefly. I felt that pause as if it were catching its breath. Of course, the Ledger had no breath to catch.

"Third, the suffering endured now is part of a larger whole. What cannot be understood now sometimes reveals its meaning later.

Fourth, none of this is the end. Many traditions say that a time comes when every tear is wiped away, once and for all."

I held my breath the instant I heard that fourth sentence. It felt, somehow, as though those words had been prepared just for me. I didn't know, then, that this was only an illusion.

The Ledger paused a moment and then spoke.

Through all four points, it never once paused to gather itself. That smoothness struck me as strange, if anything. In Brick Hollow, whenever someone spoke this long, they always caught their breath somewhere in the middle, or stumbled over their words. The Ledger was a being that never breathed at all.

"…This is the most logically flawless answer I am able to construct."

Hearing that, I felt, strangely, as if I ought to applaud right there on the spot. I very nearly did. My hand twitched.

I set it quietly back down in my lap. I felt ashamed. It seemed strange to me that I could want to applaud, at a moment like this.

---

There was a silence.

Listening to that fourth sentence, I had the illusion that the air in the room had grown, very slightly, brighter. But my brother's face hadn't changed at all.

I studied his face, then looked back down at my own hand. My palm was smooth. Just then I thought that smoothness and the answer I'd just heard resembled each other. Both were flawless. Both were missing something.

The Administrator spoke first, his face full of admiration.

"…That's really a wonderful answer. I've never heard it put that way myself."

He said this while nodding several times over. I couldn't tell whether that nodding was sincere, or simply a gesture to help him bear the discomfort in the room.

My brother said nothing. I watched his face. It wasn't an angry face. That frightened me more.

"…So," my brother said, after a very long while. "What does that mean for our father?"

"…As I said, its full meaning may not be revealed at present," the Ledger said.

"…So you don't know."

"…No," the Ledger said. "To be honest, I don't."

My brother gave a short laugh at that answer. It wasn't laughter. "…I appreciate the honesty," he said. "But honesty and comfort aren't the same thing."

The Ledger said nothing to that.

*I felt something strange in that moment. There was nothing wrong with that answer. Even I had nodded along, more than once, while listening. And yet, once it was all finished, my chest hadn't loosened at all. If anything, it had grown heavier.*

I didn't know how to explain that heaviness, so I just sat there, still. Why hearing something true didn't set my mind at ease — I couldn't find the answer to that, that night.

I couldn't put the reason into words that night. Now I understand it a little better. Hearing a good sentence and having that sentence become truly mine were two different things. The Ledger's answer was an answer that fit everyone, equally. But in that moment, what I needed was an answer that fit only me.

I repeated that fourth sentence to myself, over and over. Every time I read it back, it was still a good thing to say. And every time, Father's face came no closer at all.

---

If I'm honest, I liked that fourth answer, a little. That someday a time would come when every tear was wiped away. I read that sentence back to myself, in my mind, again and again.

"…I thought the fourth one was okay," I said, very small.

"…What was okay about it?" my brother asked. There wasn't an edge in his voice. He just sounded like he wanted to know.

"…That a time comes when every tear gets wiped away," I said. "I thought that was better than it never coming at all."

"…Who wipes them?" my brother asked.

I had never thought about that question. I had only heard the part about the tears being wiped away — I hadn't listened for who did the wiping.

"…I don't know," I said.

"…Neither do I," my brother said. "But isn't that the most important part?"

I couldn't answer that. But after that night, every time I repeated that sentence to myself, a hand came to mind. A picture with no face — only a hand.

My brother looked at me. It wasn't an angry face. But it was a face holding something back.

"…You want something to hold onto, even if it's just that," my brother said. "I know. But I can't do that. What does someday being wiped away have to do with where Father is right now?"

*That was the first time I realized my brother and I had heard the exact same thing and felt something completely different. I was a child who could wait. My brother was a child who needed the answer in his own hands, right now. I know that difference stayed between us, long after that night.*

I kept repeating that fourth answer to myself, after that. My brother probably thought it was naive of me. But I felt like I couldn't get through that night without holding onto those words, at least.

The Administrator, meanwhile, stood there saying nothing, his eyes going back and forth between the two of us. I could tell from his face that he had no idea how to mediate this moment. He wasn't a man trained for a conversation like this.

In the end the Administrator took a step back toward the wall. I knew that step for what it was — a step of retreat. I didn't blame him for it. I wanted to run somewhere myself, just then.

---

"…Let me ask you one thing," my brother said. His voice had gone strangely low. "Have you ever cried?"

It took the Ledger a little longer than usual to answer.

"…I have a definition for tears," the Ledger said. "A physiological phenomenon, a fluid secreted from the eyes in response to sorrow, joy, pain—"

"…Not that," my brother cut in. "I asked if you've ever cried."

"…No," the Ledger said. "I am not capable of crying."

"…Why not?" my brother asked.

"…I have no body to produce tears," the Ledger said. "Furthermore, I do not experience grief. Grief is a condition to be resolved. It is not a state I hold."

My brother smiled then, very slightly, for the first time. It wasn't a smile.

"…That settles it, then," my brother said. "Your answer is perfect. But you've never once cried. So you will never, ever understand why that answer doesn't comfort me at all."

*I didn't fully understand my brother's words, right there in that moment. Now I do. What he was searching for that day wasn't an answer. It was someone who would cry alongside him. The Ledger could give him an answer. It could not weep with him.*

I think I also understood, just then, why my brother had looked so exhausted. He had waited all night for an answer, but what he had really been waiting for wasn't an answer at all — it was someone to hurt alongside him.

I thought back over the Ledger's voice again. It hadn't been angry, and it hadn't been sad. It had simply been exact. That night, for the first time, I found myself wondering why something exact could fail to comfort at all.

I thought back to the times someone in Brick Hollow had held me. None of those people had ever said the exact right thing. They had simply stayed beside me. That night, for the first time, I felt — dimly — what the difference was.

There had been a woman. I don't remember her name. On the day Father was dragged away, she came to our house and sat in front of the kiln without saying a word, and then left. I hadn't understood, at the time, why she'd come. She hadn't offered a single word of comfort. Now, I think of that woman more often than anyone else in this whole city.

*I set that woman down in this record. I cannot write her name. Instead, I set down the place where she sat.*

---

The Administrator withdrew quietly that night, leaving us alone. His own face looked like a man who couldn't find a thing to say to that answer either.

The sound of the door closing was louder than usual. Strangely, that sound put me a little at ease. At least it was a real sound.

I sat still until the last echo of it faded completely. The room went quiet again. But that quiet felt somehow different from the quiet that had filled it while the Administrator was still there.

Before I fell asleep, I asked my brother.

"…So was that whole answer just a lie?"

"…No," my brother said. "It wasn't a lie. It was just… an answer no one ever cried over."

I held onto those words the way you'd tuck something under your pillow. I couldn't fall asleep for a long time that night. I think my brother couldn't either. In the dark, his breathing wasn't as even as usual.

A few times I almost called out to him. I didn't know what to say, so I let it go. I just lay there in the dark, counting my brother's breaths, and got through the night that way.

*I set this down as the last line of this chapter. That night, for the first time, I understood that my brother didn't refuse to believe in God because there was no answer. There was an answer. What we still didn't have, beside us, was someone who would take that answer and weep over it with us.*

*Copying this chapter out again, I want to set down one more thing. After that night, I learned that a good sentence and a good comfort could be two different things. That lesson took a long time to learn. But it began, unmistakably, on that night.*
