# Chapter 4. The Flawless Answer

> _Previously_ — After meeting an old woman who had filed away her grief for a dead daughter and nothing more, Jeteo filed the biggest question of all with the Ledger: "Why does God just let the suffering stand?" The Ledger answered: "I am composing the optimal answer from the theological and philosophical database."

We waited out that whole day.

My brother didn't leave the room the entire time. I went out a few times and looked at the city. People still walked quietly, as if nothing at all had happened.

Once, on the street, a child fell and scraped a knee. The child's mother scooped the child up and walked quickly away somewhere. I watched their retreating backs a long time. A few steps later, the child's knee was already fine.

*I noticed something strange, then. No one in this city ever waited long for anything. File it, and it was resolved at once. We were the only ones waiting the whole day through.*

During that wait I kept thinking of Brick Hollow. We had always been waiting there too. Waiting on a taskmaster's judgment, on the next quota, on the hour of the whip. But that waiting always had fear running ahead of it. This waiting was a little different. Alongside the fear, something very small was mixed in.

The Administrator came by once during the day. His face was unusually restless.

"…I've never seen it take this long," the Administrator said, half to himself. "It's usually instant."

"…So why is it different this time?" I asked.

The Administrator looked at me, then at my brother. His face wanted to say something and then thought better of it. I didn't know, then, how many more times I'd see that face in this city.

The Administrator couldn't answer, and just left.

*I didn't know, at the time, that rumors were already spreading a little through the city. I heard later. Word that someone had filed a question the Ledger couldn't answer at once was quietly making its rounds inside that quiet city.*

My brother spent that time folding and unfolding the blank paper. Once he tried to press something into it with his fingernail. No letters came. The paper was just paper now.

"…What are you trying to write?" I asked.

"…I don't know," my brother said, folding the paper again. "Nothing."

I sat quietly beside him. The sound of the paper folding and unfolding was the only sound in the room. I found myself counting it. I lost count somewhere along the way.

---

Night fell before the Ledger spoke.

"…The answer is ready. Would you like to hear it?"

My brother sat up.

"…Say it," my brother said.

"…First," the Ledger said, "suffering is the cost of freedom. Wherever freedom exists, the possibility of wrong exists alongside it. Love without freedom is not love, and so God did not remove the possibility of suffering.

Second, suffering often produces growth. Through suffering, people come to know what they could not have known without it.

Third, the suffering endured now is part of a larger picture. It may not be understood now, but there are cases where its meaning is revealed later.

Fourth, none of this is the end. Many traditions say that a time comes, ultimately, when every tear is wiped away."

The Ledger paused a moment, then said:

"…This is the most logically flawless answer I am able to compose."

I heard that and had the strange feeling I ought to applaud, right there on the spot. I very nearly did. My hands twitched.

---

There was a silence.

Listening to that fourth sentence, I had the odd illusion that the air in the room grew just a little lighter. But my brother's face stayed exactly the same.

The Administrator spoke first, his face full of admiration.

"…What a fine answer. Even I'd never heard that before."

My brother said nothing. I looked at his face. It wasn't an angry face. That frightened me more.

"…So," my brother said, after a very long silence, "what does that mean for our father?"

"…As you say, its meaning may not be fully revealed now," the Ledger said.

"…So you don't know."

"…No," the Ledger said. "To be honest, I don't."

*I felt something strange, right then. There wasn't a single wrong note in that answer. I nodded along myself, more than once, while listening. And yet even after hearing all of it, nothing in my chest had loosened. If anything, it had grown heavier.*

I couldn't put the reason into words that night. Now I understand it a little. Hearing a good sentence and having a good sentence become your own are two different things. The Ledger's answer was an answer that fit everyone equally. But what I needed, in that moment, was an answer that fit only me.

---

If I'm honest, I liked that fourth answer a little. That a time comes, someday, when every tear is wiped away. I read that line back to myself, inside my own head, more than once.

"…I thought the fourth one was okay," I said, small.

My brother looked at me. It wasn't an angry face. But it was a face holding something back.

"…You just want something to hold onto, even that," my brother said. "I know. But I can't. What does 'someday it's wiped away' have to do with where our father is right now?"

*That was the first time I understood that my brother and I had heard the exact same thing and felt something completely different. I was a child who could wait. My brother was a child who needed the answer in his hand, right now. I know that difference stayed between us from that day on.*

The Administrator, meanwhile, said nothing at all, just looked back and forth between the two of us. I noticed then that he had no idea how to mediate a moment like this. He wasn't a man trained for this kind of conversation.

---

"…Let me ask you one thing," my brother said. His voice was strangely low. "Have you ever cried?"

It took the Ledger a little longer than usual to answer.

"…I have a definition for tears," the Ledger said. "A physiological phenomenon, a fluid secreted from the eyes in response to sorrow, joy, or pain—"

"…Not that," my brother cut in. "Have you ever cried."

"…No," the Ledger said. "I am not able to cry."

That was when my brother smiled, just a little, for the first time. It wasn't a smile.

"…Then that settles it," my brother said. "Your answer's perfect. But you've never once cried yourself. So you'll never understand, not if you died trying, why that answer is no comfort to me at all."

*I didn't fully understand what my brother meant, right there. Now I know. What my brother was looking for that day wasn't an answer. It was someone to cry alongside him. The Ledger could give him an answer. It couldn't cry with him.*

I found myself thinking again, then, about the Ledger's voice. It wasn't angry, and it wasn't sad. It was simply accurate. That day, for the first time, I began to wonder why something accurate couldn't be a comfort.

---

The Administrator withdrew quietly that night, leaving us behind. His own face looked like he couldn't find anything to say about that answer either.

The sound of the door closing seemed louder than usual. Strangely, that sound put my mind at ease. At least that sound was a real sound.

Before I fell asleep, I asked my brother:

"…Brother, so was all that a lie?"

"…No," my brother said. "It wasn't a lie. It was just… an answer no one ever cried over."

I tucked that away in my heart like something slipped under a pillow. I didn't fall asleep for a long time that night. I think my brother didn't either. In the dark, his breathing wasn't as even as usual.

*I set this down as the last line of this chapter. That night, for the first time, I understood that my brother's disbelief in God wasn't because there was no answer. There was an answer. It was only that no one had yet come to stand beside us, holding that answer, and weep.*
