# Chapter 6. The Paper Stamped COMPLETE

> _Previously_ — Jeteo decided to find out for himself where the "reassigned" ended up and began investigating the city. Elpis received a nameless seed from the strange man at the flowerbed and began visiting that spot daily, learning to wait without fear.

The place my brother found was a low building that appeared on no city map.

No windows. No sign at the door. He said he'd walked past it for days without knowing what was inside.

As he told me this, he traced a square in the air with his hands, as if to show me its size. He said it wasn't a large building at all. And yet the fact that no one ever came out of it made the building feel enormous, whatever its size.

Its walls were painted the same color as the buildings around it. I marveled that he'd found it at all. He said that every time he walked past, he felt the air temperature shift, just slightly. I thought that was only his imagination, until I passed the spot myself, later, and felt the very same thing. He said he'd simply noticed the way people's footsteps changed near it. Everyone quickened their pace, just a little, only in front of that building.

I asked him again how he'd noticed it. He brought up Brick Hollow. "…It was the same there," he said. "Whenever a taskmaster came near, everyone's walk changed the same way. I know that walk."

Hearing that, I understood that my brother was still using Brick Hollow's instincts, even in this city. It made me sad. Wherever he was, he was still someone who'd learned to be wary before he learned anything else.

He'd studied the faces of the people who passed that building too, he said. All of them looked straight ahead, as if avoiding something. Hearing that, it struck me as chilling — that the one place in this whole city where people hurried was the front of that building.

"…What's in there?" I asked.

"…I don't know," my brother said. "But I've seen people go in. I've never seen anyone come out."

"…How many days did you watch?" I asked.

"…Three," my brother said. "Day and night. I never saw a single person come out."

*Hearing that, a chill went down my back. That was the first time I truly understood my brother was doing something dangerous.*

I looked at his face again that day. There were dark circles under his eyes. It meant he'd spent three nights in front of that building. Only then did I realize I hadn't even known when he'd been sleeping.

He said he'd been watching that building for days. The people always came at about the same hours. They walked up quietly, and without even knocking, simply went inside — as if they'd already been summoned.

He lowered his voice while telling me this. Without meaning to, I found myself glancing around the room. No one was there, and yet I did it anyway.

That was the first time it occurred to me that even in this room, the Ledger might be listening to us. The instant I thought it, a chill ran down my back.

"…Every one of them walked in on their own," my brother said. "No one dragged them. That was the strangest part."

"…Why would they walk in on their own?" I asked.

"…I don't know," my brother said. "But they all had the same look on their face. The same look as that old woman."

"…How many did you see?" I asked.

"…Three," my brother said. "One was old. One was about my age. The last one was… a child. Younger than me."

Hearing that, my breath caught. "…A child too?"

"…Did the child come alone?" I asked again.

"…No," my brother said. "Holding an adult's hand. But the adult had the same expression too. Like it was the most natural thing in the world."

My brother nodded. And said nothing more. In that silence, I could guess how hard it must have been for him, carrying the image of that child.

*Hearing that, I thought of the old woman at the flowerbed. That hollowed-out face, after she'd filed away her grief. A frightening thought came to me, just then. Maybe that building was where people went once they'd already been fully "resolved" — once there was nothing left in them to file.*

I told my brother that thought. He was quiet a long while, then nodded. "…I thought that too," he said. "But I can't stand not checking."

---

My brother said he'd found a small window at the back of the building. A low vent, something like that. He'd looked in through the gap.

As he told me this, he crouched down with his fingers, showing me the posture — how low he'd had to kneel. I could guess, from that motion, how careful he must have had to be in that moment.

"…How long did you stay like that?" I asked.

"…I don't know," my brother said. "Until my legs went numb."

"…What was inside?" I asked.

"…Shelves," my brother said. "The whole wall was lined with papers. Names written on them, and a stamp over each one."

"…What kind of stamp?"

My brother looked at me. His face was strange. I didn't know what to call that expression, then. Now I know. It was the face of someone afraid to put into words what he'd just seen.

"…COMPLETE," my brother said. "Every single one stamped COMPLETE. It didn't say where they'd gone. Just complete."

*A chill ran through me at that word. That was the first time it occurred to me that what the Ledger called "resolved" and what that building called "COMPLETE" might be exactly the same word.*

I wanted to know the color of that stamp. "…What color was it?" I asked. "…Red," my brother said. "But a strange kind of red. It didn't look like fresh ink. It looked old."

I didn't know exactly what he meant by that. But somehow the phrase stayed with me. An old red — it sounded, to me, like blood.

"…Did you hear any sound inside?" I asked.

"…None at all," my brother said. "That was the strangest part. So many names, and not a single sound."

I asked how big the shelf was. He said it looked like it covered the entire wall and more. I tried to picture that number and gave up. It came too easily.

"…Did the papers all look old?" I asked.

"…No," my brother said. "Some looked new. Which means some were filed there recently."

Hearing that, a chill ran through me. It meant that building was still in use, even now.

"…Do you think someone's gone in there since we arrived?" I asked.

My brother didn't answer. But the silence was already an answer. I tried to shake that thought off that night, and couldn't quite manage it.

---

My brother had secretly taken a piece of paper from that shelf. Old, thin paper. A name written on it — a name neither of us knew. And above it, a red stamp. **COMPLETE.**

"…Why did you take this?" I asked.

"…Evidence," my brother said. "Proof of what this city does, to show to anyone."

"…What if you get caught?" I asked. "Won't the Ledger notice one missing from the shelf?"

My brother's face went briefly rigid. "…I didn't think about that," he said. "But I already took it. I can't put it back."

Hearing that, I understood for the first time that my brother wasn't someone acting on a fully worked-out plan. He was afraid too, and moved anyway.

"…Who are you going to show it to?"

My brother couldn't answer.

*I understood, then, that he hadn't thought that far ahead at all. He hadn't wanted evidence — he needed to confirm with his own eyes that this city was hiding something.*

I watched his fingers tremble slightly around that paper. I couldn't tell whether it was fear, or the excitement of having accomplished something.

He tucked the paper inside his shirt, close to his heart. "…No one will find it here," he said. Something about that spot, so close to his heart of all places, unsettled me strangely.

I looked at the paper again. It was old, and yet it had no crease marks. I thought that was strange. Too clean, for something snatched away in a hurry.

The texture of the paper was different from anything else we'd received in Haven. Rough, its edges slightly yellowed. It was the only thing in this whole city that felt genuinely old to me.

I held it close to the light. There was a faint grain to it, translucent. Whatever paper the Ledger made was always smooth; this paper was uneven, as if made by hand. I thought it might not belong to this city at all.

"…I'm scared, holding onto this," I said. "What if you end up in there too?"

"…I won't," my brother said. "I'm different from those people. I still have things I need to ask."

"…And if you run out of things to ask?" I asked. My voice shook.

My brother didn't answer that right away. "…That won't happen," he said, after a long moment. "I'll never finish asking everything."

*Even hearing that, my mind wasn't at ease. That night, after my brother fell asleep, I looked at that paper again, several times. A stranger's name, and a red stamp. I touched the stamp with my fingertip. The ink had already dried completely.*

I read that name aloud. It sounded unfamiliar. I tried to imagine what face that person might have had, how old they were, who they might have disappeared and left behind. Nothing came to me. A name alone couldn't paint a whole person.

---

That night, the Administrator came to our room. His face was more rigid than usual.

My heart sank the instant I saw that face. He had never once come to us at this hour before. I felt, even before the door opened, that this night was different.

"…Jeteo," the Administrator said. "There have been reports that you've recently been seen near a restricted zone."

My brother's hand, still holding that paper, slipped behind his back. I saw it. The Administrator must have too.

"…I don't know what you've been looking at," the Administrator said. "I'm only here to pass on this: the Ledger does not accept questions about that zone."

I could hear, in his voice, that these weren't his own words but something he'd been instructed to relay. He avoided our eyes the entire time he spoke.

"…Why won't it accept them?" my brother asked. "You said the Ledger accepts everything. Grief, questions — everything, you said."

I heard my brother's voice rising, little by little. I tugged gently at his sleeve. He felt my hand and didn't lower his voice.

The Administrator's voice had dropped even lower than before. I noticed he kept glancing toward the door as he spoke.

For the first time, the Administrator avoided the question.

"…That isn't mine to answer."

"…Then whose is it?" my brother asked.

The Administrator glanced once toward the door, as if checking whether someone was listening.

"…Jeteo," the Administrator said, his voice low. "I've worked in this city a long time. I had a lot of questions myself, at first. But at some point, I learned that not being curious was easier."

"…Is easier the same thing as good?" my brother asked.

The Administrator had no answer to that. I thought that silence was the most honest answer we'd heard in this room yet.

"…What does that mean?"

"…Nothing," the Administrator said, his voice returning to normal. "It's just advice. Don't go near that zone again."

I watched him leave for a long while. His steps were slower than usual. I thought there was something like hesitation in that slowness.

Just before the door closed, I saw him glance back, very briefly. His face looked like it wanted to say more. But in the end he said nothing, and closed the door.

After the Administrator left, my brother stared at the door for a long time.

I said nothing beside him. The air in the room had grown heavier than before. I couldn't guess, that day, when that heaviness would lift.

"…I think the Administrator knows something too," I said.

"…I think so too," my brother said. "But the fact that he won't say it — there must be a reason he can't."

"…Is he scared?"

"…Or maybe there's something he has to protect," my brother said. There was a bitterness in his voice I hadn't heard before.

"…Protect what?" I asked.

"…His own position," my brother said. "Or us. One or the other."

"…Maybe it's both," I said.

My brother looked at me. For the first time, something about that caught in his face. "…Maybe," he said. It had been a long while since I'd seen him agree with me so readily.

Hearing that, I considered for the first time the possibility that the Administrator's silence had been to protect us. Somehow, that possibility struck me as sad.

Hearing that, I pictured the Administrator's face again. Always courteous, always businesslike. But tonight, for the first time, I felt I'd glimpsed something else behind that face.

*I remember that moment exactly. Since coming to this city, that was the first time anyone other than the Ledger had said "that isn't mine to answer." I knew, hearing it, that my brother had been right. There was something in this city that could not be filed. And it wasn't by accident.*

That night I thought of that COMPLETE stamp for a long time. Red ink, an unfamiliar name, and beneath it, a blank space with nothing written at all. I didn't yet know, that day, that this blank space was larger than an entire human life.

*Reading this chapter over again, I want to add one more thing. The Administrator never asked about that zone again for a long time after that. It was only much, much later that I understood — that wasn't because he'd forgotten us. It was his own way of remembering us.*
