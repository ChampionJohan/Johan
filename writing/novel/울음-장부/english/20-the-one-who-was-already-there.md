# Chapter 20. The One Who Was Already There

> _Previously_ — Speon revealed the truth of the Completion House. A completed person comes out alive through a well, but loses their name and the faces of those they loved along with their most painful memory. Hope that Father might be alive, and terror that he might not remember them, arrived together.

That night I barely slept.

I lay under my blanket, but my eyes kept opening. I could hear the wind striking the stone wall beside the well — a sound I hadn't heard in daylight. Listening to it, I thought that this well wore a different face at night.

I turned onto my side and looked toward the well. In the dark I could just make out the faint outline of the stone wall. My eyes kept drifting to it, at the thought that Father might really be somewhere inside it. It would have been another well, not this one, of course — but even so, looking at this well, I found myself thinking of him.

Neither did my brother. I heard him fold and unfold that paper, over and over, all through the night.

The paper had gone thin along every crease. Just from the sound alone, I felt I could tell exactly which fold he was touching. That short rustle went on, at a steady interval, all through the night. I thought it sounded like my brother's own heartbeat.

*I understood then that we were afraid of the same thing, but afraid of it differently. My brother was afraid of not finding Father. I was afraid we would find him and he wouldn't know us.*

Somewhere off, a night bird cried once, briefly. When it stopped, the silence closed back in, complete. In that silence I felt as though my brother and I were the only two awake in the whole world — as if, while everyone else slept, only the two of us were holding on to the same fear, each in our own way.

I closed my eyes and tried to picture Father's face. Strangely, it wouldn't come clearly. I remembered the shape of his eyes, but the shape of his nose had gone blurry. That blurring frightened me all at once. Were we already starting to forget him too? I pushed the thought away quickly.

After a long time turning restlessly, I opened my eyes a little and looked over at my brother. He was sitting in the moonlight with the paper spread on his knee. He didn't seem to be reading the letters on it. He was just running his fingertips over it, again and again. I thought that gesture looked like someone touching Father's face.

Toward dawn, my brother spoke quietly.

"…Elpis, if we find him and he doesn't recognize us… will you still stay beside him?"

I couldn't answer right away. Only after thinking for a long time did I speak.

"…Yes," I said. "We can just tell him his name again. Even if it takes time."

At that, my brother let out a breath of relief for the first time.

"…Thank you," he said. That was all. But I could feel that those two words carried far more than themselves.

He reached over and gave my head a brief stroke. It was an awkward gesture. He'd never been someone who was good at that kind of thing. But in that moment, it came naturally. I felt, in that touch, that he was making a promise — to me, and probably to himself as well.

"…Brother," I said, quietly.

"…Yeah."

"…Can I ask you something too?"

"…Go ahead."

"…If we never find him," I said, "will you keep looking anyway?"

He said nothing for a long while. I thought he'd fallen asleep.

"…Yes," he finally said. "There's nothing else I know how to do."

*That answer was sad, and yet strangely reassuring. My brother wasn't searching because of hope. He was searching because he knew no other way to go on living. I didn't know, then, that this too was a kind of hope.*

After that we lay side by side without speaking anymore. The sky was slowly growing lighter. Watching that dawn light, I felt afraid of what else this night might teach us, once it ended.

---

In the morning, Speon asked.

He had gotten up before us and was building the fire back up. There was practice in the way he warmed water and set breakfast out for us. I tried to guess how many mornings like this he must have repeated, and gave up. It was a number beyond counting.

The morning air was cold. I felt the stiffness in my body as I threw off the blanket and stood — proof that I'd slept on hard ground. And yet, strangely, I had slept deeply. I thought I might even have slept more deeply than I ever had in Haven's soft beds.

*I felt again, in that moment, that comfort of the body and comfort of the heart didn't always travel together. On that hard ground, I had heard news about Father, and had my first real conversation with my brother. Not one of Haven's beds could have ever given me a night like that.*

"…What will you do now?"

"…We need to find another well," my brother said. "Do you know of one?"

"…I've heard of a few," Speon said. "But I haven't been able to check them all. I can't leave this place empty for long."

"…Why not?"

"…Because someone might come out while I'm gone," Speon said. "That person comes out remembering nothing at all. If no one's here then… they'll just wander this Wilderness alone."

*Hearing that, I understood why Speon had kept this place so long. He wasn't looking for his own father. And still, he waited for someone every single day.*

I thought that reason might, in its own way, be harder than searching for our own father. Searching for Father gave us a clear purpose. Speon had no purpose like that at all. He had only this — that someone had to be there.

"…So does that mean you can't leave even for a day?" I asked.

"…I can leave for a few days," Speon said. "But not long. If someone comes out and there's no one here, they don't even know where they are, and they end up wandering alone until they're lost. That's what I'm most afraid of."

"…Have you seen someone like that?" my brother asked.

Speon nodded. He said nothing more. I decided not to ask what memory that silence was holding.

His eyes seemed, for a moment, to be looking at something very far away. I felt, in that instant, that Speon was standing here in front of us and, at the same time, somewhere else entirely — some other time, some other place. Memory seemed able to put a person in two places at once, like that.

As if to change the subject, Speon got up and walked toward the well. He lowered the bucket again as he spoke.

"…So a day became two days, and two days became a month, and that's how I ended up staying here. At first I only meant to stay a little while."

"…How long has it been?" I asked.

Speon started to count on his fingers, then gave it up.

"…If it were something I could count, it wouldn't have felt this long," he said.

The dull thud of the bucket striking water rose up from far below. Speon waited for that sound before he began winding the rope back in. I noticed that even though he could probably have done it without waiting to hear it, he chose to wait anyway.

"…I feel like you'd know without listening for it," my brother said.

"…I do know," Speon said. "But I listen anyway. If I wind it in without listening, it just becomes a chore."

*I thought about that a long while. Speon was working, every day, not to let anything he did at this well become merely a chore. The same bucket, the same rope, the same water, every single day. And every single time, he listened for that sound as if it were the first.*

---

"…So does that mean you never find anyone?" I asked. "If you only ever stay here?"

Speon smiled a little. A lonely smile.

"…I already found the person I was looking for," he said. "Now my work is helping other people find what they're looking for."

*I didn't know exactly what he meant. But something in the way he said it told me Speon had once been like us.*

I looked at Speon's hand. It was the hand holding the bucket's rope. There was an old scar on the back of it. I wondered where it had come from. Like the scar on my own hand from Brick Hollow, I thought it must be carrying some story of its own.

Speon took a sip of water. The morning sun fell across the side of his face. I tried to guess his age from that face and failed. In some moments he looked young. In others, he looked like someone who had lived far longer than that.

"…Did you used to look for someone too?" I asked.

"…No," Speon said. "I didn't go looking. If anything, I ran. Because waiting frightened me. So now I'm learning how to wait."

"…Is waiting something you have to learn?" I asked. It seemed like an odd question, but I was genuinely curious.

"…Yes," Speon said. "I thought waiting just meant staying still. But it isn't that. Waiting is something you have to decide to do again, every single day. Waiting yesterday doesn't mean you'll wait today, on its own."

*I didn't understand that exactly, either. But I saw my brother go silent at those words and look at Speon for a long time.*

I felt, in that silence, that my brother was asking himself something. He had always been someone who moved forward. Waiting must have been a foreign idea to him. That day, for the first time, I watched him handling that foreign idea carefully, turning it over.

I felt, in that moment, that something was passing between my brother and Speon that I didn't know. Both of them had once run. One had stayed here, learning how to wait. The other was only just beginning to walk.

"…Do you regret running?" my brother asked suddenly.

Speon paused a moment at the question.

"…I don't regret it," he said slowly. "But I do think I should have stopped sooner. Running becomes a habit at some point. You keep running even when there's no reason left to."

My brother turned that answer over for a long time. I could tell he was hearing it as if it were about himself.

"…You ran too, though," I said carefully. "From Brick Hollow."

He looked at me. He looked startled.

"…That's true," he said slowly. "But I always thought that wasn't running away. I thought it was going toward something."

"…Do you still think that?" I asked.

He was quiet a long while before he answered.

"…I don't know," he admitted. "Maybe it was both."

Speon, listening to that exchange, spoke quietly.

"…Running and going forward start out as the exact same step," he said. "What tells them apart comes later — the moment you decide whether to keep taking that step, or to stop."

"…Then I still haven't decided," my brother said.

"…It's all right to take time deciding," Speon said. "It took me far longer than it's taking you."

Listening to that exchange, I felt that Speon wasn't trying to give my brother an answer — he was only standing beside him. I thought it was something like what Noa had done for us. The people of this Wilderness all seemed to treat each other this way. Not with answers, but by giving up their own side to stand beside you.

---

As he prepared to leave, my brother asked.

"…Have you ever seen a strange man around here? Working at a flowerbed, or… somewhere no one planted anything."

Speon stopped walking at that.

I saw his back go slightly rigid in that instant. The hand packing his bag had stopped too.

"…What does he look like?" Speon asked. His voice had changed.

My brother described him. Worn clothes, soil-stained hands, the habit of never giving his name.

Speon was quiet for a long time.

"…I've seen him too," he said. "Here, and elsewhere. But every time, I thought it was just coincidence."

"…When did you see him?" my brother asked.

"…The first time was a very long time ago," Speon said. "Back then I thought he was just a passing traveler. Then again before I came here, somewhere else. And here, a few times as well."

Listening, I felt a strange chill run down my spine. Until then I'd thought of the man at the flowerbed only as a stranger we'd happened to cross paths with. But looking at Speon's face, I understood it went further than that.

"…Do you know his name?" my brother asked.

"…No," Speon said. "I asked once. But he never answered. He just smiled."

That question felt strangely familiar to me. I felt as though I'd brushed past a smile like that somewhere before — in Brick Hollow, inside Haven, even somewhere along this road here. I couldn't remember exactly where.

"…What kind of smile was it?" I asked.

Speon thought for a moment.

"…Sad, and gentle, all at once," he said. "It's hard to explain. You'd have to see it yourself to understand."

*Hearing that, I tried to picture the smiling face of the man at the flowerbed. I couldn't remember it exactly. But the moment Speon said it, something in me stirred with a strange familiarity.*

"…What did he do?" I asked.

"…Nothing at all," Speon said. "He just was there. But strangely, wherever he'd been, something always looked different afterward."

"…What kind of different?" I asked.

Speon looked toward the well.

"…Once, I'd already packed everything to leave this place," he said. "That day, he came by and asked for a cup of water and drank it, and left. He said nothing at all. But after he'd gone, I found myself unpacking everything I'd just packed."

"…Why?" my brother asked.

"…I don't know," Speon said. "That's the strangest part of it."

My brother stood there a long while even after his pack was ready. I could see he wanted to ask more and was holding himself back. It was probably about the man at the flowerbed. But looking at Speon's face, he seemed to decide not to press it further.

Before we left that place, I looked once more at Speon's face. He had already returned to that familiar expression — the face of someone waiting for something. I recognized it as the very same face we'd seen the moment we first arrived. In the space of a single day, he had gone back to his place, and his face.

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. For the first time, I understood that the man at the flowerbed had passed through Brick Hollow, and Haven, and this well in the Wilderness, all of them. That night, I began, faintly, to feel that it was no coincidence at all.*
