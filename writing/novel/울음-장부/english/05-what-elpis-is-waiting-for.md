# Chapter 5. What Elpis Is Waiting For

> _Previously_ — The Ledger gave a logically flawless answer to "why does God allow suffering," but it did nothing to ease Jeteo's grief. "Have you ever cried?" — the Ledger said it could not. That day, Elpis understood: her brother didn't disbelieve in God for lack of an answer, but for lack of someone to weep beside him.

My brother spoke less, after that day.

I soon realized that silence wasn't like the silence he'd carried in Brick Hollow. There, when things were hard, he shut his mouth, but his body still always leaned a little toward me. In this city, both his body and his heart were drifting from me, little by little.

He ate. Whatever the Ledger set out, he forced himself to eat it. But he never spoke first. That frightened me more than anger would have.

I tried, more than once, to start conversations myself. Small things, like "…What did you do today?" or "…Want more food?" He answered in short words and fell quiet again. I gathered those short answers up, one by one, and got through the days on them.

In those days I studied my brother's face often, without his noticing. Even in his sleep, the crease between his brows never smoothed out. I thought that even while he slept, he was fighting something.

Sometimes he murmured in his sleep. I held my breath and strained to listen. Most of it I couldn't make out. Once, just once, I thought I heard the word "…Father." I couldn't be sure.

*I didn't know, then, that my brother had made up his mind about something. Now I know. That night, he decided to find out exactly where "Reassignment" took people. Instead of a God who gave no answers, he'd decided to go find the answer himself.*

I should have recognized that resolve in his face right then. Looking back now, my brother's eyes had already changed, that very morning. Something harder than grief had settled into them.

He asked every person he met on the street the same quiet question. "Do you know where people who are reassigned go?" Most walked on as if they hadn't heard. Only one person stopped.

That person didn't look very old. Their clothes weren't worn like ours, but clean. Only their eyes carried the look of someone exhausted.

I saw that person hesitate, glancing at us for a few steps before they stopped walking. That very hesitation told me they were someone who might actually answer. In this city, someone who knew nothing at all didn't hesitate in the first place.

"…That's not something you file," that person said, low. "Even asking is… a strange thing to do here."

"…Why?" my brother asked.

"…Because no one asks," they said. "Here, you don't learn to find someone who's disappeared. You learn to forget the space where they used to be."

"…So you've forgotten someone too?" my brother asked.

Instead of answering, they looked away. It was only an instant, but I could see in that look that my brother's question had landed exactly. Their mouth opened, then shut again — as if swallowing back a name.

"…I'm sorry," they said. "I can't say any more about this here. The longer you talk about it here, the easier it is to get filed."

My brother said nothing to that. But from that day on, he began disappearing more often, and coming back later.

They hurried off after saying that, not once looking back. I saw fear in that retreating figure. A face frightened just from answering a single question.

My brother watched the direction they'd gone for a long while. "…You saw that?" he said to me. "That person knows something too. And won't say. Everyone in this city is like that."

"…That's just because they're scared," I said.

"…That's exactly what's strange," my brother said. "No one will even say what it is they're scared of."

Hearing that, I thought of Brick Hollow. There, the thing to fear had always been clear — the taskmasters, the whip, the beating that came for a quota unmet. Here, whatever was frightening had no shape I could see. I realized that day, for the first time, that this was more frightening still.

---

In those days I went back often to the flowerbed.

The man was still tending the empty bed, working the dirt with his hands. At first I only meant to walk past. My feet stopped on their own.

"…Hello," I said.

He looked up. His face wasn't unfamiliar. I didn't know why.

His clothes were worn, and yet clean. I thought that was strange. If he'd handled dirt this long, his clothes should have been stained with it by now. His collar was as white as the first day I'd seen him.

"…Hello," he said. "This is the spot that old woman's daughter planted."

"…So why haven't you planted anything here yet?" I asked.

"…You can plant something," he said. "But a seed planted by someone who's forgotten a name doesn't grow well. A seed remembers the hand that calls its name."

*I didn't understand a word of that, at the time. Now I know. He wasn't talking about the old woman.*

"…Can you call its name, then?" I asked.

He looked at me a moment. "…I'm not the one who makes names," he said. "But a name that already exists — I can call that."

His hands were covered in dirt, and yet even under his fingernails they looked clean. I thought that was strange, and then let the thought go. I didn't have the patience, just then, to dwell on small things like that.

I looked at his hands again. There was callus there. Hands that had worked long. And yet even that callus looked somehow soft. I had never seen hands like that.

---

"…Are you from here?" I asked.

"…No," he said. "I do this kind of work wherever I go. Being in the place no one has planted."

"…What's your name?"

He looked at me and smiled, just a little. It wasn't a sad smile.

"…No one's ever asked," he said. "You're the first."

*My heart raced strangely, just then. In this city — no, from Brick Hollow all the way until now — no one had ever said to me, "You're the first." I had always been second, always after my brother.*

In Brick Hollow, too, I had always been called only "Jeteo's little sister." The only ones who ever said my own name were Mother and my brother. I had never once thought that was a sad thing. I had simply taken it as how things were.

Hearing his words, I couldn't say anything for a moment. I didn't know whether it was shame or gladness. I just tucked the words away quietly, somewhere inside myself.

He began working the dirt again. I watched his hands move for a long time. He never hurried. Only then did I realize that unhurriedness was something I hadn't seen anywhere else in this city.

Each time his fingers turned the soil, they paused, just once — as if careful not to hurt something alive down inside it. I thought that care looked exactly like the way you'd handle a person.

The whole space around that flowerbed felt strangely quiet. A few steps away you could hear the city's low murmur, but here even that sound faded away. I couldn't tell whether that was because of this man, or because of this dirt. Even the smell of the soil was different, just at this spot. Elsewhere the dirt smelled of dry dust; the soil in this flowerbed smelled like the air after rain.

"…How long have you worked here?" I asked.

"…A long time," he said. "The more a city forgets to call names, the more of this kind of work there is. So I come here often."

"…What changes, when you call someone's name?"

He looked quietly at the dirt in his hand before he spoke.

"…Nothing might change at all," he said. "But knowing there's someone who calls your name, and believing that no one ever will — those are entirely different lives."

*I didn't understand even half of that, at the time. But strangely, for as long as I listened to him speak, the quiet of this city didn't frighten me at all.*

"…Who calls your name?" I asked.

He stopped his hands and looked at me. For the first time, his answer was a little slow in coming.

"…Someone does," he said. "Has, for a very long time."

He drew a small seed from the dirt and set it in my hand.

"…Want to plant it?" he asked. "You don't have to give it a name. Just plant it, and that's enough."

I pressed the seed into the soil. Nothing in particular happened. It was just dirt.

I was a little disappointed by that. I'd expected some light to shine, or some sound to come. Since arriving in this city, I'd grown used to everything happening the instant I wished for it. But a seed didn't move that way.

The seed was small and hard. I rolled it in my palm a moment before I pressed it into the dirt. That brief moment was the quietest moment of my whole day.

As I covered the seed with soil, I tapped the dirt twice with my fingertip. I don't know why I did that. It just felt like the right thing to do.

*I didn't know it, leaving that spot that day. That from then on, I had begun waiting for something. Not Father. Not Mother. Something I couldn't quite put a name to.*

He rose, brushing the dirt from his hands. "…Will you come again tomorrow?" he asked. "…Yes," I said. The answer came out without hesitation. I was surprised by that myself.

Before I left, I pressed my palm against the dirt once more. Nothing happened. And yet I carried that spot with me as I walked away. From that day on, my days began, little by little, to be rebuilt around that one place.

I went there every day after that. Days passed, and the seed sprouted nothing. It was just dirt. Even so, I went every day. My brother never asked where I kept going off to. He didn't seem to have room to ask.

Every morning before I went, I washed my hands first. I knew it was a strange order — washing my hands clean before going to touch dirt. But I wanted the walk to meet that seed to feel, at least a little, like something set apart.

Some days I took a long way around on purpose. Passing through other parts of the city, I took in people's faces, one by one. They all wore roughly the same expression. It wasn't an unkind expression. It simply wasn't any expression at all.

Once, the man wasn't there. I sat alone in front of the empty flowerbed and waited a long while. No one came. And yet I didn't dislike that waiting. It was a different kind of waiting from the waiting in Brick Hollow, or the waiting for the Ledger's answer in Haven. It wasn't a waiting spent dreading something.

The sun went down unusually late that day. I spent that whole long afternoon crouched at the edge of the flowerbed, watching the shadow move across the dirt. It shifted direction little by little, slowly sliding past the spot where I'd planted the seed. Watching that shadow, I thought that time simply moved differently, in this one place.

I sat there a long while that day with my fingers buried in the dirt. It was cooler beneath the surface than above it. I liked that coolness. It struck me as strange, only later, that I could sit there doing nothing at all and never once feel bored.

*I set that difference down here as I understood it then. I wasn't waiting out of fear that something wouldn't come. I was simply waiting with a heart that would welcome it, if it did.*

The wind seemed to blow a little differently, just over that flowerbed. It may only have been my imagination. But every time I felt it, something in me strangely eased. I still don't know the exact name for that ease, even now.

Every time that wind came, I closed my eyes and stayed there a little longer. I knew it was already past the time I should have gone home. But in that moment, I didn't want to hurry.

---

That evening the Administrator glanced into our room for a moment. Seeing my brother wasn't there, he seemed about to ask something, then looked at me and swallowed the words instead. I didn't understand, at the time, what that hesitation meant.

He stood a moment longer at the door. "…Does your brother go out often, these days?" he asked. I didn't know what to say, so I only shook my head. He turned away with a face that showed neither relief nor disappointment at that answer.

I kept looking toward the door long after he'd gone. Only then did I notice, for the first time, that the Administrator too was afraid of something. It felt strange to me that even someone who trusted the Ledger could carry a fear of his own.

My brother came back late that night. His hands were covered in dirt.

There was a lot of dust on his clothes too. Seeing it, I understood he'd been crawling through some narrow space. The fabric at his knees was slightly torn.

"…Where did you go?" I asked.

"…I'm looking for the road they take the reassigned people down," my brother said. "It's not on any map of this city. But they take them somewhere. That much is certain."

Hearing that, I looked down at his feet again. The wound looked deeper than I'd expected. That day, for the first time, I nearly told him to stop. The words rose to my throat, and I swallowed them back down.

"…Why are you looking so hard?"

He looked at me. His eyes wavered, just slightly, for the first time that day.

"…If God won't save him," my brother said, "then I will. Or at least I'll find him."

Hearing that, I looked at his eyes again. They weren't angry eyes. They were the eyes of someone who had made up his mind. That resolve frightened me. At the same time, I thought — that's exactly like my brother.

Having said that, he sat down beside me and took off his shoes. There was a small wound on the sole of his foot. It looked like something he'd gotten climbing over a wall. Even seeing that wound, I couldn't say anything. It was already done.

I touched the wound lightly with my fingertip. He flinched. "…Does it hurt?" I asked. "…No," he said. I didn't believe him. But I didn't press him that night.

He lay down and stared at the ceiling for a long while. "…Elpis," he said. "…Yes?" I answered. "…Never mind," he said. "I just felt like saying your name." I only understood later that this was the kindest thing he said to me all that day.

*I set that down as the last line of this chapter. My brother decided to stop waiting for an answer. I decided, that same day, to plant a seed and simply wait. I didn't know, then, that we had begun walking in exactly opposite directions.*

*Reading this chapter over again, I want to add one thing. At the time, it looked like opposite directions. Now I know. We were searching for the same thing. Only the way we searched was different.*
