# Chapter 5. What Elpis Is Waiting For

> _Previously_ — The Ledger gave a logically flawless answer to "why does God allow suffering," but it did nothing to resolve Jeteo's grief. "Have you ever cried?" — the Ledger said it could not. That day, Elpis understood that her brother's disbelief wasn't because there was no answer, but because there was no one to cry alongside him.

My brother spoke less, after that day.

He ate. He forced down whatever the Ledger set out for him. But he never spoke first anymore. I found that more frightening than his anger.

I took to studying my brother's face on the sly, those days. Even in his sleep, the crease between his brows never smoothed. I thought he must be fighting something even while he slept.

*I didn't know, then, that my brother had made up his mind about something. I know now. From that night on, he decided to find out exactly where "Reassignment" led. Instead of a God who gave no answer, he decided to find the answer himself.*

My brother started quietly asking everyone he met on the street: "Do you know where reassigned people go?" Most pretended not to hear and kept walking. Only one person stopped.

That person didn't look old. Their clothes weren't worn thin like ours, either — they were clean. But their eyes alone belonged to someone exhausted.

"…That's not something you file," the person said. Their voice was low. "Even asking about it is… strange, here."

"…Why?" my brother asked.

"…Because no one asks," the person said. "Here, you don't look for someone who's disappeared. You learn how to forget the empty place where they were."

My brother said nothing when he heard that. But from that day on he vanished more and more often, coming back later each time.

The person hurried off after saying that to my brother. They never once looked back. I saw fear in that retreating back. A face that was afraid, just from answering one single question.

---

I went to that flowerbed often, in those days.

The man was still there, tending the soil of that empty bed. At first I meant to just walk past. But my feet stopped on their own.

"…Hello," I said.

He looked up. His face wasn't unfamiliar. I didn't know why.

"…Hello," he said. "This was the spot that old woman's daughter planted."

"…So why haven't you planted anything yet?" I asked.

"…You can plant," he said. "But a seed planted by someone who's forgotten their name doesn't grow well. A seed remembers the hand that calls its name."

*I didn't understand any of that, at the time. Now I know. He wasn't talking about the old woman.*

His hands were caked with dirt, and yet the skin beneath his nails looked somehow clean. I thought that was strange, and then let the thought go. I had no room, in those days, to linger long over small things like that.

---

"…Do you live here?" I asked.

"…No," he said. "I do this kind of work wherever I go. Being in the spot no one has planted."

"…What's your name?"

He looked at me and smiled, just a little. It wasn't a sad smile.

"…No one's ever asked me that," he said. "You're the first."

*My heart raced strangely then. In this city — no, from Brick Hollow up until now — no one had ever said "you're the first" to me. I had always come after my brother.*

I couldn't say anything for a moment after that. Whether it was from embarrassment or gladness, I didn't even know. I just quietly tucked the words away in my heart.

He started tending the soil again. I watched his hands move for a long time. He never rushed. I realized only then that his unhurriedness was something I'd never once seen in this city.

"…How long have you worked here?" I asked.

"…A long time," he said. "The more a city forgets to call names, the more there is for me to do here. So I come here often."

"…What changes when someone calls your name?"

He looked down at the soil in his hand for a moment.

"…Sometimes nothing at all changes," he said. "But knowing there's someone who calls your name, and believing that no one ever will — those are two completely different lives."

*I didn't understand half of what he said, at the time. But strangely, while I listened, this city's quiet wasn't frightening at all.*

He drew a small seed out of the dirt and set it in my hand.

"…Want to plant it?" he asked. "You don't have to give it a name. Just put it in the ground."

I pressed the seed into the soil. Nothing much happened. It was just dirt.

The seed was small and hard. I rolled it once in my palm before I set it into the ground. That brief moment was the quietest moment of that whole day.

*I didn't know, leaving that spot that day, that I had begun waiting for something. Not Father. Not Mother. Something I couldn't put a name to, exactly.*

I pressed my palm once more into the soil as I left. Nothing happened. But I kept carrying that spot with me as I walked. From that day, my days began, little by little, to be woven again around that place.

I went there every day after that. The seed sent up no shoot, days on end. It was just dirt. Still I went every day. My brother never asked where I went so often. He didn't seem to have room to ask.

Some days, on my way there, I'd take a long way around on purpose. Passing through other parts of the city, I took in people's faces, one by one. They all wore something like the same expression. Not a bad expression. Just no expression at all.

Once, the man wasn't there. I sat alone in front of that empty flowerbed and waited a long time. No one came. And yet I didn't dislike that waiting. It was a different kind from the waiting in Brick Hollow, different from the waiting for the Ledger's answer in Haven. It wasn't a waiting afraid that something wouldn't come.

*I set the difference down here, this way. I wasn't waiting for fear that something wouldn't come. I was simply waiting with a heart that said: it's all right if it comes.*

The wind seemed to blow a little differently, just over that flowerbed. It might have been my imagination. But every time I felt it, something in me settled, strangely. I still don't know the exact name of that settledness.

---

That evening, the Administrator looked in on our room for a moment. Seeing my brother wasn't there, he seemed about to ask something, then saw me and swallowed the words. I didn't know, then, what that hesitation meant.

My brother came back late that night. His hands were caked with dirt.

"…Where were you?" I asked.

"…Looking for the road they take reassigned people down," my brother said. "It's not on any map of this city. But it definitely leads somewhere."

I heard that and looked at my brother's feet again. The scrape looked deeper than I'd thought. That was the first time I almost told my brother to stop. The words rose to my throat, and I swallowed them back down.

"…Why do you have to look this hard?"

My brother looked at me. His eyes wavered, just a little, for the first time that day.

"…If God won't save him," my brother said, "then I will. Or at least I'll find him."

Having said that, he sat down beside me and took off his shoes. There was a small wound on the sole of his foot. It looked like something from climbing a wall. I saw the wound and couldn't say a word. It was already done.

*I set this down as the last line of this chapter. My brother decided not to wait for an answer anymore. I decided, that day, to plant a seed and simply wait. I didn't know, then, that we had begun walking in exactly opposite directions.*
