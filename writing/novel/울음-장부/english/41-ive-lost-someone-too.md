# Chapter 41. I've Lost Someone Too

> _Previously_ — For the first time, Jeteo went to find the man at the flowerbed on his own. To the question "Why can't you still trust me?", Jeteo gave, for the first time, his real reason — "If I trust you and it ends up like it did with Father again… I really don't think I could bear it this time."

I still remember the air of that afternoon. It was warm. But Jeteo's voice made that air feel heavy.

I had thought I knew Jeteo's fear. But hearing that word from Jeteo's own mouth, that day, was a completely different thing.

Up until that moment, I had only ever thought of him as a person with too much anger in him. Only after hearing those words did I understand. There had always been fear underneath the anger, first. I was frustrated with myself for not noticing that sooner.

I had never once hated Jeteo. But I had often mistaken myself for knowing him completely. That mistake was, right then, the thing I felt most ashamed of.

Jeteo's words seemed to hang in the air. I could tell, just from the tremor in his voice, how long they had been held down.

Hearing that, I thought again of our last night in Brick Hollow. The night Jeteo shouted at the sky — I had thought, then, that he was only angry. Thinking of it now, the Jeteo of that night must already have been carrying this same fear. I hadn't been able to read it, then.

He heard Jeteo's words and fell silent for a long time.

*I watched that silence from a distance. I knew it wasn't a dismissive silence. It was a silence weighing something.*

I held my breath and waited to see how long that weighing would last.

Evening was drawing near. The sky had begun to turn a pale orange. I watched that light touch Jeteo's face. His expression was far more open than usual.

The tree's shadow stretched long, all the way to their feet. Watching that shadow's edge lengthen little by little, I gauged how fast the evening was coming. A wait that would normally have bored me wasn't the least bit boring, that day.

The well's bucket rope swayed lightly in the wind, creaking. That sound seemed unusually loud to me.

A bird cried somewhere far off. That cry seemed strangely unbothered by the moment, I thought. The world went on turning as usual. It felt like only inside us was time flowing differently.

I stood a little apart. Speon quietly came and stood beside me. He said nothing. Both of us just watched that spot.

I heard Speon's breathing beside me, slow. I tried to steady my own breath to match it. But it didn't work well. In the end I simply chose to hold my breath instead.

The wind stirred the trees by the well. Jeteo endured that silence and stayed right where he was. He didn't flee. That alone told me how far Jeteo had come.

I saw that Jeteo's feet didn't move at all this time. In the old days he would already have turned and walked off. Watching those feet, I understood how many moments Jeteo had learned to endure.

I saw his toes pressing slightly into the dirt. As if trying to anchor himself to that spot. Watching that, I felt both pity and pride.

Only then did I realize my own hand was gripping the hem of my clothes tightly. I'd been tense without knowing it.

*I understood, then, for the first time — I was as afraid of this moment as Jeteo was. If Jeteo broke, I felt I would break too.*

I didn't want Jeteo to know that fear. So I hid my clenched hand behind my back. I knew Jeteo had no room, in that moment, to look after me too. That was mine to bear alone.

Just then, Speon, without a word, brought his own hand behind my back. And laid it over my hidden hand. He didn't look at me. He kept watching Jeteo.

*I was startled by the weight of that hand. I hadn't told anyone. But Speon already knew. I learned, then, in my own body, for the first time — that the person beside you knows even without being told.*

"…I know that feeling," he finally said.

The answer felt so simple that it struck me as strange. Jeteo must have thought so too — his brow creased slightly.

"…How would you know that?" Jeteo asked. Still edged.

I heard the sharpness still left in Jeteo's voice. But under that sharpness, I felt, for the first time, that a real question was there too.

There was no anger in the man's face. Watching that expression, I thought he seemed like someone who had already taken in Jeteo's sharp words countless times. Not that he wasn't hurt — he was someone who looked, first, past the tone to what lay behind it.

I studied his expression more closely. There were very faint lines at the corners of his eyes. I couldn't tell if those lines came from age, or from laughing a long time, or from grieving a long time. Maybe all three.

Watching him, I thought of when we'd first met Father. Father, too, must have had someone who accepted him, angry as he was, exactly as he was, for us to have gotten this far.

Thinking that, I felt oddly at ease. We weren't the only ones who needed a place like that.

Thinking that, I suddenly looked at the man again. His eyes were still fixed on Jeteo. I didn't know how to describe the temperature of that gaze. Just calling it warm wasn't enough. It was something very old, a gaze that never tired.

I wondered, suddenly, in that moment. Had he too once sat in front of someone this angry, sometime? I didn't know the answer. But the composure remaining in his face felt less like something he was born with, and more like a composure practiced countless times.

---

He said nothing for a while. In that silence, I felt he was trying to bring out something he'd long kept pressed down. The wind at the well stirred the leaves again, once more.

I felt that this silence was a different kind from before. Before, it had been a silence weighing Jeteo's weight. This time it felt like a silence weighing his own. For the first time, I felt that he too was enduring something.

"…Because I've lost someone too," he said.

Jeteo's face showed surprise at that.

I saw Jeteo's shoulder flinch in that moment. Clearly an unexpected answer. Jeteo had always seemed to think he would dodge the answer. But he hadn't dodged — he had offered his own story first.

I watched Jeteo's expression change. He had always been someone who thought his own grief was the biggest in the world. But in that moment, for the first time, Jeteo was facing someone else's loss.

Jeteo's lips moved. He started to say something, then closed his mouth again. I felt, watching that, that Jeteo was, for the first time, thinking of the other person before his own question.

"…What did you lose?"

Jeteo's voice still trembled. But something different from before was mixed into it now. Not a probing question — a genuine desire to know.

The question hung in the air for a moment. I couldn't tell if he was avoiding the answer, or truly choosing one.

He looked off into the distance for a moment.

I watched his eyes. Looking into the distance, those eyes held something that seemed less like sorrow than an old resolve.

*In that moment I thought of the things we had lost. Mother. The old man who'd fallen in Brick Hollow. People we'd parted from without even knowing their names. I hadn't known, before that, exactly how heavy the word loss could be.*

I counted those names one by one in my mind. Partway through, I stopped counting. At some point, counting stopped meaning anything. I felt, then, for the first time, that loss doesn't come with a number attached. Losing one thing could weigh as much as losing a hundred.

I had never once thought that weight could be shared. I had only ever known that weight was something you carried alone. In that moment, for the first time, I felt that thought might be wrong.

"…It would take a long time to explain," he said. "But I can tell you one thing. I won't promise I'll never lose someone again. That's a promise I can't keep. But I can promise one thing. When you lose someone, I won't leave you alone."

*Hearing those words, tears came, strangely. I didn't know, then, why he hadn't told me it wouldn't hurt. Now I know. He wasn't someone who gave false comfort.*

Wiping my tears, I looked at Jeteo. His eyes were red too. Neither of us could quite explain why that sentence shook us so much.

I thought, in that moment, of all the things the Ledger of Haven had always said to us. "This will be resolved shortly." "We apologize for the inconvenience." I realized, then, that no matter how polite those words were, none of them had ever shaken my heart the way this one short sentence had.

The Ledger had never once told us it had lost something. Of course. The Ledger had never lost anything. Comfort offered by a being who has never lost something slips somewhere, no matter how precisely worded. That day I understood, for the first time, exactly what that slipping was.

"…Won't you tell us who you lost?" Jeteo asked.

He closed his eyes for a moment and opened them again. Even in that brief moment, something heavy seemed to pass through him.

"…Someday," he said. "Not now. Right now is your story's time, not mine."

Jeteo nodded. He didn't ask further. I thought the way Jeteo accepted that answer was something I could never have imagined before.

The old Jeteo would have pressed again and again, right there. Why not now, when would it be. But Jeteo just nodded and waited. I felt that waiting wasn't resignation — it was the beginning of trust.

*I liked that answer, strangely. I felt, then, for the first time, that he wasn't trying to cover our grief with his own story.*

I thought, in that moment, of the Ledger of Haven. The Ledger always gave the answer first. This man gave his own silence first, instead of an answer. I began, faintly, that day, to sense that this difference was the most important difference in the whole book.

The Ledger already had an answer the moment a question came in. This man seemed to be searching for something within himself only after hearing the question. I thought that difference wasn't a small one. One was an answer prepared in advance; the other was a weight carried together, in that very moment.

I looked at Jeteo in that moment. He didn't press further. The old Jeteo would surely have asked again. I felt that Jeteo was learning how to stop himself.

I glanced up slightly at Speon's face. Something like an old grief seemed to pass over it.

"…Is this the first time you're hearing this, Speon?" I asked Speon in a whisper.

"…No," Speon said. "I already know. But it's just as heavy every time I hear it."

"…How do you bear it?"

"…I don't bear it," Speon said. "I just stay beside it."

Hearing that answer, I felt I understood again, why Speon had been able to keep this well for so long. Not enduring — staying beside. I held that difference close to my heart.

"…Speon, have you lost someone too?" I asked quietly.

Speon glanced down at me for a moment.

"…Everyone's lost something," Speon said. "That's why they gather here."

Hearing that, I looked around again at the people gathered at the well. Everyone here was someone who had lost something. But that loss wasn't tearing people apart — instead, it was gathering them into one place.

I thought of Haven. That city had people who had lost things too. But they didn't know each other. Each complaint was handled only at its own window. I saw then, for the first time, how differently things turn out when you let people process loss alone, versus when you let them share it.

*I understood, that day, for the first time, that what made this well-yard was not the water but this gathering. Maybe the water was only an excuse. What people truly needed was to be beside someone who had lost something too.*

---

Jeteo said nothing for a long while after that. I knew he was choosing his next question. It was a question he'd held in for a very long time.

I endured that silence, fingering the hem of my clothes. I couldn't tell how much time had passed. But strangely, I wasn't bored. I felt that the waiting itself carried some meaning.

Speon quietly shifted his seat beside me. A little closer to me. I felt comfort in that small movement.

The sky was turning steadily red. Watching that red light touch Jeteo's face, I knew he was finally about to bring out his heaviest question.

"…Then why did it happen to our father like that?" Jeteo asked. His voice trembled. "Why didn't you stop it?"

"…I can't give you that answer either," he said. "If I could give it, I would have already."

Jeteo's shoulders shook. Watching that shaking, I clenched my fist hard enough for my nails to dig into my palm.

"…Can no one give an answer?" Jeteo asked again. His voice had gone ragged.

"…I can't either," he said again. "But not being able to give an answer doesn't mean I'm giving nothing."

"…Then what can you give?"

"…This, right now," he said. And he held out his hand. "Sitting beside you."

Jeteo looked at that hand for a long time. From a distance, I saw his shoulders shaking.

I stopped breathing entirely in that moment. Time seemed to move very slowly. I watched Jeteo's hand hover in the air for a moment. In that short instant, it seemed like months, no — years, were held whole.

The wind stopped for a moment. Even the bucket rope, even the leaves, didn't stir, in that moment. I thought the world itself seemed to be holding its breath with us.

My brother took the hand.

*I set down that answer as the last line of this chapter. I knew, then. The answer this whole book had been looking for had never existed as a sentence. That answer had taken the shape of the hand he held out. And that day, my brother answered by taking that hand.*
