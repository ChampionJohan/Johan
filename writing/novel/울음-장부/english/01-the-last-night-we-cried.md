# Chapter 1. The Last Night We Cried

Mother did not cry as she folded the paper and pressed it into my brother's hand.

The kiln fire was almost out by then, which made the room feel quieter still. Mother's hands never trembled once, the whole time she folded that paper. I watched them for a long while. The tips of her fingers were cracked.

White dust had dried into every crack in her fingertips. I wanted to scrape it out with my fingernail. My hands wouldn't move. A couple of bowls sat by the kiln, glowing faintly in the dark. I didn't know yet that I would never see them again.

I thought that was strange — that Mother didn't cry. In Brick Hollow, everyone cried. They cried lining up at the kiln each morning, and they cried under the whip each evening when the day's quota went unmet. Mother alone never cried. I had heard so much weeping in my life that at some point I stopped being able to tell whether a sound was crying or simply the noise the valley made. Even at night, some sound of sniffling drifted in from somewhere. I used to fall asleep to it, like a lullaby.

"…Once you cross the boundary stones, open it," Mother said. "Not before."

"…Why not before?" I asked.

Mother looked at me for a moment. Instead of answering, she smoothed my hair back once. Her hand was cold.

I wanted to hold onto that hand. I thought if I held it, Mother might come with us. But her hand had already turned back to the paper. I left mine in my lap. Somewhere in the kiln, ash settled with a small sound.

"…Your father just told me to do it this way," Mother said. "That was all."

My brother didn't even look at the paper. He shoved it into his waistband. My brother's name is Jeteo. Mine is Elpis. He was sixteen. I was thirteen.

*I remember that night exactly. It was the last night our family was ever all together. I have decided to begin this record there.*

Brick Hollow was a valley for firing brick, exactly as its name said. Every morning the taskmasters set that day's quota. It was always a little more than the day before. No one asked why. Asking earned a beating.

*I had never seen outside that valley. For thirteen years I had seen nothing but sky, kiln, and mud. I did not truly understand, until that night, that an outside even existed.*

The kiln smoke always drifted the same direction. I never once wondered where it went. There was no reason to wonder. In Brick Hollow, to wonder about something was already to be in danger.

The quota the taskmasters set was posted on the wall each day, in numbers. I couldn't read those numbers. My brother could, but he never told me what they said. I only learned why much later. He wanted to keep me — only me — from knowing how much they climbed, each time. Standing under that number and studying my brother's face was the thing I did every morning. If his face went hard, it meant the day's quota had gone up. I couldn't read numbers, but I could read my brother's face.

---

The paper looked like a page torn from an old Bible. Dried wax clung to one corner.

The edges were worn soft from having been folded and refolded many times. I thought that showed how long Father had carried it, pressed against himself. The wax mark stayed hard no matter how hard I pressed it with my finger. I put my nose to it. There was no smell at all. I think I had hoped some trace of Father's scent might still be caught there.

"…What is it?" I asked.

"…Something your father had," Mother said. "I never opened it either."

My brother snorted when he heard that.

"…If it's from Father, forget it," he said. "Father left us."

I didn't know what to say to that. Whenever Father came up, it was always the same for me. I couldn't take my brother's side, and I couldn't take Father's either. I just felt like I was standing somewhere between the two of them.

*I remember those words exactly, even now. By then my brother had already stopped believing in God. To be precise — he could no longer believe the story Father used to tell us, that God was watching over us. If that story were true, nothing that had happened to us made any sense.*

Father was dragged off by the taskmasters on the night he came up short of three thousand bricks. No one ever saw him again after that. The taskmasters said he had been "reassigned." No one knew exactly what that meant. No one had ever met a single person who came back to explain it.

I remember that night. Father's hands had been unusually slow that day. I didn't know why, at the time. Looking back now, I think Father already knew something, that day. He hugged me once. Longer than usual.

Even after Mother pressed the paper into my brother's hand, she didn't let go of it right away. At the time I thought that was only because she didn't want to let go of his hand.

Mother looked at my brother's face for a long time. It was the face of someone about to say something.

"…Jeteo."

"…What."

Mother opened her mouth, then closed it again.

"…Never mind," she said. "Go and live. That's all that matters."

Having said that, she turned toward me as well. "Elpis."

"…Yes," I said.

"…Stay by your brother's side," Mother said. "No matter what happens."

Mother's voice cracked, just slightly, in that moment. I didn't miss it. She went back to her usual voice again quickly, but I kept that brief crack in my memory a long time. It was only much later that I began to think Mother might have been crying too, somewhere inside herself.

I nodded. I didn't know then how heavy a request that was.

*I still don't know, to this day, what Mother meant to say and didn't. My brother says he doesn't know either. Somewhere in this record, I want to find that sentence someday. For now I only set the silence down here.*

---

We walked to the end of the valley that night.

The wind was cold. I was barefoot. The dirt against the soles of my feet was colder than the brick fired that day. I liked that coldness. Cold things were rare in Brick Hollow.

The kiln was always hot. The fire, the brick, even the night air held onto the day's heat. That night, I thought, was the first time in my life I had ever touched something truly cold.

There was no moon. My brother carried me on his back. I drifted in and out of sleep against him. Every time I opened my eyes it was the same darkness, the same smell of mud.

My brother's back wasn't wide. And yet I never once worried about being shaken loose from it. He hitched me higher with every step. I matched my breathing to that rhythm.

"…Brother," I asked. "Where are we going?"

"…I don't know," he said. "Somewhere better than here."

"…What about Mother?"

He didn't answer.

*I thought about that silence for a long time afterward. I didn't know it then — that he didn't know it either. That Mother wasn't coming with us all the way to the boundary stones, and that he himself was only just then learning it.*

Other families walked ahead of us and behind us. No one spoke. Now and then a child began to cry, and just as quickly a hand clamped over its mouth. I didn't find that sound frightening. At the time I only thought it was normal.

That night was the first time I had ever seen this many people walking together at once. In Brick Hollow, people never gathered like this except lining up before the kiln. I thought it was strange, the way all those footsteps blurred into one sound. It was as if the whole valley itself were walking.

A girl walking ahead of us kept looking back. She looked about as young as I was. Each time, her mother pulled her along by the hand. I wondered what she'd left behind. There was no time to ask.

I tried to see whether she was holding a brother's or a sister's hand, the way I was, but there was only her mother beside her. I remember thinking, just then, how fortunate I was to have my brother's hand in mine. It's a shameful thing to admit. But it's true.

An old man fell beside us. He had worked the kilns for thirty years and walked with a limp. No one stopped. My brother didn't stop either. I watched that old man's back over my brother's shoulder for a long time.

"…Brother." I said it into his back. "That old man."

"…I know," my brother said.

"…Aren't you going to help him?"

He didn't slow his pace. I felt his shoulders rise and fall, just slightly. One deep breath.

"…I can only carry one," he said.

The old man tried to push himself up from where he'd fallen. I watched his hand press into the dirt. His knuckles were thick and twisted. Hands that had worked mud for thirty years. I still haven't forgotten that hand.

*I am trying to set this scene down without shame. We left that old man behind. I know it wasn't my brother's fault. And still, I carry that image of his back with me even now.*

---

At the end of the valley stood a low row of stones, set there by someone long, long ago. Boundary stones. Beyond them lay land no one had ever gone into.

The moment my brother, carrying me, first saw those stones, his steps slowed — just for an instant. I felt his back go rigid. He was afraid too. I pressed my face a little closer against it, just then.

Beyond the stones, the sky was growing faintly brighter, little by little. At the time I thought it was dawn. I would learn later. It wasn't the light of morning.

Everyone ran at once. My brother took my hand and ran. I fell. He pulled me up. Behind us came the crack of whips and shouting. I still don't know exactly what those sounds were. I don't want to know.

Someone brushed past me. I never saw who it was. Gravel skittered under my feet. I fell and struck my knee hard against the ground. Even now, that ache sometimes brings the whole of that night back to me.

"…Don't let go," my brother shouted as he ran. "Don't let go of my hand."

*I will write that sentence down many times in this record. Don't let go. It's the sentence my brother said to me more than any other. He didn't believe in God — but that night, I understand now, he was asking someone to hold onto him. It just happened to be me.*

I didn't let go. I held on so hard my palm split open. He held just as hard. Even in that moment, strangely, I remember the exact warmth of his hand. It was a cold night. His hand alone was hot.

The instant we crossed the boundary stones, he pulled the paper from his waistband.

"…You said open it once we cross," he said. His voice shook. He was angry. "So I'm opening it. There's nothing in it, I promise you."

He unfolded the paper.

---

There was writing on it. Old printed type.

*"I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows."*

My brother read the sentence aloud. Then he laughed. It wasn't laughter.

"…Did you see?" he said to the sky. "Surely seen. Surely… heard. So why didn't you do anything? Our father was reassigned. We left that old man back there. So what exactly did you see?"

The edge of the paper began to grow warm. I saw it. He saw it too.

*I mark that moment as the beginning of this record. I watched my brother's anger turn to light and spread. I saw it happen for the first time that day.*

---

The light spread across the whole page, and where the paper had been, a door opened.

Calling it a door felt strange. It had no frame, no handle. Light had simply folded itself, at some point, into the shape of a door. All I knew was that the inside of it wasn't dark.

My brother shouted "Wait—" I didn't let go of his hand. My feet lifted off the ground.

The door made no sound when it closed.

---

We landed on a stretch of grass.

No boundary stones. No crack of whips. No smell of mud. It was a quiet morning. Birds were singing. That birdsong frightened me more than anything — everything was too quiet.

My back ached. The shock of the fall was still in my body. I reached back to touch it, and realized my brother was already checking me over. His hand swept down my arms a couple of times — making sure nothing was broken.

The grass was wet. I pressed my palms into it and felt the damp of it. Brick Hollow had no grass like this. What little grew there was always dry.

The air had no smell at all. That was the strangest part. Brick Hollow always smelled of something — mud, smoke, people. Here there was nothing, and for a moment I thought my nose had simply stopped working.

*Even now I remember that first morning by its smell. By the smell that wasn't there.*

My brother sat up first. He looked around.

"…Where are we?" he asked.

A building stood in the distance. Low and white. A sign hung above it.

Trees stood in a neat row around the building — nothing like the trees I knew from Brick Hollow. Their leaves hadn't dried; every one of them was green. I didn't understand, then, why that green looked so unfamiliar to me.

We had to get close before we could read it.

**HAVEN.**

"…What's Haven?" I asked.

My brother looked down at the paper still in his hand — empty now, the light and the letters both gone from it.

"…I don't know," he said. "But I'm not going to believe it. Whatever it turns out to be."

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. That's what he said. I took his hand again then. He didn't let go either.*

*As I begin this record, I want to note one thing. I haven't cried since that night. Neither has my brother. We both believed that night was our last time crying.*

*I would learn later that what we shed that night wasn't tears. We had already been children who'd stopped crying, long before we ever left Brick Hollow. What happened that night was only our last chance to be angry.*

*I will also set down here why I decided to keep this record. My brother isn't a person who writes anything down. I thought someone had to preserve what happened to us. I still don't fully know why that someone turned out to be me.*

*We were wrong. This record is the story of that wrongness.*

*I read back over this first chapter once I finished writing it. I don't want to judge the two of us that night unfairly, just because I now know things we didn't, then. We were afraid, that night, as fully as we knew how, and we held each other's hands as fully as we knew how. I still believe, even now, that much of it was right.*
