# Chapter 31. Humming

> _Previously_ — When Father woke in the depths of a violent confusion, the man from the flowerbed appeared again and calmed him. Jeteo asked him outright who he was, and got only: "I've always been here. You just didn't recognize me."

The man was gone the next morning, without a word.

I don't know when he left. Once, waking in the middle of the night, I'd seen him clearly, sitting across the fire. The next time I opened my eyes, he was gone.

The instant I woke, I looked first at the spot where he'd sat. Only the grass, faintly pressed down, remained. Not even a clear footprint. The trace was so slight it made me doubt whether he'd really been there at all.

I touched that pressed patch of grass. It still felt faintly warm, or maybe I only imagined it. I couldn't be certain.

Morning mist hung low. Dewdrops glinted on every blade of grass. I noticed, belatedly, that there was no dew at all in the exact spot where he'd sat. I thought about mentioning it to my brother and decided against it. It seemed like it would only make him angrier.

My brother stared hard at that spot for a long while, then finally turned away. I felt that he was angry at the lack of any trace, and at the same time, hoping for something. Hoping that next time, a little more would be left behind.

"…Gone again, just like that," my brother said, his voice hollow. "Every time. Shows up, then no explanation at all."

"…Couldn't even say goodbye," my brother went on. "Could've at least said where he was going."

"…There wasn't time to ask," I said.

"…He didn't give us time to ask," my brother said.

I nearly laughed at that. When my brother was angry, his words got precise.

But even after saying that, he stood there a long while. I understood he wasn't standing there out of anger. He was waiting, in case the man came back. Neither of us said that out loud.

My brother repacked his things twice that morning. I understood he wasn't packing so much as looking for something to do with his hands.

*I had nothing to argue against what he'd said. But strangely, his vanishing felt different to me from being abandoned. I couldn't explain exactly why, not then.*

I thought about that difference all through the morning. Someone who leaves you behind, and someone who leaves once their work is done. The two look the same from the front, but different from behind. I had never once seen him leave with the look of someone in a hurry.

Father didn't even remember that the man had come at all. That grieved me. The voice that had calmed us so completely the night before — Father himself didn't carry so much as a trace of it.

"…Did someone come last night?" Father asked over breakfast, as if it were nothing.

My brother and I exchanged a glance. Neither of us knew how to explain it.

"…Yes, briefly," I managed to say.

"…Someone you know?" Father asked.

"…No, a stranger," my brother answered for me. His voice had a slight edge.

"…A stranger, and he came at night?" Father asked again.

My brother struggled to find words for a moment.

"…That's the part I find strangest too," my brother finally said.

"…I see," Father nodded. He didn't press further. I found that indifference a relief, and grieving, both at once.

Father tore off small pieces of stale bread and ate. It was hard to believe those were the same hands that had pushed us away in fear the night before. Between the Father of daytime and the Father of night, there seemed to be no connection at all.

Crumbs fell onto Father's knee. He picked them up one by one with his fingertips and ate them. That ordinary little gesture was so plain that, strangely, it felt more unfamiliar to me than anything.

I studied Father's face for a long time that morning. There was a slight hollowness under his eyes. His body remembered last night. Only his mind did not.

*I thought, then. Perhaps there are things that stay even when memory doesn't. Like that song.*

---

That afternoon, Father hummed something low as he walked.

Sunlight fell in mottled patches through the tree shade. A gentle breeze blew. It was an ordinary afternoon. I think that sound came through so clearly precisely because it was such an ordinary afternoon.

We walked without much talk that day. Everyone was a little worn from the night before. There was nothing but the rustle of dry dirt underfoot and, far off, a bird's call.

In that quiet, Father's voice leaked out very softly. It wasn't words. There were no lyrics. Just a melody.

The moment I heard that sound, I stopped walking. I knew that melody. It was the one Mother used to sing to put us to sleep.

In Brick Hollow, Mother had always paused in the same place in that song. Whether it was because she ran short of breath, or because the song itself was written to pause there, I don't know. Father paused in exactly that same spot too.

*I heard that breath and understood. Father wasn't remembering the song. He was remembering the room. The room where Mother sang, where we lay down, where Father stood by the door.*

"…Father, what song is that?" I asked urgently.

Father stopped humming and looked at me. His face was startled.

"…I don't know," Father said. "It just… came out on its own."

Father touched his own mouth, smiling in wonder. It seemed strange even to him, that his body could remember something before his mind did.

"…Father, just a little more," I said. I found I'd caught his sleeve without meaning to.

Father looked down at my hand. He didn't look startled.

"…I'm sorry," Father said. "I didn't even know what I was singing."

"…Nothing to be sorry for," I said quickly. "There's nothing at all to be sorry for."

*My heart felt like it would burst in that moment. I looked at my brother. His face showed he'd caught the melody too.*

Some of the color had drained from my brother's face. I couldn't tell if he was glad or frightened. Probably he didn't know either.

My brother had stopped walking and couldn't say a word. I saw his lips tremble slightly. He looked like someone who wanted to sing along with that melody, and was holding back for fear something might break if he did.

---

"…Can you keep singing?" my brother asked. His voice shook.

Father tried to hum again. But it broke off after a few bars.

"…I don't remember what comes after," Father said, an apologetic look on his face.

His eyebrows drew together faintly. The expression of someone trying to grasp something that kept slipping through his fingers. I found watching that expression more painful than watching the night he'd screamed.

Father went back to the beginning several times and started over. Each time he stopped at the same place. As if the road itself broke off there.

"…This is as far as it goes," Father said. "There's nothing past this."

I felt that this wasn't only about the song.

My brother quietly came and sat beside Father.

"…It's all right," my brother said. "Maybe it'll come out again later."

Father looked at my brother and gave a small nod. I felt my brother was saying that to himself, as much as to Father.

*My brother must already have known then that there was no guarantee it would come out again next time. And still he said it. I only understood later that this wasn't a lie — it was another name for waiting.*

A strong gust of wind blew through and passed. The branches knocked together and made a brief clatter. I waited for that sound to settle and said nothing.

I got up quietly and walked off a little way from the two of them. I needed time to steady myself. As I walked, I looked up at the sky. A pale daytime moon hung faint above.

I looked at that daytime moon and, in my mind, sang the song from the beginning. It came out to the end without a single break. I felt sorry for that. That I knew all of it, while Father knew only half — it felt, strangely, like my own fault.

*I understood then, for the first time, that memory isn't fair. The same people had been in that same room. And still, what remained to each of them, in what amount, was different.*

"…It's all right," I said. Tears came. "That's enough."

I picked up the rest and sang it, exactly as Mother used to. Father closed his eyes, listening to that sound.

My voice shook. My pitch wavered more than once. I sang it through anyway. My brother came quietly beside me and started humming along. Three voices — or more exactly, two voices and Father's silence — blended together, right there.

As I sang, I watched Father's face. His mouth wasn't moving. But his fingers were tapping very lightly against his knee. The beat was exact.

*I watched that finger for a long time. It was the same hand that had counted numbers. That day, that same hand was counting a beat.*

"…Strangely, it makes me feel at ease," Father said. "Even though I don't know whose song it was."

"…It was Mother's song," my brother said quietly. The first piece of information he'd offered up first, on his own.

Father opened his eyes and looked at my brother.

"…Mother," Father repeated the word slowly. "There was someone like that."

"…What kind of person was she?" Father asked.

My brother couldn't answer for a long moment. I understood he wasn't struggling for words — he was gauging where to start without falling apart.

"…Her voice was low," my brother finally said. "And she didn't laugh often, but when she did, she laughed for a long time."

"…Sounds like she was a good person," Father said.

"…Yes," my brother said. "A good person to you, too."

Father tilted his head slightly at that, but didn't ask further.

*Hearing that, my chest ached. To Father now, Mother was only "someone like that." And yet, even at those words, there was some kind of longing in his expression. He might not know it in words, but somewhere, his heart seemed to know.*

In that moment, I thought of the room back in Brick Hollow, on the last night, where Mother had sung us that song as we fell asleep. The low lamplight, Mother's voice and Father's breath side by side in that room. I felt that memory completing itself again, right here, right now.

*In that moment I understood again why I was still keeping this record. This song, this moment — these were the things I would give back to Father someday, when he came fully home.*

That night I tried to write the melody down on paper. But I didn't know how to put a tune into words. In the end I just wrote: "Today, Father hummed Mother's song. He couldn't finish it. But he sang."

Under that sentence I added one more line. "Someday, when Father reads this paper, even if he doesn't remember today, he'll know that today happened." Writing that line, I felt, for the first time, sure that this record wasn't only for me.

I folded the paper and pushed it deep into my pocket. The paper was already worn soft from being folded and unfolded so many times. I decided that the more those creases multiplied, the more days we'd walked together.

Father fell asleep unusually early that night. I couldn't tell if singing had worn him out, or if his heart had simply been at ease for once. I watched his sleeping face for a long time. Asleep, at least, Father looked like someone who hadn't lost anything at all.

I sat beside him and hummed that song once more, very quietly. I don't know if Father heard it. But his eyebrow twitched once in his sleep.

*I decided to count that twitch as an answer. That night, there was nothing else left to count.*

---

That night, my brother spoke to me quietly.

"…I still don't know," he said. "Who that man is. Whether I should believe in God."

The firelight's shadows flickered across my brother's face. I could tell he was choosing his words more carefully than usual.

The fire crackled small every now and then as it burned down. Each time, my brother would pause and then go on. I understood he wasn't startled by the sound — he was buying himself time to speak.

"…But?" I asked.

"…But today, listening to that humming, I thought — just for a moment — that maybe, like whatever's still left in Father, there's something left in me too. Something not completely erased."

I said nothing and just listened. I thought if I interrupted, he'd close back up.

"…I still don't know what it is," my brother went on. "But I can feel that it's there. Strange, isn't it."

"…Not strange," I said. "I have something like that too."

I really did feel that. I couldn't say exactly what it was. But that day, the certainty that something uneraseable existed inside me too felt unusually clear.

My brother looked at me. A little surprised.

"…What is it?" he asked.

"…I don't know either," I said, laughing. "But it's there."

My brother laughed out loud at that. It had been a long time since I'd heard him laugh.

The laughter faded and he went quiet again.

"…Elpis." My brother called my name.

"…Yeah."

"…I still hate him," my brother said. "I hated him last night too. Even watching him put Father to sleep, I hated him."

"…I know," I said.

"…But even hating him, I want him to come back," my brother said. "That's what makes me angriest."

"…Can you hate someone and wait for them at the same time?" I asked.

"…I don't know," my brother said. "But that's what I'm doing right now. Both of them are real."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I just leaned my head against his shoulder. He didn't push me away.

*Even now, thinking back on that night, that sentence is the first thing that comes to mind. Wanting him to come back, even while hating him. I think that was my brother's first prayer. He would never have called it that himself.*

The embers gave one last small pop and died down. I watched that small light go out, still leaning against my brother's shoulder. Neither of us moved until the darkness had covered us completely.

*I set that line down as this chapter's last line. I understood how enormous it was that my brother had said those words, out loud, to himself, and to me, for the first time.*
