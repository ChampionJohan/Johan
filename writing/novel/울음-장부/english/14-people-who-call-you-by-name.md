# Chapter 14. People Who Call You by Name

> _Previously_ — Outside Haven, in the Wilderness, the brother and sister learned real hunger all over again. Toward evening they found a small tent camp and received, for the first time, a share from strangers. "There isn't much, but we can share" — they sensed something in it that Haven's sufficiency never had.

The people sitting around the fire called each other by name.

It felt strangely foreign to me. In Haven, we had always been addressed with formal courtesy — "Jeteo," "Elpis," correct every time. Here it was just the name. Short. Close.

*Only hearing them call each other did I understand it. The Ledger called our names precisely, but there was always a distance in that calling. In the way these people called each other, there was no distance at all.*

The night air was still cold. I drew my knees up, wrapped my arms around them, and shifted a little closer to the firelight. Now and then I heard the crackle and pop of the embers. I had no memory of ever seeing a fire in Haven. Light there had always come from somewhere unseen, steady and soft — never something that leapt and shivered like this.

*Watching that flickering light, I thought about it. Haven's light had been steady. But steady and alive were two different things. That was the first time I felt that flickering might, in fact, be proof of being more alive, not less.*

I sat there and listened quietly to the people calling one another. Some names were called short and quick. Some were drawn out long. The same name sounded different depending on who was calling it. I found that strange. No matter who spoke to the Ledger, it answered in exactly the same tone.

A man far off called someone's name loudly. The name carried out into the dark, and after a moment, an answer came back. Listening to that call and answer, I felt a strange stirring in my chest. The call, the answer, and the empty stretch of time between them. Haven had no such empty stretch. The moment you called, an answer came at once. But that instancy never held whatever it was this waiting held.

*That was the first time I understood that waiting was part of a calling. Even in that brief silence before the answer came, something had been growing.*

An old man beside the fire called a child's name twice. The child pretended not to hear. He called again. Only then did she come running, laughing.

*Watching that small, playful back-and-forth, I felt strangely at ease. Calling a name, pretending not to hear it, calling it again — even in that exchange, something like affection was moving between them.*

The old man ran a hand lightly over the child's hair. She squirmed a little under his touch but didn't pull away. Watching that brief gesture, I found myself guessing how many times it must have been repeated before, to look so easy. Even a stranger's eye could see it was an old habit.

A child came up to me and asked.

"…What's your name, big sister?"

"…Elpis," I said.

"…Elpis." The child repeated it back, clumsily. But strangely, I liked that clumsy calling.

*For the first time, I understood that there was a temperature to being called by name. The Ledger's calling was precise but cold. This child's calling was clumsy but warm.*

"…What's your name?" I asked her back.

"…Rachel," the child said, and gave a small, half-shy smile.

"…Rachel," I said, saying it back.

She lit up at that and sat down close beside me, right against my side. I felt the warmth of that small body against my ribs. In Haven, no one ever sat that close.

Rachel's hair smelled of smoke — the smell of hair that hadn't been washed in days. But strangely, I didn't mind it. If anything, that smell felt like proof that this child was really living here, really beside this fire.

"…Where did you come from, big sister?" Rachel asked, her eyes wide and round.

"…From far away," I said. "A place called Haven."

"…Is it nice there?" Rachel asked.

I couldn't answer right away. Rachel tilted her head at my silence.

"…I don't know," I finally said. "But I think I like it better here."

At that, Rachel's face broke into a wide smile again, and she gripped my arm tight.

"…Say my name again, big sister," she said.

"…Rachel," I said again.

"…One more time."

"…Rachel."

She laughed every time. I didn't understand why saying the same name three times over made her so happy. But somehow, every time I said it, I liked it a little more too.

*I only understood later. What Rachel wanted wasn't an answer. It was to be called. The Ledger answered anything you asked it. But no one had ever simply called us, for no other reason than to call us.*

---

The woman's name was Noa. She was traveling with two children.

"…Where did you come from?" Noa asked.

"…From Haven," my brother said carefully.

Something shifted slightly in Noa's face.

"…You're the first person I've met who came out of there," she said. "Everyone else wants to get in."

Noa pulled both children a little closer against her knees. I could tell it was an unthinking gesture — that her body reacted before she did, the instant Haven came up.

Her two children looked back and forth between us, blinking. It wasn't a wary look, the kind you give a stranger — just a curious one. I liked that unguarded look. Haven's children never looked at anyone that way. Haven's children wore blank faces, as if they already knew everything there was to know.

"…This might sound strange," my brother said carefully, "but we think we're lucky to have gotten out."

"…It doesn't sound strange," Noa said. "Because I think the same thing."

"…Why did you leave?" my brother asked back.

Noa drew one of the children into her arms.

"…That city says it takes sorrow away. That's what frightened me," she said. "I was afraid that if the sorrow disappeared, everything about having loved that person would disappear along with it."

*Hearing that, I couldn't breathe. I thought of the old woman at the flowerbed. Noa was someone who had known, before it ever happened to her, what that old woman had gone through — and had turned away from it in time.*

I studied Noa's profile quietly. In the firelight her face was carved with deep lines. It didn't look like age alone had put them there. I tried, for a moment, to guess what nights each of those lines had come through, and then I stopped. It didn't feel like a depth I could reach by guessing.

"…Who did you lose?" my brother asked gently.

"…My husband," Noa said. Her voice shook, but she carried the sentence through to the end. "In Haven, this grief would already be resolved by now. But I still hurt this much. And I have no intention of trading this pain for Haven."

One of the children pressed her face into Noa's lap. Noa stroked the child's hair slowly. The gesture was practiced — she must have said this, in front of this child, more than once before.

*Watching that hand move, I understood that sorrow wasn't always something a person had to bear alone. Noa was carrying her sorrow, and still, right beside it, tending to a child.*

*Hearing her, I understood for the first time that keeping your sorrow could itself be a choice. Sorrow wasn't only something that had to be gotten rid of.*

My brother was quiet for a long moment before he asked.

"…Aren't you scared? Going on living like this, still in pain?"

Noa looked at him. Her eyes were soft.

"…I'm scared," she said. "But if the pain disappeared, I'm afraid that the fact I loved him would blur along with it. That's what scares me more."

*Hearing that answer, I understood for the first time that even pain had a strength that could stand guard over something. Until then I had only ever thought of pain as something you simply endured.*

My brother didn't say a word the whole time he listened. But I saw that his eyes never left Noa's face. I understood he was pressing what she'd said down somewhere deep inside him. That was how he took things in — not answering right away, but holding them a long time, and later pulling them back out as something of his own.

"…So what does that pain do for you?" my brother finally asked. His voice had a rough edge to it.

Noa looked at him a long time. She didn't grow angry.

"…It doesn't do anything," she said. "Pain doesn't do anything. It just keeps telling you that person was here."

My brother closed his mouth. I could tell he wanted to argue with that answer and couldn't find a way to.

*That was the first time I ever saw someone not answer my brother's question with logic. Noa gave him no flawless answer. And yet my brother went quieter, for far longer, than he ever had standing before the Ledger.*

Noa kissed the top of the child's head in her arms. It was a brief kiss. But I could feel the weight folded into it. In that moment I saw, for the first time, that love could be carried across without anyone having to say the word at all.

---

As the night deepened, the children sang. It wasn't good singing. The notes went wrong again and again. But everyone laughed and sang along anyway.

*Listening to that song, I felt something strange. Haven had no songs. There was no need to make something that wasn't perfect. These people knew their song wasn't perfect, and they kept singing it anyway.*

Rachel took my hand and begged me to sing along. I didn't know the words. But after hearing it a few times, I could follow along on the chorus. My voice went wrong just as often as everyone else's. No one seemed to mind at all.

*That was the first time in my life I ever sang. In Brick Hollow I hadn't had the strength to sing. In Haven I'd had no reason to. Here in the Wilderness, I seemed to have gained both things at once. The strength, and the reason.*

When the song ended, the children clapped — badly out of time. But everyone looked happy all the same. Rachel begged me to sing it again, and this time I followed the chorus with a little more confidence. My voice cracked, but no one laughed. If they had, it wouldn't have been the laugh of mockery — it would have been the laugh of joining in.

Partway through the song I heard my brother humming low under his breath. His voice was very quiet. But I didn't miss that small hum. I started trying to count how long it had been since I'd last heard him hum anything at all, and then I gave up counting.

Between verses, one of the adults laid a low countermelody over the top. The notes overlapped and the sound grew a little thicker. I realized my own voice had folded into that thickened sound somewhere along the way. Even I couldn't have said, by then, which part of it was mine.

*I liked not being able to tell. In Haven, everything had been distinguishable. Whose complaint it was, whose share it was, whose turn it was. Here, not even a single sound could be sorted out as belonging to anyone in particular.*

Noa asked me.

"…What are you two looking for?"

I thought for a moment before answering.

"…Father. And… I'm looking alongside my brother, for whatever answer he's looking for."

Noa nodded.

"…That's enough," she said. "You don't have to find everything. It's enough to have someone looking with you."

"…What are you looking for, ma'am?" I asked back.

Noa looked at the fire for a moment.

"…I'm not looking for anything anymore," she said. "I just stay beside these children. That's all I do now."

*Hearing that answer, I felt strangely at ease. Not everyone had to be looking for something. Just staying beside someone could be enough, all on its own.*

As she said it, Noa quietly ran a hand along the child's back. Watching that gesture, I felt that staying beside someone was nothing at all like doing nothing. To stay beside someone, you had to keep reaching out your hand. You had to keep looking at them. That might, in its own way, take more strength than searching for anything ever could.

---

That night, for the first time, my brother showed the paper stamped COMPLETE to someone else. To Noa.

Noa looked at the name on it for a long time. She traced it with a fingertip and read it quietly, without a sound — just the shape of the words on her lips.

I felt that silence stretch on and on. Only the crackle of embers and, far off, the sound of children running filled it. I started counting how many times Noa reread that name, and then I gave up. It seemed more important to watch than to count.

"…I don't know this person," she said. "But I know this stamp. Stories about people stamped like this go around the Wilderness too. No one knows where the completed go. But everyone tries hard to remember the names."

"…Why?" my brother asked.

"…Because that's the only resistance we have," Noa said.

My brother folded the paper again and tucked it back into his shirt. His hands were more careful than usual, doing it.

The fire was dying down, and one by one, people drifted off toward the tents. Noa gathered an armful of dry grass and laid it over the flames. The fire flared up briefly, then settled low again.

"…Who watches this fire?" I asked.

"…We take turns," Noa said. "If it goes out overnight, it's hard to light again in the morning."

*That answer stayed with me strangely long. These people had a reason for someone to stay awake every single night. Haven had never had a single reason like that.*

"…Is there anyone else out here who has a paper like this?" my brother asked.

"…I've seen a few," Noa said. "None of them talk about it much, but none of them throw the paper away either. What it means might be a little different for each of them, but just not throwing it away — that seems to say something on its own."

"…Where are they now?" my brother asked. There was something urgent in his voice.

"…Scattered," Noa said. "Here and there, all across this Wilderness. Even I don't know all of them. But if you ever cross paths, I think you'll know each other. Just from carrying that paper."

My brother nodded at that. I could feel him accepting, in that moment, that this was no longer a journey only the two of us were on.

*Hearing that, I truly felt for the first time that we weren't alone. From Brick Hollow all the way to here, we had always believed it was only ever the two of us. But somewhere out here in the Wilderness, there were more people carrying the same kind of paper we were.*

My brother's hand stayed a long while at his chest, over the pocket where the paper lay. I could tell he felt some new weight settling inside it. That paper wasn't his alone anymore. Somewhere out in this Wilderness, other people were carrying that same weight.

"…I'd like to meet them," my brother said.

"…You will, someday," Noa said. "This Wilderness looks wide, but people walking the same direction always end up meeting."

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. That day I learned for the first time that the thing my brother had started alone was already something other people, somewhere out here in the Wilderness, were doing too.*
