# Chapter 21. In Search of the Recorder

> _Previously_ — Speon gave us a brief glimpse into why he keeps this well every day, and into his own past: "I didn't go looking. I ran. Now I'm learning how to wait." When he told us he had seen the man at the flowerbed in more than one place, I began, faintly, to feel it was no coincidence.

Morning fog lay low over the well. Inside it I could see Speon already up, pacing beside the water. It didn't look as though he'd slept at all.

The fog rose only to waist height. Speon's upper body seemed to float above it. I watched him a long while. He looked like someone walking on water, and at the same time like someone half sunk in it.

Every blade of grass held a bead of moisture. I swept my palm across it once. My hand was wet in an instant. It struck me as strange that water which existed nowhere else in the Wilderness gathered on this hill every single morning, on its own.

*I think I understood, a little, why this hill was different. Wherever water comes up from somewhere deep, water gathers above it too. I couldn't tell whether that was because of the well, or because of Speon.*

"…Go find someone," Speon said the next morning. "Her name is Mena. She's a Recorder."

My brother's hands stopped packing.

"…What does she record?" he asked.

"…The names of people who've been completed," Speon said. "At every well scattered across this world, she gathers up as many names as she can, from whoever's come out of them. I send her word myself, sometimes."

*Hearing that, I understood for the first time how many more people like us there must be, scattered across this vast Wilderness. Speon wasn't the only one.*

"…Is Mena a Well-Keeper too?" I asked.

"…No," Speon said. "Mena doesn't keep a well. She keeps names instead. Everyone keeps something different."

Listening to him, I thought there must be far more people in this world than we knew — people waiting for someone, keeping something, in ways we hadn't yet imagined.

"…How do you keep a name?" I asked. "A name isn't a thing you can hold."

Speon smiled a little at that.

"…I thought the same, at first," he said. "But a name really does disappear, if no one calls it anymore. A thing stays where it is whether anyone's looking at it or not. A name doesn't. So someone has to go on writing it down, and go on calling it."

"…If you write it down, does it stop disappearing?" I asked.

"…It slows down how fast it disappears," Speon said. "That's the whole of what any of us can do."

*I remembered those words for a long time afterward. Not stopping it. Only slowing it. I understood then that everything these people did was of that kind. Not a work of winning, but of holding the line without giving ground.*

"…Who is that record for?" my brother asked.

"…I didn't know myself, at first," Speon said. "But I know now. It's for the day someone who lost their name wants to hear it again. Someone has to be holding that name, when that day comes."

*Hearing that, I thought of the paper with the COMPLETE stamp that my brother carried. He had already been doing that same work, alone. Mena was someone doing that same work on a far larger scale, for far longer.*

At those words my brother reached up and pressed his hand briefly against the paper inside his coat, checking it was there. I couldn't count how many times I'd seen him do that by now. It had become as much a part of him as any of his own movements.

"…I guess I'm supposed to be doing something like this too," my brother murmured. It sounded less like something said to Speon than something said to himself.

"…You already are," Speon said. "That one piece of paper is where it starts."

Listening to that exchange, I felt my brother becoming, little by little, a different person. When we'd left Brick Hollow, he had been someone trying to leave behind evidence. Now he was becoming someone trying to keep something safe. I understood that the difference looked small, but was actually enormous.

Evidence was something meant to be held up in front of someone. Keeping was something you went on doing whether anyone saw it or not. My brother didn't seem to have noticed that about himself yet. I didn't bother telling him. Some changes, I think, are better noticed by the person going through them, in their own time.

---

"…Why does Mena do this?" I asked.

"…By her own account, she used to be someone who kept records, once before," Speon said. "I've heard she recorded one great story somewhere. I don't know exactly what it was myself."

*Something in that pulled at me strangely. I was recording this story too. I didn't know it then — that someday I'd sit and talk with Mena about exactly that.*

Up until then I hadn't even known what I myself was doing. Every night I only went back over, in my head, what I'd seen that day. Noa's face. The pebble Rachel had given me. The soil on the flowerbed man's hands. I did it because it felt like something I couldn't let myself forget. I didn't know that was a record.

From that moment I found myself curious about this person, Mena. I tried to imagine her face, her voice. In my imagination she always sat surrounded by stacks of paper.

"…A great story — what kind?" I asked.

"…I don't know exactly, either," Speon said. "But every time Mena talks about it, something changes in her eyes. Like something she's kept a very long time, and very carefully."

I held on to that. Somehow it felt like I would hear that story myself, someday.

"…Where are the people from that story now?" I asked.

Speon didn't answer right away. The fog had thinned a little by then.

"…Scattered," he said. "Each to wherever they needed to be."

"…Do they meet again?" I asked.

"…Sometimes," Speon said. "Very rarely."

His voice dropped just slightly as he said it. I could hear a longing inside that lowered voice. But it wasn't a sad longing. It sounded more like the longing of someone who knew something for certain.

"…Has Mena been here a long time too?" I asked.

"…Longer than me, I'd guess," Speon said. "She was already doing this work when I first came here. She's never once stopped, from then until now."

"…That's amazing," my brother said.

"…It's not amazing. It's just going on," Speon said. "The two look alike, but they're different."

"…What's the difference?" my brother asked.

"…Amazing is only amazing if people are watching," Speon said. "Going on is going on whether anyone's watching or not. Mena has been writing down names for more than twenty years, where no one was watching at all."

---

Before we left, Speon packed us dried bread and a water skin.

"…Where Mena lives is two days from here," Speon said. "There are marks along the way. Small notches cut into the stones. I don't know who cut them. But follow those notches and you won't lose the road."

He pressed the water skin into my hand and tied the strap tight himself. That touch felt familiar to me. It was close to the way Father used to pack our things.

The bread was more than we could eat. I understood it was the amount Speon had decided to give us by choosing to go a few days hungry himself.

"…This is too much," I said.

"…I'm here," Speon said. "There's water here. With water, a few days is nothing."

I knew that wasn't quite true, and I took it anyway. I'd already learned from Noa that refusing something can be the ruder choice.

Speon looked last at our shoes. The soles had worn thin.

"…Don't walk at night," he said. "You won't see the marks, and you'll lose the road. And if a quiet light passes overhead, that day, just lie down and don't move."

My brother's face went tight at that.

"…Does it come even this far?" he asked.

"…Not here," Speon said. "But it passes over the road you're taking."

"…Aren't you coming?" I asked.

"…I have to stay here," Speon said. He looked back at the well. "I never know who might come out."

*Hearing that answer, I understood that Speon might never be able to leave this spot for the rest of his life. I couldn't decide, then, whether that was a sad thing or a beautiful one.*

I looked at the well one last time. The water was still clear. The bottom still couldn't be seen. I found it hard to leave that spot, thinking that someday Father might rise up out of that water.

"…Elpis," my brother called. "We have to go."

I ran my hand once more along the stone wall before I finally started walking.

My brother turned back for one more look at the well.

"…If our father ever comes out here," he said, "could you tell him we came looking for him?"

Speon gripped his shoulder briefly at that.

"…I will," he said. "I promise."

---

As we left, Speon said one last thing.

"…Tell Mena Speon's still waiting. She'll understand what that means."

"…What does it mean?" my brother asked.

Speon smiled a little.

"…That's something only Mena and I know," Speon said. "You'll have words like that too, someday. Words you never have to explain to anyone."

We walked a few steps and looked back. Speon was still standing there, watching us. I held that image in my eyes. A single figure standing on a hill, beside a well. I already knew, in that moment, that the shape of him wouldn't leave me for a long time.

When I looked back again, much later, he was still there. We must have looked much smaller by then, but he didn't wave. He just stood. I understood that was a farewell that lasted longer than any wave could.

*I learned, later, that Speon always stood like that until whoever was leaving had disappeared completely down the far side of the hill. He didn't only wait for the ones who came. He saw off, to the very end, the ones who went.*

---

The first notch was on a rock not far from the well. My brother found it first and traced it with his fingertip.

"…Here," he said. "Look."

It might have been a small arrow, or it might have been nothing but a scratch. I stared at the shape a long while and still wasn't sure. But my brother had already turned his body toward it.

"…How do you know?" I asked.

"…I don't," he said. "But this direction feels right."

We walked the whole day, finding those notches one by one. On rocks, sometimes at the base of a dead tree, the marks appeared at steady intervals. Proof that someone, a very long time ago, had traveled this road again and again, faithfully.

Once, we went almost two hours without finding a single mark. My brother stopped and started retracing our steps.

"…I think we missed one," he said.

"…Should we turn back?" I asked.

"…No," he said. "Let's wait here. Once the sun sinks a little, the shadows will make it easier to see."

We sat with our backs against a rock and waited for the sun to sink. Strangely, that waiting felt peaceful to me. In Haven, we would have filed a request the moment we lost the road. And a road would have appeared right away. But it wouldn't have been the road we'd found for ourselves.

Sure enough, once the sun sank a little, a thin shadow fell across the side of the rock, and the notch showed itself. We both saw it at the same instant.

"…There," I said.

My brother laughed. It was the first time I'd seen him laugh in days.

*Looking at those notches, I felt a strange comfort. Someone, before us, had found their way through this same hopeless Wilderness. We weren't entirely alone.*

That night we slept huddled together beneath a large rock. We didn't light a fire. Speon's words wouldn't leave my mind.

Once it went dark, nothing was visible at all. That was the first time I'd ever known a night so black I couldn't tell whether my eyes were open or shut.

"…Brother, are you awake?" I asked.

"…Yeah."

"…I'm scared."

"…Me too," he said.

I liked that answer. Back in Brick Hollow, my brother had never once said he was scared. Not because he wasn't, I think, but because he thought he wasn't allowed to say it to me.

"…It helps, a little, saying it out loud," I said.

"…Yeah," he said. "Strange, isn't it."

*That night I learned, for the first time, that fear can shrink without ever being solved. Just from someone else knowing it's there with you.*

The sky was full of stars again that night. No quiet light passed over.

As we walked, my brother kept scanning for those notches, as if he couldn't afford to miss one. That carefulness in him felt unfamiliar to me. Back in Brick Hollow he had always been someone who walked looking only straight ahead. Now he walked with his eyes going back and forth between the ground, the rocks, the roots of trees. I thought that was a new way of walking he'd learned in this world.

By the afternoon of the second day, the notches had begun coming closer together. The shorter the gaps grew, the more I understood we were nearing something.

"…Someone must have come this way often, from here on," my brother said.

"…Or maybe," I said, "from here on, they thought it couldn't be missed."

My brother nodded at that.

As the sun went down, I traced one of the notches with my fingertip. It was cut deep. It didn't look like something carved in a hurry — it looked worked over, again and again, over time. I wondered whose hand had made it.

It hadn't been cut all at once. It had been added to, a little at a time, on every pass. I couldn't count how many times that person had traveled this road. But I could tell one thing for certain — that every single time, they'd been thinking of whoever would come after them.

*I thought then, for the first time, about how lonely a thing it is to leave a mark for someone else. The one who leaves the mark almost never meets the one who follows it. And still, they leave it.*

*I set those words down as the last line of this chapter. That morning we left the well behind and started following those notches cut into stone after stone. I didn't know yet that they were the footprints of someone very old, marked all across this world, long before us.*
