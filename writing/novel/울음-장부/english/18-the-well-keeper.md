# Chapter 18. The Well-Keeper

> _Previously_ — On the fourth day of searching for the well, with their water and bread gone, the brother and sister began walking toward the hill on the horizon. For the first time, they felt the sense of going forward rather than running away.

What stood on the hill really was a well.

A small well ringed by a low stone wall. Someone sat beside it. A young man. He looked a little older than us.

The last few steps up the hill were the hardest. There was no strength left at all in my legs, and still, from the moment the well came into view, I couldn't stop walking. My brother was the same. We climbed the last stretch of that hill almost at a run.

Low grass grew here and there around it. A clearly greener shade than anywhere else. Just that color of grass alone told me this place was different from everywhere around it.

A small insect lifted off from among the grass and settled again. Even that small movement caught my eye. Along the whole four days of dry road behind us, I'd barely seen so much as a single bug. This one hill alone seemed strangely full of life.

*I felt something strange then. The well stood alone in the middle of the Wilderness, in a place with nothing around it. And yet the stone wall alone was clean, as if someone tended it every single day.*

A few small wildflowers bloomed in cracks between the stones of the wall. I wondered whether someone had planted them, or whether they'd grown up on their own. Either way, the fact that something alive had taken root on this bare hilltop was, strangely, a comfort.

On legs that had walked four days, we arrived, more collapsing than sitting down before it. My brother looked like he had no strength left even to speak. I could feel the man's eyes reading our condition in a single glance.

He rose to his feet at once and came toward us. His steps were quick. But even in that quickness I could feel something careful in him — like someone worried that if he came too fast, we might vanish like a mirage.

"…Sit," he said first. "Drink water before you talk."

His voice was low and calm. There was no hurry in it at all. I found that calm strangely foreign. Everyone we'd met since coming out into the Wilderness had carried some measure of urgency. This man alone did not.

Beside where he'd been sitting, a small heap of stones was stacked with care. I couldn't guess what it was for. But I could tell those stones hadn't been set down carelessly. They'd been stacked in order of size, very deliberately.

Beside the stone heap, a few worn wooden bowls sat overturned to dry. Not one bowl — several. I understood they'd been set out for more than one person. Bowls turned over and set to dry, every day, for guests who never came.

One bowl was noticeably smaller than the rest. I knew at a glance it was made for a child. I didn't know who it was for. But that one small bowl alone seemed to say more than the whole careful order of the hilltop around it.

---

The man looked us over first. He wasn't surprised.

"…It's been a while since anyone came by," he said. "Here for water?"

"…Yes," my brother said. His throat was parched.

The man stood and looked us over once more. Worn clothes, cracked lips, feet caked in dust. It wasn't a judging look. Just a careful one.

"…You've come a long way," he said. "Which way did you come from?"

"…From a tent camp," my brother said. "The one where a woman named Noa lives."

Something flickered faintly in the man's eyes at that name.

"…You know Noa?" my brother asked.

"…I've heard word of her," the man said. "Never met her myself. But I've heard she's a good person."

"…We walked four days," I said.

"…Four days." The man repeated it back, something turning over behind his eyes. "Walking this direction four days means you had a reason for it."

The man lowered a bucket and drew up water for us. It was clear. Not muddy.

"…Why is this one so clear?" I asked.

"…This well is deep," the man said. "Water that comes from deep down is always clear."

*Drinking that water, I thought his words weren't only about the water. I didn't know exactly why I felt that.*

The bucket knocked against the wall of the well as it rose. A hollow, resonant sound, twice over. I felt that sound had been keeping this place company for a very long time. The way he wound the rope back up looked practiced — the motion of something repeated more times than could be counted, plain in the shape of his fingers.

The water was cool, and just faintly sweet. I had never drunk water like it. Not the muddy water, and not Haven's flavorless water either. I looked back down into the bucket. It was too deep to see the bottom.

I felt the coolness spread through my whole body as it went down my throat. I closed my eyes and gave myself over entirely to that feeling. Four days of gathered thirst seemed to wash away all at once. I could feel warm blood returning even to my fingertips.

"…Drink slowly," the man said. "Drink too fast and you'll make yourself sick."

Only hearing that did I realize how frantically I'd been gulping it down. I forced myself to slow. My brother slowed to match me.

"…I've seen a lot of children drink like that," he said. "Everyone's the same, the moment they get here. They're afraid the water's going to run out."

"…Does it?" I asked.

"…It doesn't," he said. "This well has never once gone dry. But believing that takes a little time."

*I remembered those words a long while after. That believing something won't run dry takes time. I understood then that everything we'd learned in Haven had, in the end, been the opposite of that. In that city, everything was filled the instant you wanted it — but nothing could ever be believed in.*

"…How deep is it?" I asked.

"…I've never measured it all the way down myself," the man said. "But I know it's very deep."

I watched his hand lower the bucket back down into the well. The sound of the rope unspooling went on and on. Just from the length of that sound alone, I could guess how deep this well must be. By the time I heard the bucket touch water, I had almost started counting my own breaths.

My brother drank too, and seemed for the first time to breathe properly.

"…Thank you," he said. There was real feeling in his voice. It was the first time, outside that city, I'd heard him say that to anyone.

Speon looked briefly startled at that, as if he'd been handed something enormous.

"…It's just a bowl of water," he said. But his voice sounded genuinely glad.

"…It doesn't look like many people come here," I said.

"…Almost none," Speon said. "Once every few months, or rarer than that. So when someone comes, I'm glad. And a little afraid, too."

"…Why afraid?" I asked.

"…In case it's nothing again," Speon said quietly. In that, I could feel that he greeted every visitor already hoping for something.

"…What kind of news?" I asked.

Speon chose his words a moment, then shook his head.

"…I'll tell you that later," he said. "Right now I'm more curious how you two ended up here."

I didn't press him further. But in that brief hesitation, I could feel that some very old story was waiting inside him.

---

When my brother finished drinking, he asked carefully.

"…Is this… the well people say the completed go to?"

The man's expression changed at that. He looked startled.

"…Where did you hear that?" he asked.

"…From people in the Wilderness," my brother said. "And about the ones stamped COMPLETE, too."

"…Who told you this?" the man asked again. There was urgency in his voice now.

"…An old man at a tent camp," my brother said. "And someone named Noa, too. We don't know the details, but they said the story's been around a long time."

The man looked at the worn paper in my brother's hand. He looked at it for a long time.

"…Would you show me that paper?"

My brother handed it over. The man read the name, and ran his fingertip over the stamp.

*In that moment, I saw something in the man's face come apart. It was too complicated an expression to call sorrow alone.*

I haven't forgotten that expression. Relief and dread crossed it at the very same instant. It was the face of someone who had waited a very long time for something to finally arrive, and at the same moment had hoped it never would.

His fingertip paused for a moment over the stamp. I saw it tremble slightly. It was the hand of someone touching, again, a very old wound.

"…This name — I don't know this person," he said quietly. "But this stamp… I know this stamp. I know it very well."

"…How do you know it?" my brother asked.

Instead of answering, he looked off into the distance. He said nothing for a long time.

I could see, in that silence, the faintest tremor in his eyes. The eyes of someone holding words back. I understood then that this man, too, was carrying a great deal that he had not yet found a way to say.

While the silence held, I looked around the well. There were small marks scored into one side of the stone wall — nicks someone had cut in, over and over, for a long time. Numbers, or a tally of some kind. I couldn't tell which.

"…What's that?" I asked, pointing at the wall.

The man seemed to come back to himself, and looked over.

"…Days," he said. "I was counting how many I'd spent here. I started counting, and then I stopped. There got to be too many."

I tried, for a moment, to count those marks with my fingertip, and gave up. There truly were too many to count. That number frightened me. Those marks were telling me that the length of time this man had spent here was an entirely different kind of time than the four days we had walked to reach him.

*In that moment I thought of Haven's COMPLETE stamp. Time that ended with a single stamp, and time that could only pass by being scored, one mark at a time, into stone. I think I already knew, even then, which of the two was the truer kind of time.*

Some of the marks were cut deep; some were barely there at all. I thought that difference in depth must have left behind, exactly, whatever mood each of those days had carried. What had happened on the days cut deep? I couldn't bring myself to ask.

---

"…My name is Speon," the man finally said. "People say I'm the one who keeps this well. Though to be exact… it isn't keeping. It's waiting."

"…Speon," I said, saying the name back. An unfamiliar name. But it settled onto my tongue strangely easily.

"…That's right," he said, smiling a little. "Just call me that. It's been a while since anyone's said my name here."

"…No one says it?" I asked.

"…There has to be someone to say it," Speon said. A trace of loneliness crept into his voice. "Even the ones who come by, once every few months, often don't ask my name at all. Everyone just drinks the water and hurries off."

*Hearing that hurt something in me. I tried to imagine how long Speon must have gone here, beside this well, with no one to call him by name at all.*

As he said it, he glanced back toward the well. It looked like an old habit. I noticed that even in the middle of talking, he kept checking on it out of the corner of his eye, as if something might rise up out of it at any moment.

"…Waiting for what?" I asked.

The wind swept across the well's edge. His clothes, caught on the rim of the stone, stirred faintly. In that brief silence, I felt that it had been a very long time since anyone had asked Speon that question.

Speon looked at us for a long time. His face was that of someone making up his mind about something.

"…To answer that question, it'll be a long story," Speon said. "Rest here tonight, and tomorrow I'll tell you everything I know. It's been… a very long time since anyone came here carrying that question."

Speon stood and walked a slow circle around the well's edge. It was the walk of someone confirming something. I guessed it was a round he made every night, something like a watch. No one had ordered it of him — it looked more like a rule he'd set for himself.

"…Do you go around like this every night?" my brother asked.

"…Yes," Speon said. "It's a habit. I can't sleep until I've made sure nothing's wrong."

"…And tonight?" I asked.

Speon stopped at that question and looked at us for a moment.

"…Tonight, something happened," he said. "You two came."

Speon came back and sat down beside us again. Then, after a moment, he got up once more and brought back two blankets. Worn, but clean.

"…Bear with it if it's uncomfortable," Speon said. "There's no guest room out here."

"…This is more than enough," my brother said. "Better than Haven's beds."

He set a small stone down beside our spot. I almost asked what it was for, then decided not to. Probably something to keep insects away overnight. That small, ordinary care lodged itself in me strangely.

Speon smiled a little at that. It was the first smile I'd seen from him.

"…So you're children who know Haven," he said. "That'll make this easier to explain."

The blankets smelled of sunlight — the smell of something dried a long time in the open air. Underneath that smell was a faint trace of earth. Breathing it in, I thought about how many times Speon must have hung these blankets out and taken them back in, for guests who never came.

A night wind blew across the well and lifted the edge of the blanket faintly. I didn't try to escape the chill of it. That coolness, arriving after the day's heat, felt like the last word of goodnight to the day.

Lying under that blanket, I still couldn't fall asleep for a long while. From the well's direction I thought I heard the faint sound of water moving, low. Maybe it was only my imagination. But listening to it, I thought this well seemed almost as if it were alive, breathing on its own.

Beside me, my brother's breathing settled into an even rhythm. I could tell he was sleeping properly for the first time in days. A little way off, Speon was still sitting up. Not lying down — facing the well. I watched his back for a while before I finally fell asleep.

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. That night, for the first time, I felt that we were drawing close to a real answer. But strangely, that feeling was closer to fear than to joy.*
