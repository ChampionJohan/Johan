# Chapter 16. Talk of a Well

> _Previously_ — A searching light sent by Haven passed over the tent camp. Noa said that if it catches you, you're taken back to Haven, and "after that, no one has ever heard about that person again." It became clear that the Wilderness's way of sharing danger was fundamentally different from Haven's way.

The next day, the oldest man in the tent camp called us over.

I never learned his name. Everyone just called him "the old man." He said his eyes could hardly see anymore.

The morning sun still hung low. Smoke was rising here and there among the tents. Each person was making breakfast in their own corner, in their own way. Watching that, I felt that these people each had their own rhythm. Haven had no such thing as rhythm. Everyone there was comfortable in exactly the same way, at exactly the same time.

The old man's spot was the innermost part of the camp, where the sun fell best. I noticed that others stepped a little wide of it as they passed. I couldn't quite tell whether it was respect or caution.

"…I hear you're curious about people who've been completed," the old man said.

"…Yes," my brother said. "Do you know something?"

*I held my breath at that. Since leaving that city, for the first time, someone was saying they knew something.*

I saw my brother's face go slightly taut — expectation and wariness rising in it at once. I remembered that he'd already worn that same face several times before, and every time it had come to nothing. Still, he put the same face on again, every single time.

The old man planted his cane and lowered himself slowly to sit before us. Moving looked hard for him, and still he'd made a point of getting up to call us over.

"…Sit," the old man said. "This isn't a story for standing up."

Noa spoke quietly beside him.

"…Not everything the old man says is true. But this particular story — I've heard it from other people's mouths, more than once, since I came here."

The old man cleared his throat at that.

"…It's true I'm an old man who talks nonsense," he said. "But this isn't nonsense. A man who's lived this long knows the difference."

The old man drew a small circle in the dirt with the tip of his cane. Even with his eyes gone dim, his hand was steady.

"…It wasn't until my eyes went dark that stories like this one started coming through clearly," the old man said. "When I was young, I only ever believed what my eyes could see."

*Listening, I looked at his hands. The knuckles were thick and weathered the color of earth. The circle they'd drawn wasn't crooked at all. I understood then, for the first time, that there were things a hand remembered that eyes did not.*

The old man paused to catch his breath. Noa handed him a swallow of water. He drank it and only then opened his mouth again.

"…When I was young, I lived somewhere like Haven," the old man said. "The name was different, but it did the same thing. Whatever you filed, it disappeared. But what disappeared turned out not to be truly gone."

"…So is that why you left?" my brother asked.

"…I was thrown out," the old man said with a short laugh. "At the time I thought it was a punishment. Now, thinking back, I wonder if it wasn't."

---

"…I don't know exactly," the old man said. "But there's a story that's gone around for a long time. That the completed ones go to a well."

"…A well?" I asked.

"…I thought it was strange too," the old man said. "Why would a completed person go to a well? But this story has been around a very long time. I heard it too, back when I was your age."

"…Which well?" my brother asked.

The old man shook his head.

"…I don't know that much. I don't even know how many wells there are out here. But people call one of them just 'the well.'"

*Hearing that, I thought, strangely, of the man at the flowerbed. He had said it was "the thing that grows where no one planted it." I didn't know then why that saying overlapped with this talk of a well.*

My brother asked carefully.

"…Do you really believe that well exists?"

The old man smiled a little.

"…It's less that I believe it," he said, "and more that I've never found a reason not to. That's the more accurate way to put it."

"…Is the well far from here?" my brother asked.

"…I've never walked there myself, so I don't know," the old man said. "But I've heard you have to go a long way toward the sunrise, along a road few people ever travel."

"…Would it be dangerous?"

"…If there's a road in this Wilderness with no danger on it, be sure to tell me too," the old man said. It was the first time he'd smiled.

I found myself strangely at ease, watching that smile. This was the first person I'd met who could speak the word "dangerous" this lightly and still mean it, all at once. In Haven, the word danger had already been a problem waiting to be resolved. Out here, it was simply a condition of living.

Noa put in beside him.

"…I've been as far as the start of that road myself," she said. "There's not much water out that way. If you're going, you'll want to carry as much as you can."

"…How long would it take?" my brother asked.

"…Four days? Five?" Noa tilted her head. "I don't know exactly. I haven't been all the way there myself — I only heard it from someone who had."

*Listening to that, I felt that the whole of the Wilderness was strung together this way. Someone who had seen a thing, and someone who had heard it from that person, and someone else who had heard it from them in turn. In Haven, every piece of information had come straight from the Ledger. Out here, it traveled mouth to mouth, wearing a little thinner each time it passed. I found that wearing-thin strangely more trustworthy.*

---

"…There's also talk of someone who keeps that well," the old man added. "They call him the Well-Keeper, I hear. But I've never met him myself. And I've never met anyone who has, either."

"…So isn't it just an old story, then?" my brother asked. There was doubt in his voice.

"…It might be just an old story," the old man said. "But here's what I think. It's a rare thing for the exact same story to travel this long, out of this many mouths, with nothing behind it at all."

*Hearing that, I looked at my brother. There was a familiar expression on his face. The same face he'd had the day he first discovered the Completion House in Haven.*

The old man planted his cane and drew himself up a little. It was the gesture of someone bringing a story to its end.

"…That's as much as I can tell you," the old man said. "The rest, you'll have to go and see for yourselves. Believe an old man's word alone, and you won't get anywhere."

"…Thank you," my brother said.

"…Save the thanks for later," the old man said. "For when you've actually found something."

---

That night, my brother said to me.

"…I want to go find that well."

"…Off to do something dangerous again?" I asked. But this time I wasn't angry.

"…This time I meant, let's go together," he said.

I laughed at that. It had been a long time since I'd laughed.

"…All right," I said. "Let's go together."

The fire was dying down. I watched the last embers and thought back over the days we'd spent at this tent camp. These people had made room for us without asking for anything in return. I understood now that this had never been an ordinary thing.

"…Let's say a proper goodbye to Noa before we go," I said.

"…Of course," my brother said. "If it weren't for these people, there's no telling what would have happened to us by now."

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. The next morning, we said goodbye to Noa and the children and set out to find that well. I didn't know then that on the day we learned who this Well-Keeper was, the whole road we had walked from Brick Hollow until now would look different to us all over again.*
