# Chapter 22. The House of Records

> _Previously_ — Speon introduced the brother and sister to Mena, the Recorder. It came out that gathering the names of the completed was the same work my brother had been doing alone. They set out on a two-day walk, following the nicks carved into the stones.

On the evening of the second day, we arrived in front of a low earthen house.

Two days of keeping my eyes fixed to the ground, so as not to lose a single nick, had left my neck stiff. Only when the earthen house came into view did I finally lift my head. The sky was already stained red.

It looked more like a storehouse than a home. There was a small sign by the door. **THE HOUSE OF RECORDS.**

*Seeing that sign, I felt strangely at ease. There were places in this world that erased names, and places that wrote them down and kept them. Just knowing both kinds of place existed gave me a little comfort.*

A few small flowerpots stood in a row beside the door. It felt strange that anyone would be growing something out here, in this parched Wilderness. I guessed those pots were something someone watered every single day.

The whole way there, my brother barely spoke. I knew what he was afraid of. No answer at all, or an answer that turned out worse — he kept weighing which of the two would be better.

*I could read that weighing plainly on his face. I said nothing at the time. I knew no words of mine could stand in for that scale.*

I walked ahead in his place, looking around, so as not to disturb his thinking even that much. A little dust stirred in the wind. Smelling it, I realized that even I had begun to feel afraid of what might lie past this door.

---

When we knocked, a woman came out. She looked much older than us. There was ink on her fingers, as if she had been writing something without stopping.

Her eyes were sharp. But sharp didn't mean cold. I felt, instead, that these were eyes trained for a very long time to see something precisely.

"…Who are you?" the woman asked.

"…Speon sent us," my brother said. "Are you Mena?"

Something shifted slightly in the woman's face at that name.

"…I am. What did Speon say?"

"…That he's still waiting," I said.

Mena closed her eyes for a moment at that. She said nothing. But I could feel something very old in that silence.

"…Is he well?" Mena asked. Her voice shook a little.

"…Yes," my brother said. "He keeps that well every day."

Mena smiled a little at that. Something like tears mixed into her smile.

"…That's just like him," Mena said.

As she said it, Mena opened the door a little wider. I could tell, from that gesture, that she had already decided to let us in. A single name, Speon's, had been enough to open that door.

"…Come in," Mena said. "This isn't a conversation for standing outside."

Mena walked ahead of us and lit a lamp. The light fell on her ink-stained fingers. I could tell the stains on those fingers hadn't come from a day or two of writing.

---

Mena let us inside. The whole place was paper. There were shelves along every wall, and every shelf was packed tight with paper covered in names.

The room had its own particular smell — old paper and ink, and faintly, candle wax. Breathing it in, I tried to guess how long this room must have been filling up like this. I couldn't quite manage it.

Some of the shelves reached all the way to the ceiling. I wondered how old the papers up there must be. I wondered whether Mena's own hand could even reach that high, until I noticed an old ladder standing in the corner and had my answer.

"…Are all these the names of completed people?" I asked.

"…Only the ones I've managed to gather," Mena said. "I haven't gathered them all. This world is too vast."

I ran my eyes over the shelves, one by one. The handwriting was different from paper to paper. I understood that these weren't all copied out by Mena's own hand, but were, instead, the traces of word carried to her by many different people and set down just as it came. Some of the paper was old and faded; some looked as if the ink hadn't even dried yet.

Looking around at the papers, my brother asked carefully.

"…Could you look for our father's name too?"

Mena looked at him for a long time.

"…Tell me his name," she said. "I'll look."

*My heart was pounding so hard in that instant it hurt. Listening to my brother say Father's name, I could feel that even after all this time, the name still sat exactly right in his voice.*

I repeated the name silently to myself as well. I didn't say it aloud. But just doing that much brought a heat to my eyes. I realized, in that moment, how many times I must have said that name over to myself, just to keep from forgetting it.

Mena wrote the name down on paper. I saw her fingertips trembling slightly.

"…Is it a common name?" Mena asked.

"…No," my brother said. "In Brick Hollow, our father was the only one with it."

"…Then it might be a little easier to find," Mena said.

Mena checked the paper once more and asked.

"…Any features that might help? A scar on his body, a particular way of walking?"

My brother thought a moment before answering.

"…One finger on his left hand is short," my brother said. "He was hurt carrying bricks."

Mena wrote that small detail down beside the name.

"…This kind of thing helps," Mena said. "People forget their names. Their bodies don't forget as easily."

As she spoke, Mena underlined that detail on the page once more, to mark it. Her hand moved with practice. I could tell from that gesture alone that she had done this — taking down small details like this — more times than could be counted.

"…Have you ever heard anything about someone with a short finger?" my brother asked carefully.

Mena shook her head.

"…Not yet," Mena said. "But I'll look now."

---

Mena moved back and forth between the shelves for a long time. The room filled with nothing but the sound of pages turning.

My brother stood in one spot the whole while, without moving at all. I sat beside him and held his hand. His hand was cold. I hoped my own hand might warm it, even a little.

Every time the candle flame flickered, the shadows of the papers on the shelves flickered with it against the wall. Watching those shadows, I thought that every single one of those papers had been, for someone, the whole of who they were.

I didn't know how much time had passed. I couldn't breathe right through any of it.

Mena finally stopped in front of one shelf. She turned back to face us. Her expression was complicated.

"…It's here," Mena said. "But it isn't the answer you're hoping for."

Mena carried the paper back toward us as if cradling it against her chest. Her steps had slowed from before. I could feel, in that slowness, that she was still choosing how to say what came next.

"…Stay here tonight," Mena said. "I'll tell you in the morning, once your minds are a little clearer."

"…We want to hear it now," my brother said. His voice had gone rough.

Mena looked at him for a long time.

"…All right," Mena said. "Then sit down."

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. In that instant I learned that Father really was alive, somewhere in this world. But at the same time, I had to endure the whole of that night before I would learn what she meant by "not the answer you're hoping for."*
