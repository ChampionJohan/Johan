# Chapter 22. The House of Records

> _Previously_ — Speon introduced the brother and sister to Mena, the Recorder. The work of gathering the names of completed people turned out to run in the very same direction as what my brother had been doing alone all along. We set out walking a two-day road, following the notches cut into every stone.

On the evening of the second day, we came to a low earthen house.

Two days of keeping my eyes fixed to the ground so I wouldn't lose the notches had left my neck stiff. I only lifted my head the moment that earthen house came into view. The sky had already gone red.

It looked less like a house than a storeroom. The walls were earth, and the roof was dried grass pressed flat and weighted down. There was only one window, and from it came a very faint light. It was the first light of another person's making that I had seen in two days.

There was a small sign by the door. **The House of Records.**

The letters had been carved into the wooden board with the point of a knife. The strokes ran deep. I touched the letters with my fingertips. The way they'd been cut looked like the notches on the stones.

*Seeing that sign, I felt oddly at ease. This world had places that erased names, and it had places that wrote them down and kept them. Just knowing that both kinds of place existed gave me, in that moment, a small measure of comfort.*

Beside the door stood a row of small flowerpots. It struck me as strange that anything was being grown at all, in this parched Wilderness. I guessed that someone gave those pots water, every day, without fail.

One of the pots had already dried up and died. I wondered why it hadn't been cleared away. Even that dead pot felt like some kind of record kept by this house. Here, it seemed, not even the dead were simply thrown out.

I could feel the heat the dried grass roof had held all day slowly leaking back out of it. Feeling that warmth cool against the back of my hand, I thought that this house, too, seemed to breathe out its day like something alive.

The whole way here, my brother had barely spoken. I knew what he was afraid of. No answer at all, or an answer that turned out worse — he kept weighing those two, back and forth, without settling on either.

*I could read that weighing plainly on his face. I said nothing, the whole way. I knew that no words of mine could stand in for that scale he was carrying.*

I walked a little ahead of him and looked around, so that at least I wouldn't disturb his thinking. Dust drifted a little on the wind. Breathing it in, I realized that I, too, had begun to be afraid of what might be waiting on the other side of that door.

My brother lifted his hand at the door, then lowered it. Then lifted it again.

"…Knock," I said.

"…I know," my brother said. And still he didn't knock.

That was the first time I truly understood him. He had come all this way to find an answer, and now that he was standing in front of the door, the answer itself had begun to frighten him. Every step from Brick Hollow to Haven, from Haven to the well, from the well to here — all of it had come to a stop in front of this one door.

"…Let's knock together," I said.

I laid my hand over the back of his. Together, we knocked.

---

A woman opened the door. She looked much older than us. There was ink on her fingers, as if she'd spent her life with something in her hand, writing.

Her eyes were sharp. But sharp didn't mean cold. I felt, instead, that those were eyes trained a very long time to look at something exactly as it was.

She didn't open the door wide. She opened it just enough to show her face, and looked past us first, into the dark behind our backs. She hadn't looked at us — she'd looked behind us. From that order alone, I understood that this woman, too, was used to being followed.

"…Who's there?" the woman asked.

"…Speon sent us," my brother said. "Are you Mena?"

Something shifted, faintly, in the woman's face at that name.

"…That's me," she said. "What did Speon say?"

"…That he's still waiting," I said.

Mena heard that and closed her eyes for a moment. She said nothing. But I felt something very old, in that silence.

"…Is he all right?" Mena asked. Her voice trembled slightly.

"…Yes," my brother said. "He watches over that well every single day."

At that, Mena gave a small laugh. Something like tears was mixed into it.

"…That sounds like him," Mena said.

She opened the door a little wider as she said it. I felt, in that motion, that she had already decided to let us in. A single name — Speon's — had been enough to open that door.

"…Come in," Mena said. "This isn't a conversation for standing outside."

Mena led the way, lighting a lamp as she went. The light caught the ink stains on her fingers. I could tell that stain hadn't come from a day or two of work.

As she closed the door, Mena dropped the latch into place. Then she reached over and pulled the cloth across the window a little tighter.

"…Is it all right if the light shows?" I asked.

"…It's all right if it shows a little," Mena said. "But only a little is good. Too dark, and no one can find their way here at all. Too bright, and it draws the ones you'd rather it didn't."

*I remembered that for a long time afterward. Every person in this world who waited for someone seemed to live like that — never able to hide completely, and never able to be seen completely, either.*

---

Mena brought us inside. The inside was nothing but paper. Every wall had shelves, and every shelf was crowded with paper bearing names.

The room had a smell all its own. Old paper and ink, and beneath it a faint smell of melted candle wax. Breathing it in, I tried to guess how long it must have taken to fill this room like this. I couldn't begin to guess.

Some of the shelves reached all the way to the ceiling. I wondered how old the papers up there must be. I doubted Mena's hand alone could reach that high — until I saw the old ladder standing in the corner, and had my answer.

A small piece of wood hung from every shelf. Each one had letters carved into it too. I was about to ask what they marked when Mena spoke first, as if she'd already noticed the question in my face.

"…Those are well names," Mena said. "One shelf, one well."

I tried counting the shelves. There were more than twenty. I gave up partway through.

"…This many?" my brother asked.

"…This is only the wells I've had word from," Mena said. "I don't even know how many I haven't."

*Hearing that answer, my legs went weak. More than twenty shelves. And that wasn't even all of them. That was the first day I felt, in my body and not just my mind, how enormous this search really was.*

I walked between the shelves, running my eyes over the names carved into the little wooden markers. Most of them were names I didn't know. But strangely, that unfamiliarity felt heavy. I thought that behind every single name, there must have been some Brick Hollow of its own.

One shelf, alone, stood nearly empty. Only a few papers on it.

"…Why is that one empty?" I asked.

Mena looked over at it.

"…That's a well with no one keeping it," Mena said. "No word ever comes. Because no one's watching."

I looked at that empty shelf for a long time. Paper missing didn't mean no one had come out of that well. It meant no one had been there to see them.

"…Are all of these the names of completed people?" I asked.

"…Only the ones I've been able to gather," Mena said. "I haven't gotten them all. This world is too wide."

I ran my eyes down the shelves, one by one. The handwriting differed slightly from paper to paper. I understood that these weren't Mena's own hand copying names — they were the traces of word carried to her by many different people, and written down exactly as it came. Some papers were old and faded. Others looked as if the ink hadn't even dried yet.

"…Who brings you these names?" I asked.

"…Anyone passing through," Mena said. "Well-keepers, traders, people on the run. Whoever brings me a name, I write it down."

"…What if it's a lie?" my brother asked.

Mena glanced at him for a moment at that question. It wasn't an unkind look.

"…Then I write it down as a lie," Mena said. "The moment I start deciding what's true, people stop bringing me the names they're unsure of. And then the real ones disappear right along with them."

*I thought that one line explained this whole room. This room didn't hold only what was certain. It held the uncertain too, all of it, right alongside the rest. Haven's ledger kept only what was certain. That's why nothing at all was left, in that city.*

My brother looked over the papers and asked, carefully.

"…Could we look for our father's name too?"

Mena looked at him for a long moment.

"…Tell me the name," Mena said. "I'll look."

*My heart pounded so hard in that instant that it hurt. Listening to my brother say Father's name aloud, I felt that even after all this time, that name still sat perfectly formed in his voice.*

I said the name over too, silently, inside myself. I didn't say it aloud. But even that alone was enough to make my eyes burn. I realized, right then, just how many times I must have repeated that name to myself, just so I wouldn't forget it.

*I understood later that repeating a name like that isn't only a way of remembering the person. It's also a way of telling yourself, over and over, that they still exist.*

Mena wrote the name down on the paper. I saw that her fingertips were trembling, just slightly.

"…Is it a common name?" Mena asked.

"…No," my brother said. "In Brick Hollow, there was only one Father."

"…Then it might be a little easier to find," Mena said.

Mena checked the paper once more and asked.

"…Anything else — a mark on him somewhere, a way he walked?"

My brother thought for a moment before answering.

"…One finger on his left hand is shorter than the rest," he said. "He got hurt carrying bricks."

Mena wrote that down small, beside the name.

"…This kind of thing helps," Mena said. "People forget names before they forget bodies."

As she said it, Mena underlined that detail on the page once more, to mark it. Her fingers moved with familiar ease. I could tell, from that gesture alone, that she had taken down details like this more times than could be counted.

"…Have you heard, all this time, of anyone with a short finger like that?" my brother asked, carefully.

Mena shook her head.

"…Not yet," she said. "But I'll start looking, from now on."

"…Why do you do this?" my brother asked, suddenly.

Mena set down her pen.

"…A long time ago, I wrote down something very important," Mena said. "Back then I thought — what's the use of writing it down? What does writing change? But I learned, later. Only what gets written down stays."

"…What did you write?" I asked.

Mena smiled. She didn't answer.

"…That's not a story for today," Mena said. "Right now, your father's name comes first."

She said that and rose from her seat. I saw her back hesitate for just a moment. Setting her own story aside didn't seem to come easily to her, either. But she turned at once and walked toward the shelves.

---

Mena went back and forth among the shelves for a long time. Only the sound of turning paper filled the room.

My brother stood in one place the whole time, without moving. I sat beside him and took his hand. His hand was cold. I hoped mine might warm it, even a little.

Every time the candle flame flickered, the shadows of the papers on the shelves flickered with it against the wall. Watching those shadows, I thought that every single paper there must have once been someone's entire world.

Each time Mena turned a page, she touched the tip of her finger lightly to her tongue first. Then she ran her eyes down it, top to bottom, one sheet at a time. She skipped nothing. I found that thoroughness almost frightening. This woman was rereading hundreds of names, right now, for the sake of one person — our father.

Sometimes her hand paused. Every time it did, my own heart stopped along with it. But then Mena would turn the page again. Through however many times that pause and turn repeated, I hoped and then collapsed, over and over, that same number of times.

I don't know how much time passed. I couldn't breathe properly, the whole time it did.

*That night, I understood for the first time that waiting could hurt the body itself. My shoulders stiffened, my jaw ached, my fingertips went cold. I thought it was almost fortunate that there was nowhere to file a complaint about that pain. That pain was the only road there was to Father.*

At last Mena stopped in front of one shelf. She turned back to us. Her face was complicated.

"…It's here," Mena said. "But it isn't the answer you're hoping for."

Mena carried the paper back to us the way you'd hold something against your chest. Her steps were slower than before. I felt, in that slowness, that she was still choosing, even now, how to give us these words.

She didn't set the paper down on the table. She kept holding it. It seemed she thought that the moment she set it down, we would read it ourselves. I could only see the back of the paper. Something was written on that side too, but the ink showed through too faintly to read.

The lamp wick sputtered faintly as it burned. Other than that sound, there was nothing. In that stillness, I understood that Mena was trying, right now, to be gentle with us. She was someone who knew how to deliver bad news carefully.

"…Sleep here tonight," Mena said. "I'll tell you in the morning, once your minds are clearer."

"…We want to hear it now," my brother said. His voice had cracked.

Mena looked at him for a long time.

She turned the lamp wick up a little higher. The room brightened, just slightly. I hoped that one small bit of extra light might help us bear whatever we were about to hear.

"…All right," Mena said. "Then sit down."

*I set that line down as the last sentence of this chapter. In that moment, I learned that Father really was alive, somewhere in this world. And yet, until I learned what "not the answer you're hoping for" meant, I had to endure that entire night not knowing.*
