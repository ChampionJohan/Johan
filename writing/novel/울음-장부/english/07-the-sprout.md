# Chapter 7. The Sprout

> _Previously_ — Jeteo learned that people walk into the unmarked building on their own, and vanish stamped COMPLETE. The Administrator, for the first time, dodged a question with "that isn't mine to answer." My brother was warned not to go near that area again.

That morning, a tiny fleck of green showed where the seed had been.

I thought at first I was seeing it wrong. I knelt down and looked again. It was real. A sprout smaller than a fingernail.

*I had never, until then, seen anything in this city grow on its own. The roads, the houses, the food — all of it arrived already made. This sprout was different. No one had made it. It had simply grown.*

The soil was still damp. I pressed a finger into it, lightly. The sprout didn't waver. That meant its roots had already taken hold of the ground.

I found that astonishing. Something so small already knew how to hold on to something. I crouched beside it and watched, for a long while, how it swayed just a little with each breeze and came back to itself.

I sat there a long time. The man didn't come that day. Strangely, watching that sprout alone, I wasn't lonely at all.

A few other people passed by the flowerbed. None of them stopped walking. I found that strange. No one was surprised to see a shoot come up. As if, in this city, even surprise disappeared once it was filed.

The old woman was on the bench beside it that day too. I pointed at the sprout.

"…Look, it came up."

The old woman slowly turned her head. It took a long time. Her eyes settled on that small green.

"…My daughter liked things like this too," the old woman said. Her voice was a little different from before. Only a little, but I didn't miss it.

"…Did you remember her name?" I asked.

The old woman thought a long time.

"…No," the old woman said. "But I remember that she liked things like this."

I found, strangely, that those words sounded gentle to me. I sat a little closer beside her.

"…What color did she like?" I asked.

The old woman looked at the sprout again, narrowing her eyes.

"…I think it was green," the old woman said. "I'm not sure. But looking at this now, I think it might have been."

I nodded at that. It seemed all right that she wasn't sure.

*I understood something strange, then. A name wasn't erased once it was filed. What was erased was the heart that used to call it. But the place where that heart had been — it wasn't erased completely.*

The old woman kept looking at the sprout a long while after that. As I stood to go, it weighed on me to leave her there. But she gave a small wave of her hand, as if to say it was all right. That gesture looked, to me, like something sent to a daughter.

---

Back home, I told my brother about it. He was fidgeting with the paper and only half listening.

"…Brother, the sprout came up," I said. "It really grew."

"…Yeah," my brother said. He didn't meet my eyes.

"…I thought that was good news."

That was when my brother looked at me for the first time.

"…Elpis." My brother said. "You talk like the whole world's all right again just because one seed grew. But I just saw a place where people walk in on their own and disappear. How can those two things exist in the same world?"

*I couldn't answer that. My brother wasn't wrong. That was the first time it occurred to me that my kind of waiting might look, to my brother, like naivety.*

I looked a long time that night at the dirt still under my fingernails. My brother might be right. But I didn't want to wash it off.

That night, for the first time, my brother told me everything about the Completion House. How many shelves there were, how many layers of paper lined them, that not a sound came from inside. I couldn't bring myself, listening, to say any more about the sprout.

My brother couldn't keep his hands still the whole time he talked. He kept folding and unfolding the paper as he spoke. I realized, only then, that it was a habit he had when he was afraid.

"…Weren't you scared?" I asked.

"…I was," my brother said. "But there was something harder to bear than the fear."

"…What?"

"…That no one else was scared," my brother said. "The people walking past that building — they all just walked past. I was the only one afraid."

"…One person was smiling," my brother went on. "Right in front of that building's door. That was the strangest thing of all to me. I couldn't figure out why."

"…Maybe they smiled because they were scared?" I asked.

"…No." My brother shook his head. "It was a real smile. That was even scarier."

*Hearing that, I finally, truly understood how alone my brother had been. I hadn't been lonely beside my sprout. My brother had been alone beside the truth.*

---

"…I know there are scary things too," I said. "But just because something's scary doesn't mean nothing good exists."

"…That good thing came from this city too," my brother said. "Everything this city gives is a trap. The bread. The healing. All of it. That seed too — that man gave it to you."

"…He's not from this city," I said. "He told me so himself."

My brother paused a little at that.

"…What did he say?"

"…That he's in the place no one's planted. That he does this kind of work wherever he goes."

"…Where does a person like that even exist," my brother said. But there wasn't the same certainty in his voice as before.

"…I don't know either," I said. "But you know, brother — being strange doesn't mean being bad."

"…There were plenty of strange people in Brick Hollow too," my brother said. "Not many of them were good."

"…This isn't Brick Hollow," I said.

My brother didn't answer that. I knew it wasn't that he had no reply — it was that he needed time to think.

*In that moment I saw, just for an instant, something in my brother's face that wasn't doubt. Something like curiosity. It didn't last long.*

---

"…I still don't trust that man," my brother said, at last. "I don't trust anyone in this city. You be careful too."

"…Don't you trust anyone at all?" I asked.

My brother held the paper — the one stamped COMPLETE — in his hand.

"…I haven't found anything to trust yet," my brother said. "When I find it, I'll trust it. Not yet."

"…What do you think you'll have to find, to be able to trust?" I asked.

My brother thought a long while. He looked out the window, then turned his eyes back to me.

"…I don't know," my brother said. "But I think I'll know it, when I find it. Whatever it is."

I took my brother's hand. My hand came down over his, over the paper stamped COMPLETE.

"…I'm going to keep watering it," I said. "The sprout."

"…Okay," my brother said. This time he met my eyes. "You do that. I'll keep doing this."

We sat a while that night without speaking. Through the window, Haven's lights burned in their straight, even rows. I watched those lights and wondered whether, under every single one, there might be someone else like us.

*I set this down as the last line of this chapter. My brother wasn't someone who couldn't trust. He was someone who hadn't yet found what to trust. I understood, that day for the first time, how great that difference was. And that night too, we did not let go of each other's hands.*
