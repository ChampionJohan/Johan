# Chapter 7. The Sprout

> _Previously_ — At a building missing from every city map, Jeteo discovered that people walked in on their own and vanished, stamped COMPLETE. For the first time the Administrator avoided a question outright — "That isn't mine to answer" — and warned my brother never to go near that zone again.

That morning, a tiny fleck of green appeared where the seed had been.

At first I thought I was seeing it wrong. I knelt and looked again. It was real. A sprout smaller than a fingernail.

I held my breath and reached out, then stopped. It felt like it might vanish if I touched it. Instead I followed its shape with my eyes alone, piece by piece. It was a very pale green.

*Until that moment I had never once seen anything in this city grow on its own. The roads, the houses, the food — everything arrived already made. This sprout was different. No one had made it. It had simply grown.*

The soil was still damp. I pressed it lightly with a finger. The sprout didn't waver. That meant its roots had already taken hold of the dirt.

I breathed in the scent of that moisture in the soil. Nothing like the dry-dust smell of Brick Hollow. I wanted to remember that smell for a long time, so I put my nose close to it, again and again.

Even the sprout's color seemed to shift, little by little, as time passed. At first it had been an almost transparent light green; as the sun rose higher, that color deepened, just slightly. I begrudged even blinking, for fear of missing that change.

The morning air was cold. The light slanting across the flowerbed was still weak, so the sprout barely cast a shadow. I stayed there a long while, wondering when a shadow might finally appear.

Far off, I could hear the morning sounds of Haven. Even those sounds were less sounds than proof that no other sound existed. A door opening, footsteps — that was all. No one spoke.

I found that strange. Something this small already knew how to hold onto something. I crouched beside it and watched, each time the wind blew, how the sprout swayed slightly and then returned to its place.

I sat there a long while. The man didn't come that day. I was alone, watching the sprout, and yet strangely I wasn't lonely.

A few other people passed by the flowerbed. None of them stopped walking. I thought that was strange. Not one of them was startled to see a sprout had come up. As if, in this city, even being startled was something that got filed away and disappeared.

I felt the urge to grab a passerby and show them the sprout. I held back. Instead, I found myself wanting to keep it as something that belonged only to me. That made it feel more precious.

One person brushed right past the edge of the flowerbed — close enough that their sleeve touched a leaf. And still they didn't turn their head. I understood that their eyes were fixed only on the road ahead. Everyone's eyes were like that, in this city. Looking only at the path, never beside it.

*I only understood the reason for that much later. Looking beside the path meant something to file. Something to file meant a resolution would come, and once resolution came, that thing would vanish too. The people of this city had learned not to look at all, so as not to lose anything.*

The old woman was on her bench beside the flowerbed that day too. I pointed at the sprout and said,

"…Look, it came up."

The old woman turned her head slowly. It took a long time. Her eyes stopped on that small speck of green.

"…My daughter liked things like this," she said. Her voice was a little different from before. Only slightly, but I didn't miss that difference.

"…Did you remember her name?" I asked.

The old woman thought a long while.

"…No," she said. "But I remember that she liked it."

Somehow that struck me as a tender thing to say. I sat a little closer to her.

Just then, the old woman's hand moved, very slightly. One finger twitched in her lap. I didn't miss that small movement. It had been a hand that stayed perfectly still for so long.

"…What color did she like?" I asked.

The old woman looked at the sprout again, narrowing her eyes.

"…I think it was green," she said. "I'm not sure. But looking at this now, it seems that way."

I nodded at that. It seemed all right that she wasn't sure.

"…How old was your daughter?" I asked.

The old woman blinked at the question. For a long while.

"…I don't know," she said. "But she was small. Her hands were small."

I thought that answer was strangely exact. There was no number, and yet the size of her hand had remained.

"…Do you still remember that small hand?" I asked.

The old woman opened her hand a little. "…I think it was about this big," she said. Small enough to fit snugly inside her palm.

"…I know exactly what my brother's hand is like too," I said. "I could tell it with my eyes closed. There's a callus under his right thumb."

For the first time, the old woman gave the smallest smile at that. Too small to really be called a smile — but I decided to call it one anyway.

*I understood something strange, just then. A name wasn't erased when it was filed. It was the heart that used to call that name that got erased. But the place where that heart had lived — that wasn't erased completely.*

I decided to call that place the heart's flowerbed. Even with nothing planted in it, that place still remained. A place where, someday, something could grow again.

*For a long time after that, whenever I looked at a person, I found myself thinking first of that place. Whether, even if nothing seemed to be there now, the place itself still remained in them. I learned to recognize that, from that old woman.*

The old woman kept watching the sprout for a long while after that. I felt bad leaving her behind as I got up to go. But she gave a small wave of her hand, as if to say it was all right. That gesture looked, to me, like something sent toward her daughter.

I walked a few steps and turned to look back once more. The old woman was still there. But her posture was a little different than before. Her back was just slightly straighter.

---

I told my brother about it once we were home. He was fiddling with the paper, only half listening.

"…Brother, it sprouted," I said. "It really grew."

"…Good," he said. He didn't meet my eyes.

"…I think that's good news."

He looked at me then, for the first time.

"…Elpis," he said. "You're talking like the whole world's fine again just because one seed sprouted. I just watched people walk into a place and disappear, on their own two feet. How can those two things exist in the same world?"

*I had no answer to that. He wasn't wrong. For the first time, I understood that my waiting might look, to him, like naivety.*

I turned that over in my mind as I left the room. I wasn't angry. But some part of me ached. I named that ache, later, as hurt.

That night I looked a long time at the dirt still under my fingernails. Maybe my brother was right. And still, I didn't want to wash it off. As long as that dirt stayed on my hand, it felt like proof that the sprout had been real.

That night, for the first time, my brother told me everything about the Completion House — how many shelves there were, how many layers of paper lined them, how no sound came from inside at all. I couldn't bring up the sprout again while he spoke.

He couldn't keep his hands still the whole time he talked. He kept folding and unfolding the paper as he went on. I realized, only then, that this was his habit whenever he was afraid.

I watched his hands quietly. That rhythm of folding and unfolding was almost like a heartbeat. I waited for his hands to stop. They didn't stop until he'd finished speaking.

"…Weren't you scared?" I asked.

"…I was," he said. "But there was something harder to bear than fear."

"…What was it?"

"…That no one else was scared," he said. "Everyone walking past that building just kept walking. I was the only one who was afraid."

"…One person was smiling," he went on. "Right in front of the building's door. That's what struck me as strangest. I couldn't understand why he was smiling."

"…Wasn't it a scared kind of smile?" I asked.

"…No," he said, shaking his head. "It was a real smile. That made it worse."

"…What did the smile look like?" I asked.

"…Peaceful," he said. "That's what's so strange about it. I've never seen an expression like that. Not in Brick Hollow, not here."

"…What kind of person was he?" I asked.

"…An older man," my brother said. "His clothes were clean. He didn't look sick either. He just looked like… someone who was finished."

"…Finished with what?"

My brother had no answer to that for a long while.

"…Nothing left to ask," he finally said. "I think that's how a person disappears here. Not dragged away. Just running out of things to ask."

*Hearing that, I understood, for the first time, how lonely my brother must have been. I hadn't been lonely beside the seed. My brother had been alone beside the truth.*

I wanted to take his hand just then. But his hands were still busy folding the paper. Instead I quietly rested my head against his shoulder.

---

"…I know there are scary things too," I said. "But scary things existing doesn't mean nothing good exists at all."

"…Even the good thing came from this city," my brother said. "Everything this city gives is a trap. The bread, the healing, all of it. That seed too — that man gave it to you."

"…He's not from this city," I said. "He told me so himself."

My brother paused a little at that.

"…What did he say?"

"…That he's in the place no one's planted. That he does that kind of work wherever he goes."

"…Where does a person like that even exist?" my brother said. But there wasn't the same certainty in his voice as before.

"…I don't know either," I said. "But, Brother — being strange doesn't automatically make someone bad."

"…There were a lot of strange people in Brick Hollow too," my brother said. "Not many of them were good."

"…This isn't Brick Hollow," I said. "Nobody hits us here. But nobody calls us by name here either. All I know is that man is the only one who's ever said my name."

My brother looked up at that. "…He said your name?"

"…Yes," I said. "Elpis, he said."

"…That's not such a big thing," my brother said. But his words trailed off a little at the end.

"…It is a big thing," I said. "In this city we're just 'you.' Jeteo, Elpis. That's not a name — it's just an order in line."

My brother said nothing to that. I understood it wasn't a rebuttal — he just needed time to think. Whenever he thought that way, he always drew his chin in slightly. I'd seen that same habit in Father, once.

*For just an instant, I saw something other than doubt in my brother's face. Something like curiosity. It didn't last long.*

I wanted to hold onto that brief instant. It was the first time that expression had appeared on his face since Father disappeared. Watching it vanish again, I couldn't hide my disappointment.

"…Brother," I said. "Will you come with me, just once? To the flowerbed."

"…What's there to see?" he said.

"…Just look. You don't have to do anything but look. You don't have to ask anything either."

He didn't answer. I understood that wasn't a refusal. My brother was someone who said no right away when he didn't want something. Putting off an answer meant some part of him had already, a little, begun to move.

---

"…I don't trust that man either," my brother finally said. "I don't trust anyone in this city. You should be careful too."

"…Don't you trust anyone at all?" I asked.

My brother took hold of that paper — the one stamped COMPLETE.

"…I haven't found anything to trust yet," he said. "When I find it, I will. Not yet."

"…What do you think you'd need to find, to trust something?" I asked.

He thought a long while. He looked out the window, then turned his eyes back to me.

"…I don't know," he said. "But I think I'll know it, when I find it. What it is."

I took his hand. Over that paper stamped COMPLETE, my hand rested on top of his.

"…I'm going to keep watering it," I said. "That sprout."

"…Okay," he said. This time he met my eyes. "You do that. I'll keep doing this."

We sat a while that night without speaking. Outside the window, Haven's lights were lined up straight and even. Watching those lights, I wondered whether, beneath each one, there might be someone else like us.

I noticed that not one of those lights ever flickered. The lamps of Brick Hollow always guttered in the wind. Here, not a single light ever wavered. That steadiness felt, that day, strangely lonely.

"…Brother, do you remember the lamps in Brick Hollow?" I asked.

"…I remember," he said.

"…They kept going out. I hated that. But now I kind of miss it."

"…Me too," he said. "Going out meant something was alive. Nothing here ever goes out."

I thought about that for a long while. It occurred to me that lights that never went out, people who never cried, and faces that never showed surprise were all, somehow, the same thing.

That night I pressed my palm against the window. The glass was cold. Watching the lights still burning beyond that cold, I found myself wishing, strangely, that I could someday watch one of them go dark.

*I set this down as the last line of this chapter. My brother wasn't someone who couldn't trust. He was someone who hadn't yet found something to trust. I understood, for the first time that day, how large that difference was. And that night too, neither of us let go of the other's hand.*

*Putting this chapter back in order, I add one more thing. Before I fell asleep that night, I thought of the sprout again. A speck of green smaller than a fingernail. I didn't know, then, how large a thing that smallness held inside it.*
