# Chapter 24. Northward

> _Previously_ — Mena brought news of Father. A month ago he had come out alive from a well in the north, but with his name and face forgotten, and had vanished somewhere in the middle of counting three thousand bricks. For the first time since leaving Brick Hollow, the brother and sister wept together.

The next morning, my eyes were swollen. So were my brother's. And yet, strangely, my body felt lighter.

Morning sunlight was coming in through the window. Watching it, I learned for the first time how strange a morning can feel after a whole night of crying. Nothing in the world had changed. And yet I felt like an entirely different person.

*I understood then, for the first time, that tears didn't erase anything. They only lifted away, by their own weight, what had been piling up inside.*

Mena's house had one small window in its wall. I watched dust drift slowly through the shaft of light coming in through it. It was a peaceful morning, as if nothing at all had happened. That peace felt strange to me, so different from last night's tears. But that the two of them could exist inside the same day — that, too, I was learning for the first time.

The moment my brother opened his eyes, he checked the paper stamped COMPLETE again. It had become a habit by then. But that day was a little different. He looked at the paper for a long time, then folded it carefully and tucked it into his innermost pocket.

Something had gone out of my brother's face overnight. It took me a long while of thinking before I understood what it was. It was the tension that had clung to his face ever since Brick Hollow. His anger hadn't disappeared. What had ended was not knowing where to put it.

"…It's not evidence anymore. Not a way of holding onto his name, either," my brother said, seeing my expression. "Now this is… proof that we made it this far."

Hearing that, I felt all over again how completely my brother had changed in these last few days. When we left Brick Hollow, he had been a person moved only by anger. The paper in his hand now was no longer evidence of that anger.

"…Brother," I asked. "Did you sleep last night?"

"…A little," he said. "Crying made me sleepy."

He said it as if it were nothing. But to me it wasn't nothing at all. It was the first time my brother had ever spoken of his own crying so plainly.

*I realized something strange then. In Haven, we had never once cried, and never once truly slept. I hadn't known that those two things were bound together.*

I reached out and touched the dust settled on the windowsill. I watched it scatter in the shaft of light, then slowly find its place again. Watching that small movement, I thought that something inside us, too, must have scattered like that last night, and slowly settled back into place.

---

Mena asked while setting out breakfast for us.

Bread and dried fruit sat in a small bowl. Looking at that plain breakfast, I found it strange that even in this house, someone made a meal like this every morning. In a room crowded with paper, the smell of cooking felt like an odd kind of comfort.

"…What will you do now?"

"…We'll go north," my brother said. "To that well, and then follow whatever path Father walked from there."

His voice was different from last night. Sorrow still clung to it, but something unshaken had settled inside it now. Hearing it, I knew my brother had worked something all the way through, sometime in the night.

"…And if you can't find him?" Mena asked. It wasn't a test. She seemed to genuinely want to know.

My brother thought for a moment.

"…Then while we're looking, we'll tell everyone we meet about him," my brother said. "That way, little by little, more people will know Father's name."

Mena looked at him a very long time at that answer.

"…Do you know something?" Mena said. "That's exactly what I do. You just gave me the same answer I would have given."

My brother's face didn't know how to answer that.

Mena nodded. She didn't try to stop us.

"…I thought as much," Mena said.

"…Won't it be dangerous?" I asked.

"…It's dangerous," Mena said honestly. "But you're people who already knew that much when you left."

At that, my brother smiled for the first time — the first smile I'd seen from him in days.

"…Take whatever you need," Mena said. "I've heard it's colder to the north than here. Take another blanket, too."

"…Thank you," I said.

Mena folded the blanket and laid it on top of my pack as she spoke.

"…This is nothing," Mena said. "Not compared to the road you're about to carry."

My brother asked while repacking his bag.

"…Do you know if there's another well anywhere along our way?"

Mena thought a moment, then nodded.

"…I've heard of one," Mena said. "I don't know exactly where. But if you keep heading north, people will tell you. This world is more connected than you'd think."

"…How?" I asked.

"…By rumor. By footprints. Sometimes just by chance," Mena said. "You two got here that way yourselves."

"…Doesn't it seem like there's too much chance in it, though?" my brother asked.

Mena's hands stopped for a moment at that.

"…I used to think that too," Mena said. "But when there's too much chance, at some point it starts to feel strange, calling it chance at all."

"…Then what do you call it?" my brother asked.

"…I still haven't found a name for it," Mena said. "You two might find one before I do."

*I let that conversation pass by, at the time. Now I think it was the most important part of this chapter.*

After that, Mena helped us pack without another word. I felt that silence carrying forward the full weight of what we'd just said. Sometimes not answering is the more honest answer.

---

Before we left, Mena called me aside, alone.

She led me to one side of the room, to the shelves standing thickest with papers. Standing among all those names, I felt, strangely, at ease. I knew that every paper here had once been someone's everything — and it comforted me that all those everythings hadn't been thrown away, but gathered here like this instead.

"…I heard you've been keeping a record," Mena said. "Speon told me."

"…I just write down what happens," I said. "It's nothing special."

"…That's exactly what makes it special," Mena said. "That's how I started too. Just writing down what happened."

Mena handed me an old pen.

Its handle had gone smooth from long use. I took it in my hand and wondered how many years it must have carried stories out of someone's grip.

The tip had worn down and shortened. Looking at that wear, I thought of the old man's cane handle at the tent settlement. Everything old in this world wore down like that. In Haven, nothing ever wore down at all.

"…Was this his, originally?" I asked.

"…Yes," Mena said. "I think your hand needs it more than mine does now."

"…Keep writing," Mena said. "Someday, your father might need this record too."

*Taking that in, I felt tears rising again, strangely. I understood then, for the first time, that what I'd been doing wasn't so different from what my brother had been doing. He had tried to keep hold of a name. I had tried to keep hold of a story. We had only been holding the same thing with different hands.*

"…Doesn't it frighten you?" Mena asked. "Keeping a record isn't just writing down the good things. You have to write down days like today too."

"…It frightens me," I said. "But if I don't write it down, today just disappears. That frightens me more."

Mena looked at me a long time at that. Then, for the first time, she gave a real smile.

"…That's it," Mena said. "That's exactly why I can't let this work go. You're so much younger than me, and you already know that."

I didn't know how to answer that, so I only held the pen tight against my chest.

"…I'll ask you one thing," Mena said. "When you write, don't write down only what you've understood. Leave what you don't understand written as not-understood. More often than not, that's what turns out to matter most, later."

"…Why?" I asked.

"…What you've understood has already become yours," Mena said. "What you haven't understood still belongs to someone else. You have to leave it as it is, or it can't become real later."

*I've kept that from that day on. That's why so much in this ledger remains what I never understood.*

---

We left Mena's house that afternoon.

At the door, Mena hugged each of us once. It was a short, awkward embrace. I didn't get the sense she was someone who did this kind of goodbye often. And yet the hug was sincere.

"…Tell Speon," Mena said, last of all. "That I'm still waiting too."

My brother said he'd remember it exactly.

Hearing it, I felt again that something very old stood between those two. I found myself wondering, then, why Speon and Mena didn't wait together, in the same place. I thought I'd learn that answer too, someday.

"…Can I ask you one thing?" I said.

"…Go on."

"…Why do you two live apart?"

Mena rested a hand on the doorframe at the question.

"…If everyone guarding the same thing gathers in one place," Mena said, "then no one standing between them ever gets found."

I turned that answer over for a long time. The two of them weren't apart. They were standing in different places.

*I wondered, then, if the whole world might be built that way. People waiting, each in a different place, each in a different way. Maybe it was precisely because they never all gathered in one place that more people, somewhere, could find their names again.*

---

We left the earthen house behind and turned north. The sun already hung close to the middle of the sky. I looked back once. Mena stood at her door, watching us go. It looked exactly like the way she must have watched Speon leave.

The road climbed almost at once. The ground grew rougher and rougher, and far off on the horizon I could just make out the blurred ridge of low mountains. Looking at that ridgeline, I felt that our journey had entered an entirely new stage.

*I thought of the first day we left Brick Hollow. That day, we had only been running. Now, our direction had a reason behind it. I understood that the difference made it a completely different kind of walking, even if, from the outside, it looked like the exact same steps.*

As he walked, my brother checked once more the two papers in his hand — the one stamped COMPLETE, and the one Mena had given him. He laid them together, neat and even, and tucked them back inside his coat.

"…This is where it really begins," my brother said. It sounded half like something said to himself, half like something said to me.

"…Yes," I said. "I think so too."

We walked without speaking for a while after that. I noticed the earth underfoot slowly changing color — from red to gray. I thought that change was proof we were leaving the Wilderness behind.

The wind changed too. The Wilderness's wind had been dry and stinging. What blew down from the north carried something damp in it. That dampness felt strange against my skin, and I rubbed my arms more than once. Breathing that scent in, I thought that Father must have felt this same wind. A month ago. Before us.

*That thought comforted me, strangely. We were following Father. Even if we never found him, one thing was certain — we were walking through the same wind.*

As I walked, I touched the pen tucked in my coat once. Rachel's pebble was still there too. I counted, one by one, everything this journey had given us. One pebble, one pen, two papers. And a handful of people's names.

Father had counted bricks. From that day, I decided I would count people.

As the sun began to sink, my brother stopped and looked back. I turned and looked back too. The road we'd traveled stretched away into the distance, hazy and far. Haven. The tent settlement. That well. The House of Records. It felt strange to think all those places now lay behind us.

I counted how, at every one of those places, someone had given us something. Noa had given us a place. Rachel had given us a stone. The old man had given us a rumor. Speon had given us water. Mena had given us a name. We had walked this whole way receiving, again and again, without ever repaying a thing.

"…Will we ever be able to pay it all back?" I asked.

My brother thought a moment.

"…Probably not," he said. "But maybe we can just give it to someone else instead. That seems to be how everyone here does it."

*That my brother was the one who said that was, to me, the most surprising thing that whole day. In Haven, he had always been someone who calculated — what you filed, and what you got back for it. But that day, for the first time, he accepted a debt he couldn't repay, without doing the math on it at all.*

"…We've come so far," I said.

"…Yeah," he said. "But there's still more road ahead."

"…Aren't you scared?" I asked.

He looked at me and smiled. A very small smile, but a clear one.

"…I'm scared," he said. "But it's all right now, because I'm not scared alone."

At that, I took my brother's hand. He took mine back. We started walking again, just like that, hand in hand.

Before the sun went fully down, its last light stretched our shadows long across the ground. I looked down at the two of them, lying side by side, reaching out ahead of us. When we'd been running, our shadows had always seemed to chase us from behind. Now they pointed the way before we did.

*I set down here the last sentence of this Part. We began walking north. Whether we could find Father, and whether he would know us even if we did — none of it was certain. But that day, for the first time, I saw something close to certainty on my brother's face. It wasn't the certainty of someone who had every answer. It was only the certainty of someone who had a reason to keep walking.*
