# Chapter 17. Toward the Sunrise

> _Previously_ — The old man of the tent camp told them an old rumor — that the completed go "to a well," and that a "Well-Keeper" watches over it. The brother and sister said goodbye to Noa and the children and set out toward the sunrise to find that well.

We had been walking for three days.

The sun rose in the east that day too, exactly as it always did. We walked straight into it. On the first day the glare kept forcing my eyes shut; by the third day I'd learned, a little, how to keep them open against it. The ground grew drier with every mile. The grass thinned more and more.

The water Noa had packed for us ran out on the second day. My brother grew quieter from then on. I knew he was holding back his thirst.

I remember the moment we shook the water skin and heard nothing at all. My brother stared at it a long while, then simply slung it back over his shoulder. He said nothing. I understood what that silence meant. It meant there was nothing left to be done.

*For the first time, I understood how far from a given thing water really was. In Haven, water had simply always been there. Even the thought of being thirsty had been a luxury. Out here, a single mouthful of water could decide the whole shape of a day.*

*I watched his stride closely then. What had started long and strong was growing shorter, step by step. But his direction never once wavered. His body was tiring, and yet his mind seemed to be growing clearer, not dimmer.*

As I walked, I looked down at my own shadow. Long in the morning, short at noon, long again by evening. Watching it change, I felt that a day wasn't simply something that passed — it had a shape of its own. In Haven, time had been one smooth, unbroken line. Out here, time had contours.

*I learned then that thirst was a more urgent sense than hunger. Hunger could wait. Thirst would not.*

My tongue kept sticking to the roof of my mouth. I tried to swallow and found there was almost nothing left to swallow. My brother kept glancing at me sideways without breaking stride. I knew he was thirstier than I was and worrying about me more anyway.

Every time the wind blew, fine sand struck my face. It gathered at the corners of my eyes, between my lips, along the back of my neck. I rubbed at the grit with the back of my hand for a while, then gave up. It only settled again as soon as I brushed it away.

There was no sound but our own footsteps. No birds, no insects. I thought about how that quiet differed from Haven's quiet. Haven's had been a quiet with the sound erased out of it. This was simply a quiet that no one had made a sound in yet.

"…Are you all right?" he asked, again and again.

"…I'm all right," I answered every time. I wasn't, really. But looking at his face, the words came out on their own.

The dry bread Noa gave us was gone within a single day too. Chewing it, I thought of the perfect bread of Haven. This bread was hard and salty. And still, I liked it better. It was bread someone had made by giving up their own.

*For the first time, I understood there were two ways hunger could be filled. Filled by having your case received. And filled by being shared with.*

The crust scraped going down my throat. Even that roughness, strangely, was welcome. Swallowing hard bread with a dry throat wasn't an easy thing. But I didn't especially want to avoid that discomfort. For as long as I was eating that bread, it felt as though I were sitting by that fire again, with Noa and Rachel.

Even after the bread was gone, I kept turning the crumbs left on my hand over and over. It felt wasteful to let them go. In Haven, nothing had ever been anything to waste — if you needed more, you simply filed for it again. I was learning that feeling, wastefulness itself, all over again, here in the Wilderness.

"…Are you going to eat the crumbs too?" my brother asked. It wasn't teasing.

"…Yes," I said. "It's the last of the bread."

He laughed a little at that, then tipped his own last crumbs into his mouth too.

"…When Noa packed this for us," he said, "she'd taken it out of her own share. I saw that, and I took it anyway."

"…You were right to take it," I said. "If you hadn't, it would have hurt her more."

"…Is that so," my brother said.

He said nothing more for a long while after, just looking down at his own empty palm.

We walked on without speaking for a while after that. The sun was climbing higher. As I walked, I rolled Rachel's little pebble in my hand. The smooth feel of it against my palm was strangely calming.

*Walking with that stone in my grip, I thought about how all we had left, on this journey, was this one pebble and the two papers in my brother's shirt. But strangely, the less we had, the larger each single thing seemed to grow.*

Far off, heat shimmered up off the ground like a mirage. I kept narrowing my eyes, thinking I saw something beyond that wavering air. Every time, it turned out to be nothing but the bare horizon. I grew used, little by little, to seeing things that weren't there. The desert seemed to have a talent for showing a person what wasn't real.

---

On the afternoon of the third day, my brother stopped walking.

"…Look at that," he said, pointing at a few low, stunted trees. "There might be water somewhere like that."

I followed the trees with my eyes. They were a little greener than everything around them. I learned then, for the first time, that a difference in color alone could tell you something. In Haven, everything had been the same brightness, the same shade. Out here, the smallest difference in color could mean the difference between living and not.

From then on, as we walked, I kept scanning the horizon. Whenever I caught even the faintest hint of a different green, I stopped. Most of the time it turned out to be nothing. But I found I didn't even mind those false alarms. Opening my eyes wide, straining to find something, was itself a new sensation.

We went over. There really was a small pool. Muddy water. Not clear.

There was shade under the trees. We sat there for a while. My body, baked all day in the sun, slowly cooled. I hadn't known until then how welcome that coolness could feel. In Haven, shade had never been necessary — wherever you went, the temperature was always just right.

"…So this is what shade feels like," I murmured.

My brother huffed a laugh at that.

"…You keep saying things like that lately," he said. "That the things we took for granted weren't actually a given."

"…Yes," I said. "I feel like I'm learning it all over again, every day."

"…Is it all right to drink?" I asked.

My brother looked at the water for a long while.

"…If we were in Haven, the Ledger would already have filtered it for us," he said. "Here, no one filters it for you. We have to do it ourselves."

*I listened to that, watching him filter the water through the hem of his shirt. It wasn't perfect. Still, when I drank it, I felt, in every part of my body, the sensation of being alive.*

He dipped the cloth in the water and wrung it out again and again, several times over. His hands turned the color of the earth. Watching them, I felt my brother was becoming someone who could do things for himself. In Brick Hollow he had carried bricks because he was told to. In Haven he had filed things because the Ledger told him to. Now he was doing something no one had told him to do at all.

The water smelled of earth. It left a rough feeling going down my throat. But strangely, even that roughness was welcome. Haven's water had never tasted like anything at all. This water had a taste. A bad one, but the fact that it had any taste at all was, to me, an entirely new thing.

He filtered my share before he filtered his own. The second straining came out a little clearer than the first. He gave me the clearer one.

"…Filter yours clear too," I said.

"…I already drank some earlier," he said.

It was a lie. I had seen him not drink a single mouthful. But I didn't call it out. Some lies, I understood then, take back what a person just gave you, the moment you point them out.

He folded the cloth back up and said.

"…Good thing we learned this one. Next time we find a puddle like this, we'll know what to do."

I could hear in that how he'd started treating this road as a time of learning. When we were running, there had been no room for that kind of ease. Now there was.

We rested beside the pool a while longer. Looking up at the sky, my brother said.

"…In Haven, I never even thought about being thirsty. The moment I thought it, the water was already there."

"…Was that a bad thing?" I asked.

"…It wasn't bad," he said. "But this one mouthful of water right now feels so much bigger than that perfect water ever did."

---

That night, my brother spoke up suddenly by the fire.

"…I've been having a strange thought lately."

"…What is it?"

"…When we were in Haven, everything was perfect. But I feel like I was more dead back then. Now everything's short of enough… but I feel more alive."

*Hearing that, I felt my brother changing, little by little. He was still angry. But something else was growing underneath that anger.*

I couldn't put an exact name to what was growing there, under the anger. It was still too small to be called hope. But it wasn't only anger, either. I thought it might be another face of his questioning. He was still asking "why." But that "why" seemed to be turning, little by little, from a why aimed at blaming someone, into a why that simply wanted to know.

"…Is that a good thing?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he said. "But I don't hate it."

The fire burned low. I sat quietly, looking at my brother's face lit by that light. In Brick Hollow, his face had always looked like it was enduring something. In Haven, it had looked like it was doubting something. Now it was neither. It was just my brother's face, sitting there as itself.

"…Elpis," he said suddenly. "Aren't you scared? Going toward some place we don't even know what it is."

"…I'm scared," I said honestly. "But it's better than not looking for anything at all."

My brother nodded at that. He didn't say anything else. I found the silence comfortable.

The fire was dwindling. I pushed the last piece of wood into it. The flame leapt up briefly, then settled low again. Watching that small flicker, I thought our journey, too, was moving forward in flickers like that. Flaring high, sinking low, and never quite going out.

The sky was full of stars. Looking at them, I found myself wondering, out of nowhere — had those same stars been above Haven's sky too? I couldn't remember at all. While we were in that city, there had never been a reason to look up. Everything was resolved below, right at our feet.

The stars looked countable at first, but the moment I tried, there only seemed to be more of them. I gave up counting and simply looked. Looking needed no reason at all.

*That was the first time I understood that a person needs, sometimes, to spend a long while looking at something utterly useless. The Ledger could never have filed a time like that. There had been no problem in it to resolve.*

"…Brother," I said.

"…Yeah?"

"…I think this is the first time I've ever looked at the stars. Like this, this long."

He looked up at the sky with me.

"…Me too," he said. "It's strange. We slept under that same night sky every night in Brick Hollow."

"…Maybe we didn't have the strength to look, back then," I said.

He only nodded at that, saying nothing more.

*I would come to remember that night for a very long time. The night I gave up counting the stars, and simply looking was enough. I didn't know then that the time spent looking long at something for no reason at all was the very first thing that city had ever taken from us.*

---

On the morning of the fourth day, a low hill appeared far off on the horizon. Something seemed to be standing on top of it. It was too far to see exactly what.

"…Could that be the well?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he said. "But we won't know until we get there."

We started walking toward the hill again. Our feet ached and our throats were still a little dry. But that morning, for the first time since leaving Brick Hollow, I felt that we were "going toward" somewhere. Not running away. Going.

Our steps picked up. I could feel my brother's pace quicken along with mine. The thing on the hill was still only a blur, but neither of us could tear our eyes away from it.

"…What if it's nothing at all?" I asked. The worry slipped out before I could stop it.

"…Then we walk some more," my brother said. There was no wavering in his voice. "We've been walking this whole way anyway."

That answer put my mind at ease. My brother was speaking now like someone no longer clinging to the outcome.

*I wrote his words down separately, in my heart, right then. If it's nothing, we just walk some more. I knew that a sentence like that could never have come out of him back in Brick Hollow. There, walking itself had already been a punishment.*

The closer we got to the hill, the clearer the shape became. Something like the outline of a stone wall came into view. I kept walking without being certain what it was. Walking without certainty — that, too, was something I learned for the first time that day.

My legs had gone nearly numb by then. But strangely, my steps seemed to be growing lighter, not heavier. I thought it was because my heart, not my body, had already arrived at that hill. My brother was matching my pace, step for step. Neither of us bothered asking the other "are you all right" anymore. In that moment, both of us were, in fact, all right.

The wind shifted slightly. In it I caught the faintest trace of moisture. The smell of water. A smell we hadn't caught once in three whole days. That smell alone was enough to bring tears to my eyes. Until then I hadn't known the smell of water could be caught so clearly.

*I set that difference down as the last sentence of this chapter. Running looks behind. Going forward looks ahead. That day, I understood that we were, for the first time, walking with our eyes forward.*
