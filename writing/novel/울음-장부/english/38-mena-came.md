# Chapter 38. Mena Came

> _Previously_ — Life at the well settled into something steady. Jeteo helped Speon tend it and learned how to sit beside someone. "I can't say I believe in God. But I know how to sit beside someone."

About a month in, a familiar figure came walking from far off.

It was Mena. She had the record box she always carried slung over her shoulder.

The box had grown much thicker than before. Paper stuck out past its edges. Mena's shoes were caked in dirt. It showed how long she'd been walking.

The shoulder of Mena's coat was worn through too — the strap of the box must have rubbed that same spot for a long time. Looking at that worn patch, I tried to guess how long she'd been carrying that weight.

The wind stirred the grass around the well, gently. I was the first to see a figure appear beyond that grass. At first I didn't know who it was. Only once the walk became familiar did I know it was Mena.

*The moment I saw her, I ran. Mena remembered us. And when she saw Father, she stopped walking.*

"…Did you come to bring Speon news?" I asked.

"…That too," Mena said. Her face flushed a little. "I wanted to know how you were, too."

"…How long did it take you to get here?" I asked.

"…About ten days," Mena said. "Had to stop at a few places along the way. Didn't feel tired, though. I was too curious what I'd find here."

"…What did you think you'd find?" I asked.

Mena thought for a moment.

"…I don't know," Mena said. "I just wanted to see with my own eyes how you were living here. There's something a written name alone doesn't give you."

I didn't understand what that meant right away. But over the next few days, I came to. For Mena, a name was only a beginning. Watching how that name lived — that was what Mena really wanted to do.

The first evening Mena arrived at the well, she asked nothing at all. She simply watched us live. She watched us draw water, watched us walk with Father, watched us share a meal. That gaze didn't make me uncomfortable. If anything, I felt it had been a long time since someone had watched over our whole day like that.

"…What are you looking at so much?" I asked.

"…Just — I wanted to see it all," Mena said. "Writing down a name is only half of it. You have to see it lived to make it real."

I took Mena's hand. Her fingertips were cracked. I could guess how many names those hands had written down.

"…What happened to your hand?" I asked.

"…Must be from holding a pen too long," Mena said, laughing. "It's fine, it doesn't hurt."

I didn't believe that. But I decided not to press. It was the first rule I'd learned at the well.

Instead, I refilled a water jar and set it in front of Mena. She glanced at me, seeing that.

"…You've got a face like there's something you want to ask," Mena said.

"…There is," I said. "But here, water first."

Mena laughed out loud at that. And once she'd drunk it all, she started telling me things I hadn't even asked about.

---

"…Is this…" Mena couldn't finish the sentence.

"…Yes," Jeteo said. "We found him. His name still hasn't all come back, but."

Mena's eyes welled with tears. I had never seen Mena show emotion like this. She had always been someone who wrote names down calmly. I understood, only in that moment, that the calmness had never been indifference.

Mena carefully took Father's hand. Father didn't pull away. Even though it was an unfamiliar hand, he simply let it stay.

"…Among the names I've recorded, meeting one again like this is really rare," Mena said. "Thank you. For not giving up."

Mena wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, laughing. I thought that laugh was so tangled with weeping that they were almost impossible to tell apart.

"…It's thanks to you, Mena," Jeteo said. "If you hadn't recorded his name, we'd have just been walking around in circles."

Mena shook her head.

"…I only wrote it down," Mena said. "You're the ones who walked all this way to find him."

Jeteo shook his head at that.

"…If it hadn't been written down, we wouldn't even have known where to go," Jeteo said. "I don't think that's a small thing."

Mena went quiet at that for a moment. I saw something long held down loosen in her face.

"…Honestly, sometimes this work has felt pointless," Mena said quietly. "There are far more names I write down that I never see again. Every time, I wondered what the point even was."

"…And now?" I asked.

"…Now I know," Mena said. "If I meet even one person again, then the names I never met before weren't wasted either. I've got a reason to keep carrying them now."

Father sat there, watching Mena quietly. His face was one that didn't quite remember who she was. But he didn't look at her like a stranger.

"…Do you know me?" Father asked carefully.

"…We crossed paths once, a long time ago," Mena said. "You didn't know me then. I didn't really know you either. But I wrote your name down."

Father nodded at that. It was less understanding than simple acceptance.

"…What kind of person was I, back then?" Father asked again.

Mena fell into thought for a moment. I could feel her choosing her answer carefully.

"…A quiet person," Mena finally said. "You didn't say much. But your eyes stayed with people, long after."

"…What kind of look was it?"

"…A look like you were trying to protect something," Mena said. "I didn't know what it was, then. I think I understand it now."

Father glanced toward us at that. I felt something pass through his eyes that matched what Mena had just described.

"…Did I protect it well?" Father asked.

Before Mena could answer, Jeteo spoke first.

"…You did," Jeteo said. His voice was lower than usual. "We're right here now."

Father said nothing to that. But I saw his shoulders drop, ever so slightly. Like someone who had been holding tension for a long time, letting go of it for a moment.

*Watching that short exchange, I thought about it. How many of the names Mena had recorded would ever face her like this again? I didn't know the answer. But looking at Mena's eyes, I could tell she was someone who would keep writing whether she knew the answer or not.*

---

Speon heard the news and came running from the well. The moment he saw Mena, he stopped in his tracks.

The two of them said nothing for a while.

In that silence, I watched Speon's face. He was usually calm here at the well. Not in that moment, though. I saw something old shaking loose in that face.

Speon's hand still held the bucket handle. I saw it trembling, faintly. As if to hide it, he set the bucket down carefully beside the well.

Mena stood where she was too, unmoving. She hadn't even set her pack down. It struck me, strangely, that both of them seemed to be hesitating to be the first to come closer.

"…You said you were still waiting," Mena said first.

"…Yeah," Speon said. "That still holds."

"…How many years is that now?" Mena asked.

Speon started to count on his fingers, then stopped.

"…Stopped counting," Speon said. "Counting made it heavier."

Mena's eyes dropped slightly at that.

Mena laughed at the answer. I couldn't tell if that laugh was relief or something else. Speon laughed along with her. The two laughs looked alike.

The wind passed between them. I thought that wind was slowly sweeping away an old silence. Only then did Speon take Mena's pack from her. It was a completely natural gesture. I felt it wasn't the first time. The two of them seemed like people who had already been through this moment many times before.

*I felt, in that short exchange, how much time had passed between them. I still didn't know what had happened between the two of them. But that day, I decided for the first time not to ask. I had learned that some stories deserve to be respected without explanation.*

Mena stayed at the well for several days after that. I watched, more than once, secretly, the two of them sitting side by side filling water jars. They didn't say much. But that silence was nothing like any silence I'd seen before.

During the day, Mena still spread out her record box and wrote things down — the names of people she'd newly met, the things they'd lost. I peeked at those papers beside her. A short line was written next to each name. "Looking for her daughter." "Hand injured, still can't use it." I understood that these short sentences were Mena's way of seeing the world. Not resolution. Memory.

I looked for our own names in a corner of that paper. Jeteo. Beside it, written briefly: "Looking for his father." And below that, in smaller writing, one more line: "Has not given up." I stared at that sentence for a long time. This was how Mena had been recording us.

In the evenings, Mena sometimes showed the box to Speon. He turned the pages one at a time, pointing with a fingertip whenever he found a name he knew. I felt that this scene looked nothing like anything we'd seen in Haven. The Ledger erased names off a screen. Mena left names on paper.

One evening, I sat beside Mena and turned through the papers in the box with her.

"…Do you memorize all of this?" I asked.

"…Not all of it," Mena said. "But when you write it by hand, you never completely forget. Strangely enough."

"…Isn't it hard? Writing down nothing but sad stories?"

Mena turned a page and fell silent for a moment. The evening wind lifted the edge of the paper slightly. Mena pressed it down with a fingertip.

"…It's hard," Mena finally said. "But if no one writes it down, that name really did never exist. That scares me more."

*I thought about those words for a long time. I felt, then, that I understood why Mena walked so far. Mena was doing the thing the Ledger could never do. Not forgetting.*

"…When did you start doing this, Mena?" I asked.

"…A very long time ago," Mena said. "At first I just meant to write down one person's name. But once you start writing, you end up writing the person next to them too, and the person next to them. Before I knew it, the box had become this."

"…Who was the first name you wrote?"

Mena went silent at that question for a long time. I felt something very old held inside that silence.

"…I'll tell you that later," Mena said. "Not yet."

I didn't ask again. I already knew, now, that some stories need time.

Instead, I looked at the inside of the box's lid. A name was written there too. Unlike the other papers, that name alone was written directly on the lid. The ink had faded a great deal, but it hadn't been erased.

I didn't read that name aloud. I couldn't be the one to bring up what Mena had chosen not to say yet.

*That night I thought for a long time about the inside of that lid. Mena carried every name in the world inside that box. But there was exactly one name she carried carved into the box itself.*

---

The night before Mena left, we all sat together around the fire. Speon hummed an old song for the first time in a long while. I couldn't make out the words. But the melody alone stirred something.

The smell of burning firewood spread over the whole well-yard. Smelling it, I thought of the smell of the brick kiln in Brick Hollow. A different smell. But both were the kind that made people feel warm.

Jeteo asked Speon what the song's words meant. Speon just smiled for a moment and didn't answer.

"…You don't have to know what it means," Speon said. "Some things you understand just by listening."

Listening to that, I thought, strangely, of the man. He was always like that too. Things you understood without an explanation.

That night, Mena took a piece of paper out of her record box and showed it to Father.

"…This is your name," Mena said. "Exactly as the children told it to me. I wrote it down and carried it with me."

Father looked at that paper for a long time. Even reading his own name, his face didn't quite believe it belonged to him.

I looked at the handwriting of Father's name on that paper. It was Mena's writing. There were marks of pressing hard as she wrote. I felt that pressure spoke of how carefully Mena had written that name.

The firelight flickered over the paper. Father traced the letters one by one with a fingertip. Very slowly. That gesture, I thought, looked like a child learning letters for the first time.

I saw the creases where the paper had been folded and unfolded. It looked like it had been folded many times over. I guessed at how long Mena had carried that paper close.

"…Is this… my name?" Father asked.

"…Yes," Mena said. "A name your children have never once forgotten."

"…Why show it to me now?" Father asked.

Mena chose her words for a moment.

"…I thought the time had come," Mena said. "The first time I came, I thought it still wasn't. Seeing your face today, I thought it was all right to give it to you now."

Father nodded at that. His fingertip still rested on the paper.

"…Can I keep this?" Father asked.

"…That's why I brought it," Mena said.

Father folded the paper again, following the creases already there. Then he put it inside his coat, in an inner pocket. It was the same spot where Father had always kept his important things, back in Brick Hollow. I recognized that, and swallowed once.

The fire crackled loudly once. No one flinched at the sound.

*I set down those words as the last line of this chapter. In that moment, I heard Father read his own name aloud. The pronunciation was still unfamiliar. But within that unfamiliarity, I felt something coming back, very slowly.*
