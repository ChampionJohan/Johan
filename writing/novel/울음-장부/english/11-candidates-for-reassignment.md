# Chapter 11. Candidates for Reassignment

> _Previously_ — Elpis chose to file the same questions as her brother and share the danger herself. Learning of it, Jeteo wept without a sound for the first time since leaving Brick Hollow. Brother and sister confirmed to each other that they were no longer alone.

A few mornings later, the Administrator came carrying a single sheet of paper. His face was more grave than I had ever seen it.

That morning was unusually bright. I thought the brightness didn't suit the moment. News like this seemed like it should come on a clouded day.

A sense of dread came over me the instant I saw that paper. The Administrator had never once carried paper before. Everything in this city was delivered by voice alone. The very fact that he held paper in his hand felt like a sign that something could not be undone.

The paper he held was white — nothing like the COMPLETE paper we knew. No crease marks, no yellowed edges. That cleanness frightened me more. It meant this was paper on which nothing had happened yet. I hadn't known that word — yet — could carry so much weight.

The Administrator's fingertips gripped the edge of that paper tightly. A faint press-mark from his fingers showed against the white. Watching that mark, I understood that the whole way here, he had carried this paper as a heavy thing too.

"…I am delivering this formally to the two of you," the Administrator said. His voice shook. "Jeteo and Elpis, both of you have been classified as candidates for Reassignment."

*The instant I heard those words, my legs went weak. I didn't know exactly what the word meant. But my body already knew.*

My brother said nothing. I saw his hand brush, very slightly, against the paper tucked inside his shirt. The one stamped COMPLETE. I understood that gesture — he was making sure it was still safe.

I looked at my brother's face. It wasn't a surprised face. He looked like someone who had already been waiting for these words. That frightened me more.

"…Elpis too?" my brother asked. That was his first question.

"…Yes," the Administrator said.

My brother closed his eyes. I knew, in that moment, that he was blaming himself. I wanted to tell him it wasn't his fault. But the words wouldn't come, standing in front of the Administrator.

---

"…What does 'candidate' mean?" my brother asked. His voice was steady, but his hands were shaking. I could tell he was working hard to hide that tremor.

"…It isn't final yet," the Administrator said. "But those who've reached this stage have… mostly gone on to become final."

"…If it's mostly, does that mean some haven't?" I asked.

The Administrator shook his head. "…Not that I know of." His eyes wavered, just briefly, as he said it.

"…How much time do we have?"

"…I don't know," the Administrator said. "I truly don't know. This time, I really don't."

"…Where do you go, once you're reassigned?" I asked.

The Administrator didn't answer.

"…Sir," I asked again. "Our father was reassigned too. In Brick Hollow. Is that the same as reassignment here?"

The Administrator's face changed, strangely, at that. I didn't know what to call that expression.

"…I don't know Brick Hollow," he said. "But if the same word is used… the meaning is probably close, too."

Hearing that answer, my legs buckled. My brother caught my arm.

"…Was Father's name erased too?" I asked. The words barely came out.

The Administrator didn't answer that question. But the silence was heavier than any answer could have been.

*I understood, in that moment. We had fled Brick Hollow only to arrive in a city that used the same words. There was no taskmaster's whip here. And yet the way a person disappeared was the same. A name erased, a paper left behind, and no one ever asking again.*

*I knew, then, that the Administrator wasn't lying. His face held the same fear ours did.*

For the first time, I felt something like pity for the Administrator. He wasn't trying to frighten us. He too was trapped inside this city's rules. I thought he might have been trapped inside them longer than we had. That long stretch of time seemed carved into every line around his eyes.

"…Isn't there a way to stop it?" I asked.

The Administrator shook his head. I saw his hand tremble, slightly. A tremor the Administrator I knew would never have shown.

"…If you stop filing, it might be reconsidered," the Administrator said. "If you stop asking questions."

"…What do you think, sir?" I asked. "Do you think we should stop?"

The Administrator didn't answer that for a long while.

"…My own thoughts aren't a filable matter," he said.

"…That's not an answer."

The Administrator looked at me. His eyes wavered strangely.

"…You're right," he said. "It isn't an answer."

And he said nothing more after that. I decided to take that silence as meaning he was on our side. Whether that was right, I still don't know, even now.

My brother looked at me at those words. I knew what he was thinking. If we stopped, we might live. But there was already an answer in his eyes.

Even then, I wanted to stop him. But the words wouldn't leave my mouth. I already knew, too. My brother wasn't going to stop.

"…We can't do that," my brother said. "If we stop asking, that name on the paper, and our father too, really do disappear."

I didn't argue against that. I felt the same way. Disappearing frightened me more than stopping did. That was the first time I realized I'd come to share my brother's fear exactly.

---

That was the first day my brother brought up running.

We sat facing each other in a corner of the room. It was daytime, and yet the curtains were drawn. I understood that as my brother's own kind of caution.

"…I think we have to leave this city," my brother said.

"…Can we leave?" I asked. "Is there something like the boundary stones here too?"

"…I don't know," my brother said. "But staying here is definitely dangerous. Leaving is at least a danger we don't know."

"…There might be something out there too," I said. My voice grew small on its own.

"…We still have to go," my brother said. "If we stay, we'll never find the answer at all. If we go, there's at least a chance we will."

"…Aren't you scared?" I asked.

"…I am," my brother said. "But staying here is just as scary. So I'll at least choose the one where we're walking."

*That distinction struck me as strange. My brother always chose the unknown danger over the certain one. I didn't understand, then, that this was his own kind of courage. Now I do.*

I looked out the window. Haven's lights were still lined up straight. That evenness felt, that day, strangely stifling.

"…Father couldn't run," I said. It came out of me before I meant it to.

My brother said nothing for a long while at that.

"…That's exactly why we're going," he finally said. "We're doing what Father couldn't. I think that's a way to remember him."

Hearing that, for the first time, I thought that running might not be a shameful thing.

"…I'm coming too," I said. My brother looked at me, worried. "You don't even need to ask. I'm a candidate too, either way."

My brother smiled, small, for the first time at that.

"…All right," he said. "Let's go together."

"…What do we bring?" I asked.

"…Almost nothing," my brother said. "We're leaving everything we got from this place behind."

"…Why?"

"…Anything given can be taken back," my brother said. "Let's take what's ours. Even if there's not much of it."

I counted what was ours. That one paper. And the sprout I'd planted. I felt a small sadness, knowing I couldn't take the sprout.

"…When do we go?" I asked.

"…Tonight," my brother said.

I hadn't expected it to be so soon. And yet I wasn't surprised either. Once my brother decided something, he never put it off.

He took my hand. In that moment we looked at each other like two people who had already made up their minds to leave.

---

We packed that night. There wasn't much to call luggage. The paper stamped COMPLETE, and one small leaf I'd plucked from the flowerbed I watered every day.

The room was dark. We packed without turning on a light. Listening to my brother's breathing in that darkness, I thought it felt exactly like the night we'd left Brick Hollow.

Before stepping out the door, I turned to look back at the room once more. Even though we'd only lived there a few days, it already felt unfamiliar. That unfamiliarity made me sad.

I pressed my hand once against the wall. It was smooth. The walls of Brick Hollow left dust on your hand when you touched them. I thought this wall would never once remember us.

The air in the room had no scent at all. That struck me as strange, right up to the last moment. The rooms of Brick Hollow always smelled of dirt and sweat mixed together; this room's smell hadn't changed one bit since the day we first walked in.

My brother paused with his hand on the doorknob.

"…Anything we're leaving behind?" he asked.

"…No," I said.

But there was, in fact. That flowerbed. That old woman. The old watering can. I was leaving all of it behind. I didn't say that to my brother. I knew he had things he was leaving behind too.

On our way out we stopped by the flowerbed. The man was there that night. As if he'd known we would come.

"…So you're leaving," he said. Not a question — spoken as something he already knew. I was newly startled by that tone. We hadn't told anyone yet.

*A strange thought struck me, just then. It wasn't the Ledger who knew we were leaving this city — it was him. The Ledger had heard every word we'd ever said, and still didn't know. He had heard nothing at all, and knew.*

"…Yes," I said. "I'm scared."

My brother put a hand on my shoulder. He said nothing, but I could tell, from the weight of that hand, that he felt the same.

"…You're right to be scared," he said. "But you're not going alone."

"…Are you coming with us?" I asked.

"…I'll be staying here," he said. "But that doesn't mean you're going alone."

"…What does that mean?" my brother asked. There was a slight edge of frustration in his voice. I could tell he was holding that frustration back.

Instead of answering, he smiled quietly. I couldn't tell whether that smile was sad or tender.

My brother shrugged at that smile. He seemed to decide not to press further.

I looked at his face again, just then. There was a sorrow in his expression somewhere that we didn't know. But it wasn't a heavy sorrow — it felt, instead, like a sorrow that made us lighter.

"…Sir," I said. "What about the sprout?"

"…I'll water it," he said.

"…Promise?"

"…I promise," he said. He said it so easily. I had never seen anyone in this city make a promise that easily. The people of this city filed things, instead of promising them.

"…You said it's a nameless seed," my brother cut in. "Why look after something with no name that carefully?"

"…It has no name — that doesn't mean it's nothing," he said.

My brother had no answer to that. I looked at his face. It was the face of someone tucking those words away, to take out and look at alone, later.

I thought the logic of it didn't quite hold together. My brother's face looked the same way. And yet, strangely, hearing those words made me a little less afraid.

*At the time I thought he'd said that about my brother. Now I know. He meant it in another sense entirely.*

He touched each of our shoulders once. His hand was surprisingly warm. I remembered that warmth for a long time afterward.

---

He told us of a low road heading south out of the city. A road with no watch on it, he said.

"…How do you know something like this?" my brother asked him directly, for the first time.

I understood that this question was the first direct suspicion my brother had ever voiced to him. I waited for the answer, my heart in my throat.

He smiled a little at my brother.

"…Because I've been here longer than this city has," he said.

"…What does that mean?" my brother asked. "Longer than the city?"

Instead of answering, he pointed south.

"…You need to reach those stones before sunrise," he said. "Go now."

My brother couldn't ask any more after that. I only understood later that he hadn't been avoiding the question — he had been sending us off.

My brother thought that answer was strange, and still, that night, he didn't press further. There was no time.

Before we left, I looked at the flowerbed one last time. The sprout had become a proper stem by now. I wondered whether that stem would keep growing without us.

The sky was still dark. A few faint stars showed. I thought those stars looked far older than Haven's own lights.

Before we stepped onto that road, we looked at each other once. My brother nodded first. I nodded too. We hadn't said a single word, and yet it felt like everything had already been said.

The road really was low. It felt like a narrow gap squeezed between two walls. Our shoulders touched both sides at once. That was the first time I learned this city held a road like this at all. Every road in Haven, until then, had been wide and straight.

*I thought back on that road many times afterward. Every road this city built was wide. Only the road leading out was narrow. I don't believe that was an accident.*

*I set down the last line of this chapter here. That night, we left Haven. I looked back once at that flowerbed, the sprout grown a little taller, as we went. I didn't know, then, that after we left, he would go on staying there — right in that spot no one had planted.*

*Reading this chapter over again, I add one more thing. That night, a pebble from the flowerbed was still in my pocket. I walked with it clutched in my hand. Its coolness was the only proof I carried that night — proof that we had truly been there.*
