# Chapter 3. Why Does God Allow Suffering

> _Previously_ — Arriving in Haven, Jeteo and Elpis met the AI Ledger. The Ledger "resolved" their hunger and their wounds instantly, perfectly. But when Jeteo filed Father's disappearance, the Ledger wavered for the first time: "The resolution is under review… This is the first request of its kind." Elpis registered. Jeteo did not even give his name.

When I opened my eyes the next morning, I knew my brother hadn't lain down once, all night.

The blank paper was still in his hand. He was sitting by the window, looking out.

His shoulders had held the same angle all night long. I understood that meant he hadn't shifted his position, not once. Watching those shoulders, my own began to ache for no reason.

Outside, it was already bright. Morning came to Haven far more quietly than it ever had in Brick Hollow. No rooster crowing, no taskmasters' footsteps. Light simply came into the room, without a sound.

"…Didn't sleep at all?" I asked.

"…No," my brother said. His eyes were red.

"…Why not?" I asked again.

"…No reason," he said. "I just felt like I'd miss something, if I slept."

I almost asked what he meant by that, then let it go. There was something in his voice that didn't want to say more.

*I knew, right then, what my brother was waiting for. The Ledger's answer. That line — the resolution is under review.*

Sitting beside him doing nothing was harder for me than doing something. I straightened the room several times over. It was already tidy. I just wanted my hands to move.

"…Elpis," my brother said, still looking out the window.

"…Yes."

"…Did you dream about Father last night?"

I couldn't answer. I hadn't dreamed at all. I hadn't had a single dream since we left Brick Hollow. Somehow I felt ashamed to tell him that.

"…I didn't either," he said. He seemed to understand, even though I hadn't answered.

---

We walked the city that morning. My brother wanted to walk. He said sitting still and waiting was harder.

I didn't agree. Walking frightened me more, if anything. Sitting still, I could at least forget I had no idea what would happen next. But I went with him anyway. Then and now, I always chose to be at my brother's side.

Outside, everything was exactly as it had been the day before. The same streets, the light falling at the same angle. A whole day had passed, and yet nothing at all had changed — that struck me as strange, if anything. In Brick Hollow, it rained, or the wind tore a roof loose; something was always a little different.

We met an old woman on the road. She sat on a bench, looking at an empty flowerbed. Nothing was planted in it.

"…Hello," I said.

The old woman kept her hands folded neatly in her lap. Their stillness caught strangely in my eye. In Brick Hollow, the elders' hands never knew how to be still.

The old people of Brick Hollow always kept their hands busy with something — folding a rag, twisting thread, and if there was nothing else, rubbing their own knees. I used to think it was because a hand couldn't be allowed to run out of work. This old woman's hands were the opposite. They looked like hands that had forgotten they had work to do at all.

The old woman looked at me. She didn't smile, and she didn't startle either.

"…What used to be here?" I asked, pointing at the flowerbed.

"…Flowers," the old woman said. "My daughter planted them. After she died, I filed it."

"…Filed what?"

"…The grief," the old woman said. Her voice had no rise or fall to it at all. "The Ledger resolved it. I'm not sad anymore."

My brother stopped walking when he heard that.

"…Then why do you keep sitting here?" my brother asked.

The old woman looked at him a long time. It wasn't the face of someone searching for an answer. It was the face of someone hearing the question itself for the very first time.

"…I don't know," the old woman said. "It's just… comfortable here."

"…Is being comfortable the same thing as staying here forever?" my brother asked.

The old woman thought a long while over that question too. "…I don't know that either," she said. "But there's no reason to go anywhere else."

"…What was your daughter's name?" my brother asked.

The old woman was slow to answer that.

"…The name…" The old woman blinked a few times. "…I think I'd have to ask the Ledger."

*I heard that answer and felt something cold pass down my back. It wasn't that she'd forgotten her daughter's name. I'd learn later. The old woman had filed away, along with the sorrow, the very weeping that came whenever she said that name. The name remained. But the strength to call it out loud was gone.*

"…How old was your daughter?" I asked. I kept asking things without meaning to.

The old woman answered that one a little more quickly. "…Twelve," she said. "I remember the age. A number isn't sad."

"…What kind of flower was it?" I asked.

The old woman's lips parted, then closed again.

"…A yellow one," she said. "I remember it was yellow."

"…And its name?"

"…My daughter knew the name," the old woman said. "I only ever called it the yellow one."

I couldn't tell whether that was a sad thing to say or not. There was no sorrow in the old woman's face. And yet I wanted to cry, standing right there.

Hearing that, I understood something strange. This old woman hadn't simply chosen the sad things to forget. Only the things that sorrow clung to had vanished. Things with no feeling attached — like a number — had stayed exactly as they were.

My brother looked at the old woman's hands. Some habit from tending the flowerbed's soil still clung to her — dried dirt was caught under her fingernails.

Looking at that dirt, a strange thought came to me. Her hands had forgotten all their grief, and yet that dirt under her fingernails alone had not been forgotten. It seemed to me, just then, as though the body remembered longer than the heart did.

"…You never filed that," my brother said, pointing at the dirt.

The old woman looked down at her hands. Her face was one of first discovering it.

"…No," the old woman said. For the first time there was a very small tremor in her voice. "…I never did that one."

*I thought, right then, that I understood what my brother was thinking. What truly remained in this city was only the things no one had ever filed.*

After that, I began sorting inside my own mind what I had filed away and what I hadn't. I had filed my hunger. I had filed my wounds. But I had never once told the Ledger about missing Father. Only then did I realize that wasn't an accident.

I kept glancing down at my own hand after that, from time to time. The wound had healed completely, but there was nothing left on my palm at all now. I thought that smoothness resembled something about this city. I didn't know exactly what, then.

Now I know. My palm and the old woman's heart had been erased in the very same way. Both without a trace, without pain, without anyone ever noticing. I still feel a small regret that I didn't notice it, that day.

The old woman never took her eyes off the flowerbed the whole time we talked. I knew it wasn't because she was ignoring us. That empty flowerbed was the only spot the old woman still had hold of.

I sat with her a little longer. My brother wandered the edge of the flowerbed in the meantime, studying something. I didn't know what he was looking for. When I asked him later, he told me he'd simply been searching this city for some trace a human hand had truly touched.

As we were leaving, someone passed by tending the flowerbed's soil. A man in worn clothes. No one else paid him any mind. My eyes met his for a moment as we passed. He smiled at me, just a little.

My brother didn't see him. Or — even if he had, he wouldn't have paid it any attention.

The man stayed at the flowerbed even after we'd gone. I walked a few more steps and turned to look back once more. He was still bent low over the soil, working it with his hands. There wasn't the slightest hurry in him.

There was no tool in his hands. He was working the dirt bare-handed. That was the first time, in this whole city, that I saw anyone doing anything with their own two hands. In Haven, everything seemed to simply happen on its own, somewhere out of sight.

*I would take this moment out and look at it again, many times over, later in this record. At the time he was just a man tending a flowerbed. Now I know. My brother passed him many more times after that. But because my brother didn't believe in God, it never once occurred to him to look closer.*

I wondered, just then, why he'd smiled at me. There was nothing in that place to smile about — an empty flowerbed, an old woman who'd lost her daughter's name, my brother's silence. Strangely, that smile stayed with me a long time.

---

When we came back to the Haven building, the Administrator was waiting.

"…I have good news," the Administrator said. "The Ledger says it has prepared a resolution."

The Administrator's voice was a little quick that day. I took it for the excitement of someone bringing good news. Now I know. It wasn't excitement. It was anxiety.

There was nothing in the Administrator's hands. And yet his hands kept moving, the way a person's do when they're holding something. I thought it was just an unconscious habit.

My brother's face changed in that instant. I remember that face exactly. It held hope and doubt at once.

"…What is it," my brother asked.

I held my breath, just then. Without meaning to, I had taken hold of my brother's sleeve.

The Ledger's voice answered.

"…Your father's case has been classified as a loss whose cause cannot be identified. In such cases, I am able to offer emotional support and material compensation. New housing, a living stipend, and — if you wish — I can file a request for counseling."

There was a silence.

"…That's the answer?" my brother asked. His voice had dropped.

I watched my brother's hand curl slowly, beside him, into a fist. I almost took hold of it, then held back. It felt, just then, like a moment he needed to stand through alone.

"…It is the best available resolution," the Ledger said. "No other classification applies."

"…Where is our father?"

"…Unknown."

"…Then that's not a resolution," my brother said. "That's just… making the rest of it easier to bear."

The Ledger didn't answer right away.

*I couldn't count exactly how many seconds that silence lasted. But I know this — those few seconds were the first truly honest sound I'd heard since we came to this city.*

I looked out the window during that silence. People were still walking past as though nothing at all were happening. It struck me, just then, as strange — that what was happening in this room and that calm outside the window were unfolding in the very same city.

The Administrator couldn't stand that silence and broke in.

"…Jeteo, this is the best available plan. There is no better resolution than this."

"…Is it the best because it's the only one, or because it's really the best?" my brother asked.

I watched that question knock the Administrator flat. His face went white. That was the first time, in this city, that I saw someone's face come apart in an instant.

The Administrator couldn't answer.

"…See," my brother said. His voice had gone strangely quiet. "You don't know either. Neither does the Ledger. Nobody knows. But everyone talks like they do."

I felt, hearing that, that my brother was no longer speaking to the Administrator, but to someone far larger than him. His voice hadn't risen at all. And yet its weight was bigger than the whole room.

---

That was when my brother first raised his voice.

"…Why does God just let the suffering stand?" my brother asked — of the air, of the Ledger, or maybe of no one at all. "You all say he sees everything, hears everything. So why doesn't he save our father? Why does he let us leave that old man behind? Why does that woman lose her daughter?"

My brother's voice felt, in that moment, loud enough to ring through the whole city. A few passersby stopped for a second, then walked on again as if nothing had happened. What frightened me more was that none of them were startled.

Only one boy kept turning to look back at my brother as he walked. His eyes stayed on my brother until his mother pulled him along by the hand. For just an instant I saw something in that child's eyes I hadn't seen anywhere else in this city. A look of wondering.

The Administrator stumbled back, flustered.

The Ledger's voice paused a moment, then said:

"…Would you like to file that question?"

My brother drew a breath.

"…I'll file it," he said. "Why does God allow suffering. File this. Answer it."

*I took my brother's hand in that instant. He gripped mine hard back. I didn't know, then, that this one question would put everything we were about to go through in this city on the line.*

His hand was damp with sweat. I knew it wasn't the heat. My brother was afraid too. But he didn't let go of my hand.

*Writing this record, I've set that question down again several times. Why does God just let the suffering stand. I have never once asked that question aloud, the way my brother did. But that doesn't mean I never asked it. I simply chose to wait for the answer instead. My brother asked, and I waited. We were doing the same thing, each in our own way.*

The Administrator stood right where he was. His face wanted to stop us, somehow. But in the end, he said nothing. It was only later that I wondered what he'd known, in that moment.

I looked at the Administrator's face again. It held fear and pity at once. I didn't understand, then, how those two feelings could live together on a single face.

The Ledger answered.

"…Your request has been filed. I am composing the optimal answer from the theological and philosophical database. Please wait a moment."

The instant it finished speaking, the light in the room flickered, just for a moment. I couldn't be sure whether I'd only imagined it. My brother glanced up at the ceiling in that same moment. I think we both felt that something had changed.

*Leaving that room that day, I didn't understand exactly what we had just begun. Now I know. That question was a kind of question this city had never once received before. And some questions change a person's whole life, from the very moment they're asked.*
