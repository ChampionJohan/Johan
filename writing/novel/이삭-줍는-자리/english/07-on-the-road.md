# Chapter 7. On the Road

> _Previously_ — On the third night, Glyke asks Xene to stop calling
> her Glyke and call her Pikra instead. She won't say what it means.
> Xene can't say the name aloud at first, then finally speaks it that
> afternoon. The two see the city for the first time, in the distance.

I can still draw the moment I saw that city, exactly, even now.

We were crossing a low hill. Pikra reached the crest first. I was a
few steps behind. Seeing her stop, I hurried up after her.

And I saw it.

*I tried writing this scene several different ways before settling on
this one. I tried* magnificent. *I tried* dazzling. *I crossed them
both out. Neither word holds what I felt, that moment. It took me a
long time to find the right word. Here it is — I was afraid.*

---

It was an unusually hot afternoon. The season was turning. Four or
five days into our walk, the air had begun to change. Mornings still
cool, but by noon the heat rising off the ground reached you through
the soles of your feet.

The dust had gotten worse from around then too. The dirt roads back at
the settlement turned to mud in rain and hardened when it was dry, but
they never raised much dust. The closer we got to the city, the more
dust gathered on the road. Every cart that passed kicked up a pale
cloud, and before it settled, the next cart was already through it.

"…Is this all dirt dust?" I asked Pikra.

"…No. A little different," Pikra said. "This time of year, threshing
dust mixes in too. It means someone's already beating grain,
somewhere. It rides the wind all the way out here."

I rubbed my nose. The inside of it felt gritty.

*I took that dust, at the time, for nothing but an annoyance. I
learned later. That dust meant the harvest was close. This whole city
was entering its busiest season of the year. We had arrived, as it
happened, right into that season.*

Fields stretched along both sides of the road. What had been scattered
patches at first grew closer together, until it all looked like one
endless plain. And that whole plain, every part of it, without
exception, had ripened to the same color.

That struck me as strange. Back at the settlement, even within one
field, the ripening speed differed — sunny patches came in first,
shaded ones later. Here there was none of that. It looked as if
everything had agreed to be planted the same day and ripen the same
day.

"…Here the whole field ripens together," I said.

"…That way you reap it all at once," Pikra said. After a moment she
added, "The seed is matched. The water's matched. The feed's matched.
It's all calculation."

*I took that, that day, for a remark about farming technique. Now I
know. It wasn't about technique at all. It was a miniature of what
this city does to people too. Matching everything. Refusing to let
anything grow differently.*

---

The Harvest was vast. It looked dozens of times larger than my whole
settlement. There was a wall, and even the wall alone was larger than
any building I'd ever seen.

Inside the wall, roofs packed close together. Roofs of every height,
overlapping into one great mass. And beyond that, fields spread out.
Even all the fields I'd ever seen put together seemed like they
wouldn't match this one.

The wheat was ripe. Golden, as far as the eye could see.

"…So much wheat..." I couldn't finish.

"…Yes," Pikra said. Something complicated was in her voice.

I looked at that field a long while. When the wind blew, the wheat
rippled like water. But the ripple felt strange. Too even. As if
someone had calculated exactly how it should be planted.

*Now I know. It really had been calculated exactly. That even ripple
was the beginning of the fear I first felt. I couldn't put it into
words then. I only felt that something was wrong.*

---

We stood a long while at the top of that hill. Pikra spoke first.

"…Thirty-some years," Pikra said. "Since I left there."

"…That long?"

"…It was before I married," Pikra said. "The walls weren't so high
then. The fields weren't so wide either."

"…The city grew bigger?"

"…Much bigger," Pikra said. "See that field there. That wasn't there
when I was a girl. There used to be a small woods, there."

I looked at the field again. Where a woods had been, there was now
wheat. Not a single tree, all even.

"…Where did the woods go?"

Pikra didn't answer. Probably she didn't know. Or knew and didn't want
to say.

*I got the answer later, from someone else. The woods had been
cleared. As the Harvest grew, the woods, the marshes, the fallow
ground — all of it became field. Nothing was left unused. I took that,
at first, for diligence. Later I found another word for it.
Leaving-nothing.*

---

We started down the hill. The road grew more crowded the closer we
came to the city.

Carts went back and forth. Carts loaded with wheat, empty carts, carts
carrying people. Everyone looked busy. No one slowed their pace.

One cart passed close by us without stopping and splashed us with
muddy water. Mud spattered Pikra's skirt. The driver didn't even look
back.

"…He didn't even say sorry," I said, a little angry.

"…They're busy," Pikra said, brushing off her skirt. No anger in her
voice. "Everyone's like that here. There's more to be gained from one
more step forward than from stopping to apologize."

That remark stayed with me.

*I took that exchange, that day, for nothing but a passing annoyance.
Later I learned this was a sentence that summed up how the whole city
runs. There's more to be gained from one more step than from an
apology — that's exactly how the Roll treats people, too. That's
precisely why there's no field for sorry.*

---

We walked the fields road all afternoon. Some sections had already
begun harvest.

I saw a machine cut wheat for the first time. It wasn't a person with
a sickle. A great piece of iron moved at a steady pace from one end of
the field to the other. Behind it came sacks filled not with bound
sheaves, but with grain already threshed clean.

I watched that machine a long while. It was far faster than any
sickle. What our household's three of us would have taken all day to
cut, that thing would probably finish in an hour.

"…Is that all wheat?" I asked, pointing at the sacks piled beside the
machine.

"…Yes," Pikra said.

"…One of those would feed our whole settlement for years."

Pikra said nothing. I didn't understand, then, what that silence
meant.

*Now I know. The arithmetic was right — one of those sacks could feed
our settlement for years. But something was missing from that
arithmetic. That sack was never going to our settlement. It already
had somewhere else to go. Abundance wasn't something shared. It was
something calculated, and assigned.*

A few people followed behind the machine, checking something. I
thought they were gleaners.

"…What are those people doing?" I asked.

"…Checking if anything fell," Pikra said. "Not gleaners from below —
that's Roll work. They're checking there's nothing left, because
there shouldn't be."

"…Why shouldn't anything fall?"

Pikra looked at me for a moment before answering.

"…Because it's a loss," Pikra said. "Wheat on the ground goes to no
one. They don't like that, here."

I turned that over as we walked. Wheat that goes to no one. I didn't
know, then, that the phrase held another meaning too. Fallen wheat
wasn't wheat that went to no one. It was wheat that was, by rights,
meant to go to someone like me.

*I would learn that a few chapters later. That afternoon, I only
marveled at the machine and walked on.*

---

As we neared the gate, a different kind of line came into view. Not a
line of people — a line of carts.

Carts loaded with grain stretched in a long queue before a smaller gate
beside the main one. Each one went in, someone came out and counted
the sacks, wrote something down somewhere, and only then let it
through.

I watched that scene a long while. It didn't take long for each cart
to pass. The counter's hands were fast. They didn't even touch each
sack. Their eyes swept it, they called a number, and wrote it down.

"…What are those people measuring?" I asked.

"…Quantity," Pikra said. "Which field it's from, how much came in.
That all goes into the record too."

"…There are records even for wheat?"

"…For wheat, and for people," Pikra said. Her voice dropped. "Here,
everything has a record. That's how this city runs."

Hearing that, I thought that tomorrow would be our turn to be counted
that way. Eyes sweeping, a number called, written down. It looked
quick and simple, for a cart. Whether it would be the same for a
person, I didn't know yet.

*I learned the answer the next day. A person is counted exactly the
same as a cart. The only difference is that a cart isn't afraid of
being counted.*

---

As the sun sank, we reached a village not far from the gate. Just
outside the wall, a settlement of people who made their living from
those passing through it.

A small market stood at the edge of the village. I'd never seen one
like it.

Every stall was piled with goods. Bread, dried fruit, cheese,
vegetables preserved in oil. One stall sold nothing but white bread.
Back at the settlement, we saw white bread once or twice a year, only
on holidays. Here it was just being sold, every day. Anyone could buy
it.

"…They sell that every day?" I asked.

"…Seems so," Pikra said.

I stopped a moment in front of the stall. The smell was good. Fresh-
baked bread. Breathing it in, I felt something that wasn't quite
hunger. I didn't know exactly what it was, then.

*Now I know. It wasn't envy. It was something stranger than envy. The
thought that something so common had never once, not once, been this
plentiful at our settlement. I'd thought scarcity was simply how
things were. Standing there, I felt for the first time that scarcity
wasn't the natural state of things — we had simply been on the side
where things were scarce.*

Pikra gave my hand a light tug.

"…Let's go," Pikra said. "We can't afford it."

I felt ashamed at that. Had I looked like I meant to buy something?

"…I was just smelling it."

"…I know," Pikra said. And smiled a little. "Smelling's free, so
that's fine."

We left the market and walked toward an inn. Walking, I kept glancing
back. The white bread on the stall stayed in my sight. No one hesitated
in front of it. People just came, set down money, and took the bread.
I found that ease strange. So common, and yet so at ease with it.

*I would meet that ease again, later. People were just as at ease in
front of the Roll. To someone who possesses the common thing, it looks
as though it was always naturally theirs. Someone who's never gone
without is clumsy at even imagining what it is to go without.*

We decided to stay there that night. Something like a small inn. We
had almost no money, but what Pikra had covered one night.

At night I looked at the city wall through the window. Lights were lit
in a row along the top. Those lights felt, somehow, like eyes watching
us.

"…We go in tomorrow?" I asked.

"…Yes. In the morning," Pikra said.

"…Are you scared?"

Pikra was quiet a long while before answering.

"…If you ask am I scared, I'm scared," Pikra said at last. "But scared
doesn't mean I'm not going."

I liked that. Admitting you're scared, and going anyway. I think that
was the day I first learned that this is what courage is.

*Courage isn't the absence of fear. I would confirm that sentence
again and again through the rest of this book. There wasn't a single
person who stood before the Roll unafraid. There were only those who
stood there afraid, and those who were too afraid to come at all.*

---

Before sleep that night, I asked one more thing.

"…Pikra." The name came fairly naturally now. "What will I be called
tomorrow?"

"…What do you mean?"

"…My name isn't on the Roll. What do they write, when I'm
registered?"

Pikra looked a little flustered by the question, for the first time.

"…They'll write Xene," Pikra said. "It's your name."

"…That's all? No more questions?"

"…There will be," Pikra said. "Where you're from, whose daughter you
are, who you married. They'll ask things like that."

I didn't know, then, how my answers to those questions would be
turned into arithmetic.

*I'll write, in the next chapter, about standing at the gate. Before I
close this one, I'll set down one thing. That night, before I fell
asleep, I said my own name aloud a few times. Xene. Xene. I sensed,
dimly, that the sound would carry a different meaning from tomorrow
on. I didn't know exactly what meaning.*

Outside the window I could hear insects crying. The same sound I used
to hear at the settlement. That gave me a strange comfort. However
large the city was, the insects that cried at night made the same
sound they'd made in our field.

*I remember that insect sound for a long time. After that night, I
could never again listen to it so easily. After I went into the city,
I developed a habit — even hearing insects cry at night, trying to
tell whether the field they came from had standing or not. That night
was the last night I ever slept without calculating anything.*
