# Chapter 20. The Administrator

> _Previously_ — At the corner, Xene spent more and more time near the
> quiet man. Somehow, there always seemed to be a little more when he
> was around. Xene believed it was chance.

The first time I met the administrator was the first time since my registration review. I'd only heard the name before that — the one who scores standing, who oversees the Roll. It had never once occurred to me that the position had a face.

The day I received a standing of zero at the gate, I learned that a post called "the administrator" existed. The one who assessed me that day wasn't the administrator himself, but someone working beneath him. The first time I actually saw the administrator was that day.

---

That day there happened to be a routine inspection at the corner zone.

I learned later that zones under Article 9 are inspected in person by the administrator several times a year — checking that the marker stands correctly, that the boundary is exact, that the clause is being kept. A formality.

I was sifting the dirt as usual that day. In the distance, I saw a few people walking my way. The one at the center was the administrator. I didn't know it at the time — his clothes were different from the other hired hands', and I only guessed he must be someone important.

The party numbered four. The administrator, two people carrying clipboards, and behind them, a young person walking a little apart. All of them moved slowly. No reason to hurry, it seemed. I only learned afterward that the young person was Lytron. That day I simply took him for one more attendant. Lytron said nothing that day. He stood near the administrator, looking over documents, occasionally jotting something down.

The two with clipboards worked through the zone item by item. Marker condition, boundary stake spacing, ditch drainage. Each time, they said "no irregularities" and made a note. I started counting how many times I heard that phrase that day, then gave up. Everything was without irregularity. There seemed nothing on that clipboard to note, besides the fact that I'd been sitting there for days.

---

The administrator was well along in years. His hair had gone half gray, his walk slow. Slow, but unwavering.

When the party reached the corner's boundary, the administrator stopped and looked over the whole zone. Checked the marker. Checked the ditch. Then he looked at me.

My body went rigid in that instant. There was no reason to run. I hadn't done anything wrong. But my body stiffened before I could think.

"…Unregistered?" the administrator asked one of the others. Not directly to me.

"…Yes. Comes every day, apparently." the other answered.

The administrator nodded.

---

The administrator walked closer to me. I stood where I was and waited. Part of me wanted to run, part of me wanted to ask something. In the end I chose not to run.

"…What's your name?" the administrator asked. The first thing he'd said to me directly.

"…Xene."

"…Xene." The administrator repeated it once. Not as though committing it to memory. Just confirming it.

"…I'm told you come here every day."

"…Yes."

"…What do you get?"

I hesitated a moment before answering.

"…Often not even a full day's food."

The administrator wasn't surprised by the answer. His expression didn't shift at all.

"…That would follow." the administrator said. "The loss rate is 0.02 percent."

I found the nerve, in that moment.

"…Why is my standing zero?"

The administrator looked at me a moment. This didn't seem to be the first time he'd heard the question.

"…It's a figure calculated from origin, history, relations." the administrator said. "You came from outside the city. You have no history here. And no registered relation to vouch for you. The calculation is exact."

"…So the calculation isn't wrong."

"…It isn't wrong." the administrator said. "It's only low. Wrong and low are different problems."

I couldn't help but admit that distinction was exact too. Admitting it only left me more frustrated. It would have been easier if I could have said he was wrong.

---

I felt, in that moment, as though I might be allowed to ask something more. Looking back, it was a strange feeling to have had. The administrator hadn't treated me unkindly. Nor had he been warm. But something in his manner seemed to draw speech out of a person.

"…Article 9 says the corner has to be left." I said. "The corner is there. Why is there nothing in it?"

The administrator looked at me a moment. Not eyes of anger.

"…The corner is there." the administrator said. "You can see it — the boundary stakes, the marker, a precisely designated zone. The law says to designate the corner. It never says how much is to fall there."

I turned that over in my mind.

"…So it's within the law, even if nothing's there?"

"…That's right." the administrator said. "The loss rate falling isn't a violation of law. It's a good harvest. The law says to leave the corner. It doesn't say to harvest poorly."

---

*I've gone back over this conversation many times. I was looking, then, for some crack in what the administrator said. A lie, a stretch, a dodge. There was none. Everything he said was true. I had nothing to say, standing before that truth.*

"…Then what am I supposed to do?" I asked.

For the first time, the administrator tilted his head, just slightly. He looked almost interested.

"…What do you mean, what are you supposed to do?"

"…The law is being kept. And I still can't eat."

"…The law is being kept." the administrator said.

I remember that tone even now. Not angry, not apologetic. The tone of someone stating a fact.

---

*I hold onto that sentence as though it were the title of this chapter. "The law is being kept." I hear that sentence again, many times, through the rest of this city's story. Always in the same tone. I never once found a flaw in that tone. That there was no flaw was the frightening part.*

*The overseer in Book Two was ashamed of his own work, I learned later, from Mena. He'd stay behind at night, going back over the ledgers alone. The adjudicator in Book Three faltered too, in the end — his hand shook, I was told, standing before the scales. Every time I heard those two stories, I thought: at least there was something, inside those two, that snagged.*

*This administrator was different. I never once saw him waver. His voice never rose. He never looked away, never hesitated over an answer. A few days later, passing that spot again, I tried, on the chance of it, to recall his expression. However hard I searched, I found nothing that snagged. The reason he never wavered was simple. He had no reason to. He truly hadn't broken anything.*

---

"…Do you know why the corner clause exists?" the administrator asked.

"…To leave something for those in need."

"…Correct." the administrator said. "And that's still exactly how it stands. No one blocks that place. No one drives you out of it. You can come every day. That is a right the law guarantees."

"…But there's nothing there."

"…That falls outside what the law guarantees." the administrator said. "The law guarantees the place. It never guaranteed how much would be in it."

I felt, in that moment, for the first time, how exact that distinction was — and how completely that exactness could leave a person with nowhere to stand.

"…Then who decides how much is left there?" I asked again.

The administrator looked at me a moment. For the first time, his face looked as if he were choosing his words.

"…That's a matter for harvest operations." the administrator said. "There are people who adjust machine placement, harvest speed, that sort of thing. I only confirm that the law is being kept. How much is left is not my remit."

It struck me as odd that this answer didn't sound like an evasion. It really was structured that way. This city had divided the work among so many hands that no one needed to answer for the whole of it.

"…Who are those people?"

The administrator glanced back, briefly. That was the moment Lytron looked up from his documents. I didn't know, at the time, what that meant. I only registered that the administrator had glanced at someone.

"…No need to concern yourself with that." the administrator said, turning back to me. "They're within regulation too. No one here has broken any rule."

*Writing this over again later, I've come back, many times, to that moment Lytron briefly looked up. Lytron told me, much later, that he remembered that day. He said it was the first time he'd ever seen, in the same frame, the number that was the loss rate and the person standing in the place that had made it.*

---

The administrator said a few words more, then turned to leave.

"…Keep coming." the administrator said, last of all. "No reason for me to stop you."

That could have sounded kind. I knew, even then, that it wasn't. It was simply a statement of fact. No reason to stop you meant, equally, no reason to help you.

*I turned that sentence over many times that night. "No reason to stop you." I came to think that sentence explained this entire city. No one stopped me. No one helped me either. Someone like me stood in the space between those two.*

The party left as they'd come. The ones with clipboards went first, the administrator following slowly behind. Lytron, moving last, turned back once. Our eyes met.

I thought, in that instant, that I'd seen that face somewhere before. I couldn't place it exactly. I let the thought pass, a stray one.

*I didn't learn until later that this was the same face that had first found me in Chapter 15. I didn't know it that day. My head was too full of the administrator to spare Lytron any thought. In this city, I kept crossing faces I would only recognize later.*

---

Back home, I told Pikra what had happened.

"…I met the administrator today."

Pikra's expression tightened slightly.

"…What did he say?"

"…That the law is being kept."

Pikra was quiet a long while before she spoke.

"…That's the most frightening thing he could say." Pikra said. "The overseer, the adjudicator — people like that at least know what they're doing, and it eats at them. That one doesn't."

"…Why not?"

"…Because he has nothing to feel bad about." Pikra said. "Because everything he says is right."

"…If it's right, why is that frightening?"

Pikra looked at me a long moment.

"…You can't even get angry at someone who's right." Pikra said. "You can fight back against someone who's wrong. Shout that it's unjust. Against someone who's right, you can't even do that. All you can do is sit there and listen."

I sat a long while, saying nothing, after she said that.

*I wondered, that day, how Pikra could know so exactly, without ever having met the administrator herself. I understand now. Pikra had already lived it, more than once. Standing in front of someone who is never wrong. Through those years — losing her husband, losing both sons, watching her own standing whittled away — Pikra must have met people like that many times over. People who did nothing wrong at all, and gave nothing at all.*

---

I kept going out to the corner after that.

Nothing changed. I filled about half a palm again the next day, and he still worked quietly beside me. All the administrator's visit had left behind was a marker standing a little straighter than before.

For days after, I kept turning his words over. "The law guarantees the place. It never guaranteed how much would be in it." I kept coming back to that sentence — walking, sifting the dirt, even lying down to sleep. It felt as though there had to be some gap in it somewhere. However hard I looked, there wasn't.

*I realized, around then, that this had been the first time I tried to argue with this city over something. The argument never got off the ground. An argument requires the other side to say something wrong, and the administrator never once did. That was the day I first learned that against something true, there's no argument to be had at all.*

I met the administrator again much later. By then I had different questions. That day, I only walked home turning over why the words "the law is being kept" could leave a person feeling so utterly without ground beneath her.

*Writing this chapter, I felt the pull, more than once, to paint the administrator as worse than he was — to write his voice cold, his expression scornful. That would have been a lie. The administrator never once raised his voice at me. He never mocked me. He was simply exact. That day I learned, for the first time, how frightening an exact person can be.*

*I will never, anywhere in this book, write a scene where the Roll breaks the law. The helplessness I felt that day, standing before the administrator, is exactly why. If it had been unlawful, I'd at least have had somewhere to fight — someone to appeal to, someone to protest against. Before what is lawful, there is no such place at all. I felt that absence of a place, that day, with my whole body. And that absence of a place is what this book, from beginning to end, turns out to be about. I didn't know that then. I know it now.*
