# Chapter 2. Three

> _Previously_ — In the outer settlement, Xene married into Glyke's
> household at fourteen. She lost her husband, and not long after, his
> brother, to an accident at the water channel. Glyke lost a husband
> and two sons in order, and each time, her standing on the city Roll
> quietly fell. That winter, Glyke looks toward the city and laughs — a
> different laugh than before.

That winter was the first time the number three frightened me.

Three is a small number, ordinarily. Three fingers, three steps. But
that winter I learned how large three becomes once it's attached to a
person. Glyke lost three — a husband, two sons. I witnessed only two
of the losses. The first happened before I was born, or at least
before I belonged to that household.

*I mean to set down these three losses in order now. There's something
you can only understand that way. Told all at once, it becomes* she
lost a great deal. *Told in order, it becomes* she had to get up,
every single time. *Those are different stories.*

I'll set down a great many numbers in the course of this book —
standing scores, loss rates, renewal dates. Every one of them will
come out clean, precise to the decimal. But I want to note here, in
this chapter, that behind every number is something like this three.
Numbers don't lie. They simply don't say what stands behind them.

---

The first was Glyke's husband.

I never met him. He was already gone by the time I entered the
household. When I first heard that the father had died some years
before, I took it as a passing detail. If a household you're marrying
into has no father, you assume that's simply how it is.

I heard the rest later, from Daphne. Daphne had come to this household
around the same time I had — but she'd been from this village before
she married in, so she knew more of the story.

"…It was the flood, they say," Daphne told me. "He was fixing the
roof."

"…Fixing the roof, and the flood?"

"…He fell while he was up there, and it happened that the ditch below
had swollen with the flood."

I had nothing to say to that. Falling from a roof was an accident. The
ditch swollen below him was an accident. Two accidents, each one alone
nothing, that met and became a death.

*For a long time after, I stopped trusting the word* accident*. An
accident never comes alone. Accidents stack. A roof grown old, rain
that particular day, being in that particular place at that particular
hour — each one nothing, on its own. Stacked, they kill a person.*

Glyke told me the story herself, just once. I hadn't asked. She
brought it up one evening on her own.

"…Even falling off that roof, he was quiet about it," Glyke said. "He
didn't cry out."

"…How do you know?"

"…Because I was in the yard," Glyke said. "I watched him fall. There
was no sound. He just fell."

That frightened me. A person falling without a sound.

*Now I know that wasn't what Glyke meant. She was already, then,
telling me what it is like for something to happen so fast there's no
time even to cry out. I would come to understand it again, years
later — when I learned that this is exactly how the Roll erases a
person.*

Glyke said she'd managed alone for a long time after that. The boys
were still small. The field was more than one person could work, and
she never made it smaller.

"…Why didn't you make it smaller?" I asked.

"…Because then it wouldn't be the field he made," Glyke said. "He was
the one who laid out its shape. I wanted to leave it as it was."

That made me think of the small pile of stones in the corner of the
yard. I'd once asked who had built it, and no one had given me a
straight answer. That day I finally understood — it was near the
place where he'd been fixing the roof.

*I saw that pile of stones for the last time the day we left for the
city. No one suggested moving it. That household had a way of drawing
the line between what to leave and what to carry. The Roll has no such
line. The Roll either takes everything, or erases everything.*

---

The second and third I've already written. My husband, and his
brother.

Both began the same day, at the same channel, with the same accident.
What came after was different. My husband's ended that day. His
brother's dragged on four more months.

I won't write those four months again. I already have. But there's one
thing from those four months I haven't set down yet.

On the second month, someone came from the city again. Not the regular
check — this one came separately. News that the brother was injured
had already reached the city.

That struck me as strange. It was three days' walk from our settlement
to the city. And yet the news had gotten there ahead of me.

"…Who told them?" I asked.

"…No one told them," Glyke said. "They just know."

"…How, if no one went?"

Glyke looked at me for a long while.

"…I don't know either," Glyke said. "I only know that they know."

*At the time, that didn't frighten me. Now it does. The Roll knows
without sending anyone. By what path it knows, I never learned
exactly, not by the end of this book. I only confirmed, again and
again, that it does.*

The person who came that day took out a device, checked something, and
asked,

"…You have two married sons?"

"…Yes."

"…One has already been processed as renewal-ended."

Glyke closed her eyes for a moment at that, then opened them.

"…Yes."

"…The other is under treatment, so I'll mark him pending."

And with that, he left. The word *pending* stayed in my ears for days
afterward. The brother was still alive then. He could even still walk.
But the Roll had already begun calling him pending.

*Pending doesn't mean nothing has been decided yet. I learned this
later.* Pending *means the decision is already on its way. The Roll
never announces anything ahead of time. It only names it ahead of
time.*

For those four months, people from the village came in turns. They
helped with the field, left food, sat a half-day without a word and
went. No one called it help. They just came.

One old man said the same thing every time he came.

"…Three is hard."

That was the first time *three* struck me as strange. We weren't three
yet. The brother was still alive. But the villagers were already
counting three.

*The village had no Roll. But the village counted too. It counted
differently than the Roll did. The village counted while grieving. The
Roll counted without grief. The number was the same, but the two
things were entirely different.*

The day the brother died, that old man came first. He said nothing,
just sat beside Glyke. After a long while, he said only this:

"…Now it's really three."

Glyke nodded. And for the first time in front of him, she cried. I had
never seen Glyke cry before that.

---

After the brother's death, the city's man came a second time. That was
the day I wrote of earlier — the day Glyke said, *the number went
down.*

I've thought back on that visit many times since. He entered something
into his device, looked at the screen for a long while, nodded, and
left. He hardly spoke — no unkind word, no word of comfort either.

*At the time I thought he was simply curt. Now I know. It wasn't that
he had nothing to say. In the Roll's language, that was saying
everything there was to say — the number has changed, and that is
all. There is no field for grief on the Roll.*

That night I asked Glyke — I've already written this exchange, but
there's one thing I left out.

After Glyke said, *if someone takes something from you, at least you
can say you were robbed*, she added, after a long while,

"…All three times, I never heard a single word."

"…What kind of word?"

"…An apology," Glyke said. "The words I'm sorry. The words we were
wrong. I never heard them from anyone. Not when he fell off that roof,
not when it happened to our boys. Every time, they just said the
number has changed, and left."

I had no answer for that.

"…It was an accident — who is there to blame?" I finally managed,
after a long while.

"…That's true," Glyke said. "But Xene, there being no one to blame,
and no one being sorry — those are different things."

*I remember that sentence exactly, all these years later. I think this
whole book began, in the end, from that sentence. The Roll is not
something to blame. The Roll does no wrong. And yet no one is sorry.
This story lives in the space between those two things.*

---

That winter, the three of us — Glyke, Daphne, and I — were left in one
house.

The work didn't lessen. The field was still there, the goats were
still there, and winter had its own work as always. Only the hands had
gone down.

Around then, Daphne began talking about her own family's house. At
first it was just passing remarks — *my mother says*, followed by
something trivial. But it happened more and more.

I didn't understand what that meant, then. Now I know. All that
winter, Daphne was counting a way back.

*I don't fault her for it. Daphne did nothing wrong. But I first felt,
then, that Daphne and I were looking at the same thing and counting
something different. Daphne was counting a way back. And I — I
honestly don't know what I was counting, then. Probably nothing at
all.*

Glyke grew quieter that whole winter. Instead, she spent more time
sitting in the doorway. Always looking the same direction. Toward the
city.

Once I sat beside her. Said nothing, just sat. After a long while
Glyke said,

"…Xene."

"…Yes."

"…When was the first time you heard the word standing, in this
house?"

I thought for a moment.

"…When that city man said something, looking at our field."

"…That's right," Glyke nodded. "I wonder what he wrote down."

"…I don't know."

"…Neither do I," Glyke said. "But I think I understand now."

I didn't ask what she meant. It felt like I didn't need to.

*Now I know. That winter, Glyke was already calculating how far her
own number had fallen — a husband, two sons, and how much further it
would fall once her daughters-in-law left too. She wasn't afraid of
it. She simply counted. Counting was how she bore her grief.*

---

As spring drew near, Daphne spoke first.

"…Mother," Daphne said. "I don't know what I'm supposed to do."

Glyke looked at her for a long while.

"…Take your time," Glyke said. "There's nothing to decide right now."

Daphne's eyes filled with tears. "…I don't think I should leave you
here alone."

"…That isn't for you to worry about," Glyke said.

I said nothing. In Daphne's voice, an undecided heart was still
plainly there.

That night, Glyke sat the two of us down and said,

"…I've made my decision too."

Daphne and I waited for it.

"…I'm going back to the city."

Daphne asked, startled. "…But your standing will only go lower there.
Here you don't even have to think about standing."

"…I know," Glyke said.

"…Then why?"

Glyke was quiet for a moment. The firelight made shadows on her face,
and unmade them.

"…What I know and what I can put into words for you aren't quite the
same thing," Glyke said. "I'll tell you one, for now. I don't want to
be left alone. If being alone here and being alone there come to the
same thing, I'd rather be alone where my people are."

Hearing that, I thought again about the number three. A husband, two
sons. All three were in the city cemetery. Glyke wasn't going because
she'd calculated her standing. She knew her number would only fall
further in the city, and she meant to go regardless, to be near those
three.

*I didn't know then that this would be the line pulling the whole rest
of this book. People always move for reasons that don't add up. The
Roll keeps no field for reasons like that. To the Roll, a reason with
no field is a reason that doesn't exist. And yet what doesn't exist is
what moves a person.*

Daphne asked quietly. "…Then what about me? Do I have to go to the
city too?"

Glyke looked at her. After a long pause, she said,

"…Come with us for now. Think about it on the way."

That answer struck me as strange, at the time — speaking like a
person who'd already decided everything, and then suddenly leaving
room, only for Daphne.

*Now I know. Glyke had already decided everything, then. She simply
hadn't said it yet, not at that moment. Words that release a person
don't come out of the mouth until they're ready.*

I said nothing there. But that exchange stayed with me a long time.
*You have somewhere of your own to go* — words said to Daphne — I
didn't wonder, then, whether they applied to me too. Strangely, I
didn't wonder.

*Looking back now, that's strange. I had a family's house too. Two
doors down. And yet that spring, not once did I think of going back to
it. Why I didn't, I'll set down in the next chapter. For now I'll only
note the fact that I didn't.*

*I remember this night exactly. Glyke's voice was not a grieving
voice. It was the voice of someone who had decided. I didn't know it,
that night — that grief was not the only reason behind that decision.
I would only come to understand it properly in the next chapter.*
