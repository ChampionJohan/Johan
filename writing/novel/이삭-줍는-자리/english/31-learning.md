# Chapter 31. Learning

> _Previously_ — Mena said there was nowhere to fix it. Leaving it
> accurately behind was the only resistance left to her. That night,
> Xene decided to learn how to leave things behind, instead of how to
> fix them.

The next morning, I went to Mena with a different question.

I'd tossed and turned over that question half the night, revising it
again and again. First I thought I'd say, "I want to become a
recorder too." Too grand. Then, "Teach me how to do that." Also
somehow awkward. In the end I opened the archive door with nothing
polished at all.

"…Mena," I said. "Can you teach me how to write things down?"

Mena's hand stopped. She looked at me for a long moment. I felt
something being weighed in that look. Not weighing me — weighing, I
think, how heavy this question was for her to receive. The case on
the table still sat closed today. I guessed that the seven sentences
inside it had shaped, in some way, how she was receiving this
question.

"…Why?" Mena asked.

I couldn't answer right away. I hadn't prepared a reason.

"…I don't know," I finally said. "It just feels like what you do is
the most necessary thing in the world. But you won't stay in this
city much longer. If you leave, it feels like no one here will be
writing anything down at all."

Mena closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, something had
been decided in her face.

"…All right," Mena said. "I'll teach you. But it isn't something
you learn in a day. Today I'll teach you the most important thing."

---

Mena handed me a sheet of paper and an old pen.

"…First rule," Mena said. "Write only what you've seen. Not what you
haven't."

"…Isn't that obvious?"

"…It's obvious, and it's the one people break most," Mena said.
"When people see something, without realizing it, they attach a story
to it afterward. That person must be angry. That person must be sad.
But that isn't what you saw. It's what you guessed."

"…So can I never guess at all?"

"…You can guess. Just keep it separate when you write it down," Mena
said. "'That person's face was scowling' — that's what you saw. 'That
person was angry' — that's a guess. I always attach 'it seemed to me'
or 'I guessed' when I write a guess. Otherwise, whoever reads it later
will take the guess for a fact."

I wrote that down on the paper. My hand was slow. Mena waited.

---

"…Second rule," Mena said. "Write down when you don't know. Say you
don't know."

"…Isn't that the same as the first?"

"…Similar, but different," Mena said. "The first is separating what
you saw from what you guessed. The second is knowing when to leave
something blank because you know nothing at all. People can't stand a
blank. They want to fill it with something. I guard against that
temptation more than anything else."

Mena pulled out the sheet I'd seen before — the one with no name, only
a case number.

"…Remember this?" Mena asked. "The one where there was no name, only
a case number."

"…Yes."

"…I could have guessed at his name," Mena said. "If I'd dug through
records from around the same period, a few likely names would have
come up. I didn't. If I wrote a guessed name, someone later might take
it for the real one and copy it further."

*That was the first time I understood how heavy those two syllables,
"unknown," could be. Until then I had thought not knowing something
was a thing to be ashamed of. Mena taught me it was a thing to
protect.*

---

"…Third rule," Mena said. "Write the action. Don't write the heart."

I thought at once of the sentences I'd seen the day before.

"…Like 'no remorse'?" I asked.

Mena nodded. I felt a small pride at having found the question myself.

"…That's right," Mena said. "Whether he repented or didn't — that
isn't visible to me. I've never once gone inside another person. I
only see what they said, what they did. And that's all I write."

"…But people keep wanting to know the heart."

"…They do," Mena said. "I do too. But if you write down what you want
to know, it stops being a record. It becomes a wish. Writing down what
you'd like to believe."

I felt that distinction was subtle, and yet, once I'd grasped it, very
clear.

---

"…Fourth," Mena said. "Don't write everything down."

I was startled at that. Everything until now had been about writing
exactly, in detail. This was the opposite.

"…Why?"

"…There's what's worth writing and what isn't," Mena said. "I don't
write down what won't be forgotten even if I don't write it. I write
down only what would stop having happened, if it were forgotten."

I remembered what Mena had said before, when I'd asked why she wrote.
Her answer, then.

"…Because if it's forgotten, it never happened," I said, echoing it.

Mena looked at me. A little surprised.

"…You remembered that."

"…I've been thinking about it ever since," I said.

Mena smiled a little.

"…That's the bottom of every rule I have," Mena said. "All the other
rules come out of that one. Writing only what I saw, writing that I
don't know when I don't, writing action instead of heart — all of it
is so nothing gets forgotten. If you're not exact, something worse than
forgetting happens. Being remembered wrong."

---

"…Fifth," Mena said. "Write on paper. Not on a screen."

I found that strange. Until then I'd only known paper as slower and
more inconvenient than a screen.

"…A screen's faster," I said. "The Roll does everything on a screen
too."

"…That's the problem," Mena said. "Anything written on a screen, if
someone decides to, can be changed without anyone knowing. Paper isn't
like that. Once something's written on paper, erasing it leaves a
trace. Scrape marks, overwriting. I like that trace. It's honest."

"…What if the paper's destroyed? Burned, or soaked through?"

"…Then the record is lost," Mena said. "I'm afraid of that too. But I
think it's better for something to disappear entirely than to be
secretly changed. You can know a thing that's gone is gone. Something
changed in secret, you never even know was changed."

I thought of the smooth typeface I'd seen on the screen the day
before. Flawless letters, without a single blemish. That flawlessness
looked different to me now. Being clean didn't mean no hand had ever
touched it. It might mean, instead, that whatever hand had touched it
left no trace behind.

---

Mena pointed to the paper in front of me.

"…Try writing something," Mena said. "Anything from today."

I hesitated a moment, then picked up the pen. I thought of what Pikra
had said that morning.

I wrote:

**"Pikra seemed angry this morning."**

Mena read the sentence and shook her head.

"…This is a guess," Mena said. "What did you see, to make you say
'seemed angry'?"

I thought again.

**"Pikra said only three words this morning. Fewer than usual."**

Mena read that one again. This time she nodded.

"…This is good," Mena said. "No one can read this differently. That
she said only three words is a fact. Whether that was because she was
angry, or tired, or simply had nothing to say — that's for the reader
to decide. I don't do that deciding for them."

I looked at the sentence again. Even though I'd written it, it felt far
more honest than the first one had.

"…Write one more," Mena said. "Something harder, this time. About
someone who matters to you."

I hesitated, then thought of Lytron. I wrote:

**"Lytron is not a bad person."**

Mena read that one and said nothing for a moment.

"…Is this a guess too?" I asked first.

"…No, this is a different problem," Mena said. "This is a judgment.
Whether he's bad or not isn't something you get to decide, Xene.
That's for the reader."

I felt a small unfairness at that.

"…But I really do think that."

"…Thinking it is your own business," Mena said. "But if you write it
into this record as a fact, whoever reads it will take it as a fact.
You can write what he did. Leave out whether it was bad or not."

I wrote again.

**"Lytron saw me empty-handed at the corner, and came to check on me
more often than others did."**

Mena read it and nodded.

"…This is much better," Mena said. "Whoever reads this sentence can
decide for themselves whether he's a bad person or not. You don't
decide it for them."

I felt something strange in that moment. Once the judgment was taken
out, more showed through, not less. *He isn't a bad person* said only
one thing. *Came to check on me more often* said far more about who
Lytron was. I read the sentence over several times. Each time, I saw
something slightly different. First I saw kindness in it. Then I
thought — maybe it was only curiosity. I felt that letting those
several layers of possibility stand, rather than covering them over
with one judgment, was in fact more honest.

*That day I learned, for the first time, that judgment actually hides
a person. Calling someone good or bad locks that person inside a
single word. Writing the action as it was leaves them complicated
still. I understood, that day, for the first time, that leaving that
complication in place might be the most honest way to treat someone.*

---

"…Mena," I asked. "What's the use of writing this way? It could get
changed somewhere else anyway."

Mena looked at me for a moment.

"…Even so, what it was at the start stays there," Mena said. "I told
you yesterday. You have to leave behind what was right at the start,
so someone can compare it later. What you compare against is exactly
this kind of exact sentence. A loosely written sentence has no power
to be compared against."

I looked again at the paper in my hand. A short single line. And yet
there was a different weight to it than there had been a moment
before.

*That was the day I first learned that this whole record would come to
be written in the manner I learned that day. I didn't know, then, that
it would become a book. I only started forming the habit of setting
down each day this way.*

---

"…Mena, why teach me?" I asked. "You could teach anyone else."

Mena looked out the window for a moment.

"…I taught this to one other person, a long time ago," Mena said. "She
asked me something similar then."

"…What is that person doing now?"

"…Writing, I'd guess," Mena said. There was something warm in her
voice. "I haven't seen her in a long time. But I believe she's still
writing the way I taught her."

"…Will I become that too, someday?"

Mena looked at me for a long moment.

"…That's up to you," Mena said. "I can only teach. Continuing to write
is up to the one who learned."

"…Wasn't it hard? Passing this on to someone else?"

Mena thought a moment.

"…It was hard," Mena said. "I've done this work alone a long time.
Handing it to someone means letting go of what I've been holding. I
was afraid of that. Afraid of handing it over wrong. Afraid I'd taught
it the wrong way."

"…Then why did you hand it over?"

"…If I don't, it ends with me," Mena said. "That frightened me more.
If I died, everything I'd seen would disappear along with me. I
couldn't bear that."

For the first time, I fully understood why Mena was teaching this, in
a strange city, to a stranger like me. It wasn't kindness. It was
closer to a struggle to survive. From that day on, I saw Mena a little
differently. Behind that calm face was someone just as desperate as I
was.

*I didn't know, then, who that person from long ago was. Later I would
hear the name. For now I'll only set down the expression on Mena's
face when she spoke of that name. Half longing, half pride.*

---

That evening, for the first time, I wrote out the day with my own
hand. It was short. Only a few lines. But it took far longer to write
than I expected. I revised a single word several times.

Pikra saw and asked.

"…What are you doing?"

"…Practicing what I learned," I said. "Mena taught me."

Pikra glanced at the paper. I didn't hide it from her.

"…What's different about this? Isn't it just a diary?"

"…It's different," I said. "I don't write my feelings here. I write
only what happened."

Pikra looked at the paper a moment and nodded.

"…That sounds harder," Pikra said. "Leaving your feelings out."

While Pikra worked her stalks beside me, I recited aloud, one by one,
what I'd learned that day.

"…Write only what you saw. Write that you don't know when you don't.
Write action, not the heart. Don't write everything. Write on paper."

Pikra listened without stopping her hands.

"…That's five," Pikra said. "Did you memorize them all?"

"…I'm not used to them yet," I said. "But I feel like I shouldn't
forget them."

Pikra set down the stalks for a moment.

"…Say them to me again sometime," Pikra said. "So I don't forget
either."

---

On my way to draw water, I stopped by the corner for a moment. He was
there.

"…What did you learn today?" he asked first. That was unusual.

"…How to write things down," I said. "Mena taught me."

He stopped his hands and looked at me. Something glad passed through
his eyes.

"…Good," he said.

"…Why is it good?"

"…Someone has to keep doing it," he said. "Mena will leave this city
someday too."

I was a little surprised at that.

"…How do you know?"

"…All recorders are like that," he said. "They never stay in one
place long. That's their work."

I felt an unexpected sadness at that. It wasn't that I hadn't already
thought Mena wouldn't stay in this city forever. But hearing it said
outright felt different.

"…Then when Mena leaves, will I have to write alone?"

He nodded. He didn't try to console me. Somehow that was a comfort
itself.

"…You won't be doing it alone," he said. "If what's written stays
behind, that isn't doing it alone."

*I didn't fully understand that, that day. I do now. Even in this
moment, I'm not writing alone. The five rules Mena left me are still
holding my hand.*

---

That evening, I wrote out the day with my own hand for the first
time. It was short. Only a few lines. But it took far longer to write
than I expected. I revised a single word several times.

Pikra saw and asked.

"…What are you doing?"

"…Practicing what I learned," I said. "Mena taught me."

Pikra glanced at the paper. I didn't hide it from her.

"…What's different about this? Isn't it just a diary?"

"…It's different," I said. "I don't write my feelings here. I write
only what happened."

Pikra looked at the paper a moment and nodded.

"…That sounds harder," Pikra said. "Leaving your feelings out."

"…It is. My feelings keep jumping out first," I said. "Crossing that
out and starting over takes the longest."

"…Maybe I should try it too," Pikra said, half joking. But something
sincere seemed folded in under the joke.

"…Try it. I'll teach you. It isn't hard."

Pikra laughed. A real laugh, out loud. I thought it had been a long
time since I'd heard that laugh.

"…You, teaching me?" Pikra said. "The world's really changed."

"…Maybe Mena learned from her own student once too."

Pikra laughed even harder at that.

"…Keep at it, anyway," Pikra said. "I want you to keep doing this."

"…Why?"

Pikra looked at the hearth fire for a moment.

"…No one's ever written down exactly what I did, my whole life," Pikra
said. "Everyone just guessed at me, however they liked. A sad woman. A
pitiable woman. But no one ever wrote down what I actually did. I want
you to be the one who does that."

Something caught in my throat at that. I couldn't answer. I only
nodded.

*I read that paper over many times that night. Short sentences. I had
no idea, then, how important those sentences would become to someone,
much later, much further on. I had only left the day behind, exactly
as it had happened, the way Mena taught me.*

*I tried to write every sentence of this book by the five rules I
learned that day. Whether I succeeded, I can't say. But I can say, at
least, that I tried.*

*And I'll set down one more thing. That night, for the first time, I
gave this bundle of paper a name. Nothing grand. I only wrote, at the
top, "Starting today." I think of that as the true first line of this
book. Every chapter I've written since has grown out of those two
words.*
