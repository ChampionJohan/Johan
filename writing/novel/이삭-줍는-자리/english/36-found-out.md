# Chapter 36. Found Out

> _Previously_ — Lytron reverted the loss rate to 3 percent. No one
> broke the law. That day, for the first time in years, grain fell at
> the corner. Xene nearly filled a sack.

The first part of this chapter, too, is a story I didn't live through.
I heard it from Lytron himself, much later. He spoke more slowly,
telling this one, than any other chapter he ever gave me.

---

The next morning, at the Lytron family table, his father asked the
question he always asked.

"…Yesterday's loss rate."

This same question had already come up twice before, in Chapters 16
and 21. Each time, Lytron had given the same answer. 0.02 percent. No
change.

That morning was different.

"…3 percent," Lytron said. "There was a change."

His father set down his chopsticks. The sound of it, Lytron said, was
unusually loud.

"…What did you say?"

"…I changed the setting yesterday," Lytron said.

His father looked as if he couldn't believe it at first, Lytron said.
The same table described in Chapter 16, the same seat. For three
generations, the same question and the same answer had passed across
that table every morning. And now, for the first time, it was that
table's turn to hear something other than "good."

"…That was within your authority?" his father asked.

"…Yes. It's within a zone management assistant's authority," Lytron
said. "I thought it was view-only. It turned out I could change it
too."

"…So it just sat untouched for years, is that what you're telling
me?"

"…Yes."

His father was silent for a long moment after that. Lytron said that
silence had weighed heavier on him than anger would have.

---

*I've pictured this scene over more than once. I think Lytron could
have lied, in that moment. Called it a system error, or said it was
still being confirmed. He didn't. I believe this is the quietest,
and also the most courageous, thing he does in this whole book.*

His father was silent for a while. Then he asked,

"…Why."

Lytron had already imagined this question many times before. In
Chapter 21, he'd rehearsed it in his mind already. Say "because I felt
sorry for her," and it sounds sentimental. Say "because of Article 9,"
and it becomes a lie. He hadn't found a better answer, even then.

That morning, weeks later, he still hadn't found a better one.

"…I don't know," he said.

---

His father looked at him a long while.

"…You don't know," his father said. "You're the one who did it."

"…I'm the one who did it," Lytron said. "But I can't quite put into
words why I did it."

"…You did something like that with no reason at all?"

"…There's a reason," Lytron said. "But if I tell you the reason, I
don't think you'd understand it."

*Hearing this answer, I understood that it wasn't distrust that had
made Lytron say it that way. He simply couldn't yet translate his own
reason into words. Someone who had just seen, for the first time, that
he'd never lost anything — that knowledge needed more time before it
could become a sentence. It wasn't a sentence you could use at a
morning table, in front of his father.*

---

His father asked no further.

That same morning, he learned later, his father had sent someone
directly to the administrator's office. A request to confirm the
change history on the setting.

The administrator met the request plainly. The change history was
already recorded, exactly, within the Roll. Who, when, what, by how
much. Nothing to hide, nothing to erase.

"…There's no procedural problem," the administrator told his father's
man, so Lytron later heard. "Lytron acted within an authority that
belonged to him. It wasn't an item that required approval."

"…So you're saying there's no problem at all?"

"…Legally, no," the administrator said. "Though the yield will be
affected. That's already been reflected."

---

*I heard this part directly from the administrator himself. I asked
him, at the time, "So nothing at all was done wrong?" The
administrator answered, as calmly as ever.*

*"…There's no violation," the administrator said. "There's a loss.
We keep the two separate."*

*I've thought a long time about that distinction. Violation, and
loss. I confirmed again, that day, that this whole book has been
unfolding in the space between those two words.*

Within days, word went around that Lytron's family score had been
adjusted. Not a large drop. But by that household's standard, it was
the first thing of its kind in several generations.

---

In the field, people's manner toward him began, little by little, to
change.

Nothing anyone could point to outright. A greeting arriving half a
beat late. People not quite stopping their conversation when he passed,
but lowering their voices a little. I only noticed it later. Lytron
said he'd noticed it from the very start.

"…People treat me differently now," he told me. "Not badly. Just —
not as a given thing, the way they used to."

"…Is it hard?"

"…Yes," he admitted. "But strangely, I think I understand now what
that is. I was given respect my whole life as a given thing. I thought
that was simply how it was. Now I know it isn't."

His brother, hearing the news, came to see him once. He wasn't angry.
He only said,

"…I don't know what you were thinking. But know one thing. This isn't
something you can undo. A score falls fast and climbs slowly."

"…I know," Lytron said.

"…You knew, and you did it anyway?"

"…Yes," Lytron said.

His brother seemed about to say something more, then only shook his
head and left. Lytron said, watching him go, he couldn't tell if his
brother was worried for him or simply couldn't understand him at all.

*I don't want to paint his brother badly in this scene either. He was
only looking at his brother through the arithmetic he himself had been
taught. Within that arithmetic, what Lytron had done had no other name
but loss.*

---

That evening, Lytron sat across from his father again.

This time his father didn't raise his voice. He only looked at him a
long while, and asked the same question once more.

"…Why."

Lytron said he still couldn't answer. But this time the silence was
different.

"…The first time, I was quiet because I couldn't find the answer," he
said. "That evening, I was quiet because the answer was too large.
Some things don't shrink down into one sentence."

His father sat with that silence a long while, then rose from the
table.

"…I still don't know what it is you've done," his father said. And
he walked out.

---

*I asked Lytron about this more than once. I wanted to know whether he
resented his father. He always said no.*

*"…My father isn't a bad man," he told me. "He spent his whole life
believing that keeping the number was the right thing to do, and he
taught me the same. I can't say that teaching itself was wrong. That
number kept our family standing for generations. I only saw, for the
first time, what stood behind that number."*

*Hearing that, I confirmed again what this book has been trying to say
from the very start. This book doesn't paint Lytron's father as a
villain. Nor the Roll, nor the administrator. No one here is a bad
person. Only, a household that had spent generations teaching itself
never to lose anything had, for the first time, raised a son who lost
something.*

---

I heard the news a few days later. Lytron told me himself.

"…It was me," he said. "The loss rate. I'm the one who reverted it."

I couldn't say anything for a long while, hearing that.

"…So that's why grain was at the corner, that day?" I finally managed.

"…Yes," he said.

"…Why didn't you tell me?"

He thought a moment.

"…I don't know," he said. "I thought something would change, if I
told you. That you'd come to see it as something you got because of
me."

"…And isn't it?"

"…I can't say it isn't," he said. "But I didn't give you anything. I
only put it back where it belonged. Back where it should have been all
along."

---

I turned that over in my mind for a long time.

---

That evening, I told Pikra too.

Pikra was quiet a long while by the hearth.

"…He did that?" Pikra asked at last.

"…Yes."

"…Cutting his own family's score to do it?"

"…Yes."

Pikra shook her head. Not an angry face. I saw something old in it.

"…I only ever knew one person like that," Pikra said. "My husband. When
he was young, he once paid off another family's debt on their field.
A family with no connection to ours at all."

"…Why did he do it?"

"…I don't know," Pikra said. "I asked. He never answered. Thinking of
it now, I don't think even he knew. Some things aren't done for a
reason you can fully name."

I thought of Lytron again, hearing that. He'd said the same thing to
me. *I don't know.*

"…Is that a bad thing, then?" I asked.

"…No," Pikra said. "It isn't bad. But being near someone like that
means, someday, there's a price you have to pay along with them too."

I didn't fully understand what she meant, then.

---

The next day, at the corner, I told him about it too.

He stopped his hands and listened a long while. He said little,
afterward.

"…Did you know?" I asked.

"…I guessed," he said.

"…Why didn't you tell me sooner?"

He brushed the dirt off his hands.

"…It wasn't mine to tell," he said. "That was his to say for himself."

I thought that answer sounded like something he'd said before, in
Chapter 35. *Good news carries its true weight only when it comes from
the mouth of the one who lived it.* I understood, only then, that he'd
been keeping that same rule with me, and with Lytron, exactly alike.

"…What happens to this place now?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he said. "But once even one person has seen a day
like this, this place doesn't go all the way back to what it was
before."

I held onto those words for a long time.

---

A few days later, I stopped by the archive and told Mena too.

Mena listened to all of it and wrote a few lines on her paper. She
attached no judgment at all.

"…What do you think?" I asked. "Have you seen this kind of thing
often?"

Mena set down her pen and looked at me.

"…Not often," Mena said. "I've traveled through many cities. Someone
who cuts down their own share to give it to another — that's truly
rare. But not entirely unheard of, either."

"…What happened to those people, afterward?"

"…All different," Mena said. "Some came to regret it, later. Some
didn't. I can't tell you ahead of time which it'll be. I only write
down what a person has done, now."

I wasn't satisfied with that answer. But at the same time, I knew it
was exactly the kind of answer Mena would give.

*That evening, I set these two meetings down side by side. In what
Lytron had done, Pikra saw her own husband. Mena saw the names of
similar people from several cities. It had only been Lytron's own act.
And yet that single act called up, inside each of them, an entirely
different memory. I think this is the thing I saw most often, in this
third part. That the same fact settles into a different shape inside
each person who receives it.*

*That night, I wrote this on my paper. "Lytron reverted the loss rate.
His family's score fell. His father asked why, and he could not
answer." I attached no judgment after that. I didn't write that it was
good, or that it was a pity. Just as Mena had taught me.*

*But writing this book now, I want to set one more line beside those
sentences. The Roll broke the law not even once, that day. Not
Lytron's father, not the administrator, no one at all committed a
violation. Every procedure was exact. And within that exactness, a boy
lost something, for the first time in his life, and beside him, a
girl, for the first time, held something whole and entire in her own
hands. I set these two sentences down again now, closing this third
part, and confirm once more that both of them were true, the same day,
in the same city, at the very same time.*

*I began this third part with the scene of Mena teaching me how to
write. Closing it now, I know I couldn't have written this final
chapter without those five rules. I wrote this chapter without hating
Lytron, without hating his father, without hating the administrator.
That isn't because I'm a good person. I only learned how to write
action instead of the heart. And once I had set down only the actions,
exactly, there turned out to be, strangely, no place left for hatred
to stand at all.*

*I named this part Mena's Record. I know now. What I truly learned in
this part wasn't Mena's five rules. Those were only the method. What I
came to know, by that method, was something else entirely. That having
done no wrong, and being good, are not the same thing. And that the
difference between them never shows itself in any courtroom, or on any
screen of the Roll. It shows itself in exactly one place. In who has
lost something, and who has not.*
