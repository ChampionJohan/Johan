# Chapter 33. Lytron and Mena

> _Previously_ — Xene confirmed with numbers that a little more always
> turned up wherever he sat. When she asked him why, he neither got
> angry nor smiled — he only said, "I don't know."

This chapter, too, is a story I didn't live through myself.

I wasn't there for it. This is pieced together from what Lytron told
me himself, much later, and from a few things Mena confirmed. I wrote
this chapter in the same spirit I wrote Chapters 16 and 21. Even
something I didn't witness, if I carry it over without lying, isn't a
story I've invented.

---

By then, Lytron had already said, three separate times, that he meant
to go see Mena.

Once to me, twice to himself. I asked him afterward why he kept putting
it off.

"…What was holding you back?" I asked.

"…I don't know," he said. "It just felt like, if I went, I'd learn
something."

"…Why was that so unwelcome?"

"…It isn't that it was unwelcome," he said. "It's that I always
calculate before I know something. But in front of that woman, I had
no idea what there even was to calculate."

In the end, one afternoon, his patrol having ended early, he found his
steps turning toward the archive. There hadn't been any particular
resolve behind it, he said. His feet had simply gone that way that
day.

---

The archive door was open.

Mena sat at the table, sorting through paper. Lytron hesitated in the
doorway for a moment.

"…Excuse me," he said.

Mena looked up. No surprise showed.

"…Lytron," Mena said. "Xene's told me about you."

Lytron told me, later, that he hadn't given much thought, in that
moment, to already being a known quantity to someone. He was used to
his own name arriving somewhere before he did.

"…I hear my name is in here somewhere," he said. "May I see it?"

"…Yes," Mena said. That was all.

*When I heard about this part from Lytron, I could tell he'd been
briefly thrown off by that short answer. He'd expected something to
follow that single word — who are you, why do you want to see it,
which household are you from. Nothing followed at all.*

---

Mena took a thin sheet of paper from a drawer and handed it to him.

Lytron took the page and read it for a long while. His own name, his
family's score, three generations of harvest-management history. And
beneath all of it, a single short line.

**"Zone management assistant. No change in score history."**

That was all.

"…Is this all?" he asked.

"…Yes," Mena said.

"…I expected something more."

Mena looked at him for a moment.

"…What did you expect?" Mena asked.

He couldn't answer that right away, he told me later.

"…I don't know. I thought it would say something about me, good or
bad."

"…This is saying something about you," Mena said. "You've had no
change. I've written that down, exactly as it is."

Lytron set the page down on the table, and his eyes drifted to the
papers stacked beside it. Names showed on the pages. One of them caught
his eye.

"…Isn't this Xene's name?" he asked.

Mena nodded.

"…Can I see it?"

"…That one, no," Mena said. It was the first real refusal she'd given
him. "That belongs to her to show, if she chooses to show it. I won't
show it for her."

Lytron nodded at that. He told me, later, "I felt a little ashamed, in
that moment. I'd brought up your name first, and now that it was
actually your own to keep, I'd asked to see it without thinking twice.
I'd never questioned looking at my own — and there I was, asking to
see someone else's without a second thought."

*Hearing this part, I was struck that he'd even noticed that shame in
himself. The Lytron of Chapter 16 probably wouldn't have felt anything
about why that question shouldn't be asked. He'd already been
changing, in those weeks, in small ways he himself hadn't noticed
yet.*

---

A leather case sat on one side of the table, its lid closed.

"…What's that?" Lytron asked.

Mena glanced toward it for a moment.

"…Things I wrote a long time ago," Mena said.

"…May I see?"

"…That one, no either," Mena said.

This time Lytron didn't ask why. He explained it to me, later, this
way: "Looking at her face, I could tell — it wasn't that it was
forbidden to me. It was a door not yet open even to her. I didn't know
I could sense something like that. That day was the first time I
could."

---

Lytron studied that sentence for a long time.

"…Is no change a good thing?" he asked.

"…I don't know," Mena said. "I'm not someone who writes good or bad."

"…Then why do you do this work?"

Mena's hand paused.

"…To leave behind, accurately, what someone did and didn't go
through," Mena said. "No change, too, is a very exact record, for
some people."

*I heard this part from Lytron many times over, afterward. He said he
hadn't fully understood it, that day. Now I think it was a sentence
that quietly foretold the next chapter. But the Lytron standing there
that day only felt a small sting — that nothing at all had been
written beside his own name.*

---

He told me, later, that he had tried to do something, right there.

That was simply who he was. Someone who read what another person
wanted from him first, and gave it. He assumed Mena would want to know
more about his family. So he offered it up himself.

"…My family has managed this field for three generations," he said.
"I can tell you more, if it helps. If it's useful for your record—"

"…That's all right," Mena said. "I'll ask if I need to."

Lytron's words trailed off there. He told me he felt as if he'd walked
into something, from that small refusal.

"…I felt something strange, right then," he said, later. "I've always
given people what they seemed to want from me. But she didn't take
what I offered. Not angrily. Just — she didn't need it."

---

He tried again.

"…If there's anything I can help with, let me know," he said. "My
family has some standing in this city. If you need more material—"

"…I don't," Mena said. Still short. "I don't need anything right now."

Lytron said that was the moment he first truly felt himself standing
somewhere he didn't know what to do.

*Writing this, I found myself thinking back to Chapter 16 again. There
I'd described Lytron's world as one where he never had to wonder. Now
I have to add another sentence to that world. Lytron hadn't only lived
in a world where he never had to wonder — he'd lived, too, in a world
where he'd never once gone unasked-for. People always wanted something
from him. Respect, goodwill, a nod to his family's name. He was
skilled at giving those things. Mena wanted nothing.*

---

"…Does this make you uncomfortable?" Mena asked.

Lytron said he was startled by the question. He hadn't known he was
showing discomfort.

"…Does it show?" he asked.

"…A little," Mena said. "People make that face, often, when they try
to give me something and I don't take it."

"…Why do you think they make that face?"

Mena thought a moment.

"…I'm not certain. I can guess," Mena said. "I guess people feel safe
when they give something. If it isn't taken, they lose the sense of
what they are."

Lytron had no answer to that, he told me.

---

He tried one last time, he said. He'd turned to leave, then turned back
and said,

"…Do you need any supplies? Paper, or food. There's some to spare at
our house."

Mena looked at him for a moment. Something different crossed her eyes
for the first time, Lytron said. Not irritation. More the look of
someone hearing a familiar question yet again.

"…That's kind of you," Mena said. "But I carry what I need. I've
traveled this way a long time."

"…Still, if there's ever—"

"…Lytron." Mena cut him off. The first time she'd used his name. "You
keep trying to give me things. Have you ever thought about why?"

Lytron said the question left him speechless. He walked out without
being able to answer it at all.

---

*I want to pause here and set this down. When I first heard this
exchange from Lytron, I thought Mena was testing him on purpose. I
don't think that anymore. Mena tests no one. She simply looked, as she
always did, at the person in front of her, wanting nothing. Lytron's
discomfort didn't come from anything Mena did. It came from her doing
nothing at all.*

Lytron told me he'd walked out of the archive that day with something
unresolved still hanging on him. Normally that wasn't like him at all.
He always left an errand knowing exactly how it had ended. That day
wasn't like that.

"…I didn't even know what the problem was," he said, later. "I'd just
gone to see what was written beside my name. I came out carrying
something completely different."

---

He told me he turned that question over the whole walk home. *Have you
ever thought about why.*

He stopped for a moment in front of his own gate. He could hear the
sound of water running inside, as always — water that simply ran, for
show, to dress up the yard.

That evening at dinner, his father asked the question he always asked.

"…Anything unusual today."

"…No," he said.

"…Good."

Lytron said that, hearing that "good," he thought for the first time
about what it took for granted. It always came when he'd kept
something, when he hadn't lost anything. His father hadn't wanted
nothing from him. His father had always wanted exactly one thing. That
the number stay the same. That one thing had felt so obvious, for so
long, that Lytron had never even recognized it as a demand.

*Hearing him describe this dinner, I understood, at last, why what he'd
felt at the archive that day had struck him as so foreign. In his own
house, everyone — even the silences — wanted something of him. In
Mena's room, no one wanted anything at all. He had never once, in his
whole life, had the chance to feel that difference.*

---

That evening, I ran into him by chance at the corner. He wasn't on
patrol. He was simply standing a long while at the edge of the field.

"…Is something wrong?" I asked.

"…No," he said. But his voice wasn't its usual self.

"…Did you see Mena?" I asked.

He looked at me, a little startled.

"…How did you know?"

"…Your face looks strange."

He said nothing for a while, looking out at the field. The evening
light lay low, and the stubble cast long shadows.

"…Is Mena a little frightening?" I asked. I'd meant it as a joke, but
he took the question seriously.

"…Not frightening," he said. "But none of the things I usually do work
in front of her. I find that unfamiliar."

He started to smile, and the smile wouldn't quite come.

"…She didn't ask me for a single thing," he said. I didn't fully
understand what he meant, then.

"…Why is that strange?"

Lytron was quiet a long while before answering.

"…I don't know," he said. "But it's the first time in my life. That no
one wanted anything from me."

---

*I didn't think much of that, hearing it that day. I didn't understand
why Lytron was carrying those words with such weight. I had lived my
whole life never able to demand anything from anyone. It hadn't seemed
strange to me at all. But for him, going unasked-for was something he
was living for the first time in his life.*

*I set these two sentences side by side now and look at them a long
while. I went out to the corner every day because I could demand
nothing. He wavered, for the first time, because nothing was demanded
of him. The same city, the same evening, two people standing, each for
their own reason, in front of the same word. Demand.*

*I didn't know, then, what the discomfort he felt that day would come
to open up inside him, a few days later.*

---

That night, writing this down, I found myself, for the first time,
careful about how I described Lytron.

I wanted to write, "Lytron was shaken." That would have been a guess.
I wrote instead only that his face had looked strange, that he'd
spoken differently than usual, that he'd used the phrase "the first
time in my life." Whether he'd really been shaken, he himself probably
didn't know that night.

*Looking back now, I know. He was shaken. Only the shaking made no
sound. Outwardly, nothing at all happened that day. He ate dinner as
usual, heard "good" said to him as usual. I'll write this same sentence
several more times in this book. Outwardly, nothing happened. But this
time, inside that nothing happening, something was, for the first
time, quietly changing places.*
