# Chapter 39. The Price

> _Previously_ — Medon refused. Not because he was a bad man, but for one
> reason: "My score would be cut." The Roll found no fault in him. He had
> broken no law. That night, Lytron sat quietly with his own thoughts.

For a long time, I thought the word *price* belonged only to things.

The price of a sack of wheat. The price of a goat. A number set against
something you could hold in your hand. That people could have a price
felt, to me, like counting a person as an object, and I never much liked
the word for that reason.

But that summer, I learned the word again. Price wasn't only a thing's to
carry. Some prices a person pays with their own two hands. And the moment
of paying it, I found, could be astonishingly small.

Outside, paying a price was always visible. You gave wheat and received
salt; you lent a hand and borrowed one back. What passed between people
always passed from hand to hand. In the city it was different. A price
moved inside a screen. The only thing your hand ever touched was the
screen itself. And yet what moved inside that screen was never lighter
than what moved from hand to hand.

*This was the hardest chapter to write. The largest thing in this book
happens here, and yet what actually happened was so small that a few
lines hold all of it. I decided to leave it that small. Not to swell it.*

Mena told me once that the thing a recorder must guard against most
isn't writing a small thing large. It's writing a large thing small. A
large thing tends, by its nature, to look small, and it is easy to be
fooled by that smallness and miss its true size.

I kept remembering that, writing this chapter. What is recorded here
really was very small. And knowing that smallness was no lie, I still had
to write it so the reader would never forget how large it truly was.

---

I learned only later that it took Lytron three days to reach his
decision.

He'd begun calculating, he told me, the night he left Medon's office. His
own family standing, the registration threshold, the distance between
them. He couldn't say exactly how many times he ran the numbers again.

"…At first I thought half would be enough," he told me later. "It won't
clear the threshold with half. It has to be the whole thing."

"…And if it's the whole thing?"

"…My family standing drops one full tier," he said, "from where it is
now." His voice was even, but I only learned later how much effort had
gone into that evenness.

---

Lytron came to our house three days after he'd met Medon.

He stood at the door for a long time without speaking. I knew, just from
his face, that he'd come with something already decided.

"…Come in," Pikra said.

He stayed where he was and spoke.

"…I'll do it."

I didn't understand at first what he meant.

"…The standing transfer," he said. "I'll give mine to Miss Xene."

---

Pikra reacted first.

"…Your family standing's already fallen once," Pikra said. "If you cut
it further—"

"…I know," he said. "I'll do it anyway."

"…How much does it come to?"

"…I've calculated it," he said. "It's enough. Enough to bring Miss Xene
above the registration threshold."

I knew, that moment, I should say something, and no words came. Not
because my throat had closed, but because I truly did not know what to
say. *Thank you* seemed far too small a thing to say. *No, don't* seemed
far too cold. I found nothing that stood between them.

"…Why," was all I managed.

Lytron looked at me. There was nothing of resolve in his face, nothing of
heroism. He only looked tired.

"…I'll tell you the reason later," he said. "Right now I just want to do
it."

*I would hear that reason later. Many times, in many ways. But even now,
I think what he said that day — I just want to do it, right now — was the
truest answer of all. A great decision sometimes arrives before its
reasons do.*

Pikra asked again.

"…Have you told your parents?"

"…No," Lytron said.

"…You should tell them first."

"…If I tell them, they'll stop me," he said. "So I haven't."

Something went cold in me at that. He had already decided to cross some
bridge with his family. In the direction there was no going back from.

"…They'll have things to say, later," Pikra said.

"…I know," he said. "But if I don't do it now, I don't think I ever
will."

*I've never forgotten those words, even all these years later. He knew
exactly what kind of person he was. That if you don't act on a resolve
the moment you feel it, that resolve doesn't come again. Everyone is
given a handful of such windows, in their life. Most people don't see
them standing open until they've already passed.*

---

We went to the administrator's office the next day.

The administrator received us. Even as always. Lytron stated his
purpose, and the administrator showed no surprise.

"…I'll confirm this," the administrator said. "You wish to transfer your
own standing, of your own will, to Miss Xene."

"…Yes."

The administrator tapped the screen a few times. I watched his fingers
move — neither slow nor fast.

"…The application is prepared," the administrator said. "Read it, and
sign if you're satisfied."

That was all.

---

I looked at the application. A single sheet of paper. Few words on it.
The one transferring. The one receiving. The amount transferred. A
signature line below.

"…Is that all?" I asked. The words came out of me before I meant them
to.

The administrator looked at me.

"…That's all there is, procedurally," the administrator said. "There's
no reason for it to be complicated. The Roll already knows each person's
value precisely. A transfer is only a reallocation of that value."

"…No one checks it? There's no approval process—"

"…Why would approval be needed?" the administrator asked, in return.
"This is a person using what is theirs to use."

*Those words stayed with me. Using what is one's own to use. The Roll had
never had any logic by which to prevent it. This wasn't breaking a rule.
It was a rule that had permitted this from the beginning. It was only
that no one had ever used it.*

"…I'm curious about something," I asked the administrator. "How long has
this procedure existed?"

The administrator checked the screen for a moment.

"…It's existed since the Roll was first established," the administrator
said. "It's an older provision than this city's entire Roll system."

"…Then why has no one—"

"…I don't know the reason myself," the administrator said. For the first
time, something like hesitation crept into his voice. "May I offer my own
opinion?"

"…Please."

"…A person who gives up what is theirs doesn't boast of it," the
administrator said. "And a person who doesn't give up what is theirs has
no reason to even know this procedure exists. So the procedure was never
going to be widely known. No one has used it, so there's no story to
tell about it, and with no story, the next person never learns of it
either."

"…May I ask one more thing?" I said.

"…Yes."

"…If the first person to use this procedure used it with bad intent —
what would happen? Forcing it on someone, for instance."

The administrator thought for a moment.

"…A transfer is only completed by the giving party's signature," the
administrator said. "The receiving party's consent isn't required. In
that sense, yes, it could be abused. Someone forcing their low standing
onto another person, for instance."

"…Has that happened?"

"…No," the administrator said. "In a world where no one is willing to
give up what's theirs, there's not much reason to force anything onto
anyone else, either. Taking something from someone starts, in the end,
from wanting to gain something. This is a losing procedure, not a
gaining one."

That explanation sounded, strangely, sad to me.

*Good things come from the hands of people who don't boast, and so they
never pass on to the next person. Bad things spread easily precisely
because no one bothers to hide them. I saw that imbalance for the first
time that day. And I would learn, much later, that it isn't only found
in this city.*

---

Lytron stood before the signature line.

I remember that moment exactly, even now. No special instrument, no
ceremony. He simply touched his finger to the signature line on the
screen — the same way he always did to collect a ration, or leave a
record at a gate.

"…Wait," I said.

He stopped his hand and looked at me.

"…Are you sure about this?" I asked. "You said it can't be undone."

"…It can't be undone," the administrator answered, in his place. "A
transferred value is not returned."

Lytron looked at me and smiled. It had been a long time since I'd seen
that smile.

"…I'm sure," he said.

And he touched his finger to the screen again.

I found myself holding my breath, without meaning to. It was a very
short moment. The screen flickered once, then returned to itself.

That was all.

I had expected something more. A confirmation screen, perhaps, or a
second prompt asking again. There was none. Only a small word, printed
above the application: *Processed.*

---

The numbers on the screen changed.

His number went down. Mine went up. That was all. No sound. No light
flickered. The screen only showed two new numbers.

I'd expected something grand to happen, in that moment. Nothing did. The
air in the room was the same. The sounds outside the window were the
same. The administrator was already paging to the next screen.

*That quiet was the strangest part of all. That the moment a person set
down a number they'd carried their whole life could pass as though it
were nothing at all. I understand it now. That's the true face of a
price. A price always looks small, in the moment it's paid. You only see
how large it truly was later, looking at what's left in its place.*

The administrator turned the screen toward us. Two numbers side by side.
One that had gone down, one that had gone up.

I looked for a long time at the number now beside my name. It was the
first number I'd had beside my name in sixteen years of living. Not
zero.

"…Is this mine?" I asked. I knew how foolish the question sounded, and
asked it anyway.

"…It's yours, Miss Xene," the administrator said.

Lytron wasn't looking at the screen. He was looking out the window.

*I wanted to ask why he wasn't looking. I didn't ask. Some moments, I
have learned from this family, are not meant to be asked about.*

---

There was someone else in the office besides us. Someone in the corner,
there on other business, waiting their turn. That person never once
looked our way. Theirs was the face of someone only waiting for their own
name to be called.

*That, strangely, stayed with me. That a moment when one person's life
was being changed was, to the person sitting right beside it, nothing
more than the background of their own waiting. That's how the world is
made. Some moments are enormous only to the person inside them, and
nothing at all to anyone standing a single step away.*

The administrator gathered his papers as he spoke.

"…Your registration will be completed today," the administrator said,
turning to me. "Congratulations."

I have never heard the word *congratulations* land so heavily.

The administrator turned next to Lytron.

"…Your side requires processing as well," the administrator said. "The
family standing reassessment. It will be reflected at the next scheduled
renewal."

"…Understood," Lytron said.

"…Would you like to reconsider—"

"…No," he said. Fast. Firm. The administrator didn't ask again.

*I learned later that the brief exchange was the administrator's own
kind of consideration. It was a question he was required, by procedure,
to ask once, and he closed it quickly, as a formality, to spare him. It
was his own way of being kind.*

I looked at Lytron. He was already looking elsewhere. Out the window.

"…Are you all right?" I asked again.

"…Yes," he said. Short. As though he didn't want to say more.

*I didn't press him further then. Later, I asked him many times what he'd
been thinking in that moment. He gave a different answer every time. I
think that means he still hasn't found the exact answer himself.*

---

Lytron's father heard the news that evening.

I never saw the scene myself. Lytron never described it in detail,
either. All he said, a few days later, was this.

"…My father didn't say anything at all."

"…He wasn't angry?"

"…I would rather he'd been angry," he said. "Saying nothing at all is
more frightening."

I understood what he meant. I already knew, myself, that some silences
weigh heavier than any anger.

"…Do you regret it?" I asked.

Lytron thought for a long while.

"…No," he said. "But I am afraid. I only found out this time that the
two things can exist together."

*No regret, and afraid. I didn't fully understand what that meant then. I
understand it now. Being the right choice doesn't mean it isn't
frightening. Sometimes it's precisely the rightest choice that's the most
frightening one — because there's no way back to where you were.*

I couldn't sleep that night and went out and sat outside. I looked up at
the sky. The same sky I used to watch from the outer settlement. The
stars were in their same places.

*That was the first time I truly felt how the sky over the city and the
sky over the outside were the same sky. The Roll had completely remade
the way a person was counted, and the sky hadn't changed at all. There
are some things that don't change, no matter how great a price passes
beneath them.*

Pikra came out and sat beside me.

"…Awake?"

"…Yes."

"…Me too," Pikra said.

We sat a long while without speaking. Then Pikra said,

"…What do you think your husband would have said."

I had no answer for that. I truly didn't know what he would have said. But
I thought I knew one thing.

"…I think he would have said not to be sorry for what's spilled," I
said. "That what's spilled is left behind."

Pikra nodded. She said nothing more.

---

Outside, the sun was strong.

The three of us stood for a moment without speaking. What had just
happened inside that office already felt like something far away, out
here.

Pikra took hold of Lytron's arm for a moment. She said nothing. She just
held it, and let it go.

"…I don't know if 'thank you' is the right thing to say," Pikra said,
finally. "I don't even know if that's the right word for this."

"…You can just say that," Lytron said.

"…No," Pikra said. "This isn't a thing that thanks can settle. I know
that. That's why I don't know what to say."

Lytron smiled a little.

"…Then leave it," he said. "It's all right if it doesn't come out
right."

*I've never forgotten that exchange. Pikra was never, in her life, a
woman who was careful with words. That day, she couldn't find the right
ones. Some things simply can't be priced in words. Gratitude, apology —
both grow small in front of a thing like this.*

"…Can I ask you something?" I said to Lytron.

"…Yes."

"…That signature, just now — how many seconds did it take?"

Lytron looked at me. He didn't seem to think the question strange.

"…A few seconds," he said. "I couldn't say exactly. It was short."

"…That something that short could be something this large — I still
find that strange."

Lytron smiled a little. A different smile than before.

"…So do I," he said.

*I meant to end this chapter here. But I'll add one more thing.*

I told "him" the story that night, too. He was alone at the corner of
the field. Quietly working, as always.

"…Lytron transferred his standing to me," I said.

He stopped his hands. He looked at me for a long time. Something crossed
his face — not surprise, I felt. More the face of someone who had waited
a long time for something.

"…I see," he said.

"…Did you know? That this would happen?"

"…I didn't know," he said. "But I hoped for it."

The words struck me strangely. He had always seemed a man who hoped for
nothing at all.

"…What were you hoping for?"

He began moving his hands again.

"…For there to be someone who knows how to give up what's theirs," he
said. "In this city, too."

*I let that answer pass by that night. Reading it again now, there was a
weight in those words that reached beyond this one city. I wouldn't
understand that until much later.*

Even years after, I keep returning to this scene. The Roll broke no law
that day. Neither did the administrator, nor Medon, nor Lytron. Each of
them moved only as far as they were permitted. Medon refused as far as
he was permitted to refuse. Lytron gave as far as he was permitted to
give. The Roll received both, equally, in the same silence.

*I think that is this city's truest face. The Roll compels no one. It
only leaves the door open, and leaves it entirely to the person whether
to walk through it. And in front of that door, some people walk in and
some don't. The Roll does not weigh that difference. A person does.*

I mean to end this chapter here, truly. One signature. A few seconds.
That was all of it. And that it was all of it is the heaviest thing I
learned from this whole book.
