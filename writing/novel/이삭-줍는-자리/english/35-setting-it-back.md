# Chapter 35. Setting It Back

> _Previously_ — Lytron saw that his never having done wrong and his
> never having lost anything were the front and back of the same
> fact. He had carried that weight alone for days.

Lytron told me about that morning much later, and very briefly.

"…Nothing special about that day," he said. "I just got up early, as
usual, and went to the office, as usual. And then I opened that screen
again."

"…And after that?"

"…I pressed it," he said.

That was the whole of his account. I think the brevity itself is more
exact than anything longer would have been. Some moments come closer
to a lie the longer they're told.

---

*I felt the temptation, more than once, writing this chapter, to swell
that moment out — his hand trembling, a long hesitation, his eyes
closing and opening again. I chose not to write it that way. Lytron
never told me anything like that. He only said he pressed it. I choose
to believe that, exactly as he said it.*

I know the days hidden behind that short answer. He'd carried that
weight alone through the whole of Chapter 34. I asked him, more than
once, what had been different that particular morning. Each time, he
answered a little differently.

Once he said, "I just didn't want to put it off any longer."

Another time: "What Mena had said kept staying with me. Have you ever
thought about why. That morning, for the first time, I understood that
knowing the answer and doing nothing about it would from then on just
be pretending not to know."

Another day still: "My father said 'good' again that day too. I heard
it, but this time I understood what it meant while I heard it. That was
the hardest part."

*I want to note that these three answers came on three separate days.
I didn't want to stitch them into one tidy reason. A person's reasons
for deciding something usually aren't just one. I chose to leave all
of them standing.*

The confirmation window came up. The same wording as the one in
Chapter 21. *Change the loss-rate setting? Projected yield will be
adjusted accordingly.*

Beneath it, a number. From 0.02 percent to 3 percent. The same screen,
the same figure he'd opened weeks before, one night. What had changed
wasn't the number. It was the person standing in front of it.

He said he checked that number once more. It wasn't a large figure. A
single decimal point had moved. But the drop in projected yield that
followed that small movement wasn't small at all.

He said he didn't look at that window for long.

He pressed *Confirm.*

---

*I won't set that number down here either, for the same reason I
didn't set it down in Chapter 21. Writing the number turns this into
a matter of arithmetic. Much, little, worth bearing. I don't want this
chapter read that way. I'll only say this. The number was large. Large,
measured against everything Lytron's family had spent generations
keeping.*

The confirmation message appeared. *Setting changed.*

That was all. No alarm sounded. No lock engaged. No request for a
superior's approval. The screen flickered once and moved on, without
concern, to the next item. The Roll accepted the change at once.

"…It was strange," Lytron told me, later. "I thought there'd be
something more. Some procedure to stop it. There wasn't. I pressed it,
and it just changed."

He said he left the office that day, and didn't go home right away.
Normally, he'd have spent the morning on patrol and then written his
report. That day he simply walked toward the field instead.

He watched the corner from a distance. He said he didn't go closer,
afraid something might change if he did.

"…What did you see, from there?" I asked.

"…You," he said. "Crouched, looking at something. I couldn't see your
face from that far. But your hands kept moving."

"…Did you see him too? The one who glean's beside me."

"…Yes," he said. "He didn't see me. I was too far off. But strangely,
what he was doing kept catching my eye. Just his hands moving, next to
you. I don't know why that stayed with me so long."

I felt something strange, hearing that. I hadn't known, that day, that
Lytron had even been there, or that he'd watched the two of us for so
long. Three of us in the same field, that same afternoon, and only one
of us had been watching the other two the whole time.

"…What were you thinking, watching me?"

He was quiet a long while before he answered.

"…Nothing came to me," he said. "I just watched. That's all there
was."

*Hearing this scene, much later, I came to know something I'd never
known at all, that day. I'd thought I was crouched alone at the corner,
counting grains of fallen wheat. I wasn't. Someone was watching me
from a little way off. That person said nothing, and never came
closer. I didn't know, then, how heavy that silence would turn out to
be.*

*I thought, too, of something the administrator had said in passing,
in Chapter 22. Transfer is possible, in principle, but rarely used in
practice. I'd let that pass, at the time. Now I know. This city's doors
had never truly been locked. They'd only been closed because no one
had ever opened them. That morning, Lytron pushed on a door no one had
ever pushed. And it simply opened.*

---

That day, I went out to the corner as usual.

The sky was clear. No sign of rain. I set about sifting the dirt with
no great expectation, same as any other day. Since Chapter 24, I'd
known I only ever gained anything on rainy days. That day, it hadn't
rained at all.

And yet something was there at that spot.

At first I doubted my eyes. It wasn't trampled, broken stalks. It was
whole grain. Scattered here and there, not much, but unmistakably
there. I crouched and looked at it for a long while.

"…And no rain fell," I said, half to myself.

---

He was there too, that day. I watched his face as he looked down at the
dirt in front of him. The same face as always. But that day, that
sameness felt strange to me in a way I couldn't place.

"…Doesn't today feel a little different?" I asked.

He glanced for a moment at the few stalks in his hand.

"…It does," he said.

"…Why do you think that is?"

He didn't answer right away. After a moment he only said,

"…I don't know. But it's a good thing, isn't it."

*I believed that, plainly, that day. I know better now. He must have
known. I've thought a long while about why he didn't just tell me the
truth that day. What I guess now is this — he wanted, still, to leave
that fact as Lytron's to give. Some news carries its true weight only
when it comes from the mouth of the one who lived it.*

He paused a moment in gathering his own share. I saw his hand stop.

"…What are you doing?" I asked.

"…Nothing," he said. And then his hand started moving again.

I let that brief pause go, that day, without much thought. But as
evening came on, he said, out of nowhere,

"…Days like today don't come often." he said. "Take a good look while
it lasts."

"…Why?"

"…There'll be times, later, you'll want to see it again," he said.
"And it might not be there to see."

That struck me, strangely, as something to hold onto. But for some
reason, I didn't ask any further, right then.

*I didn't come back to that remark until much later. I can guess, now,
something of what he already knew that day. I don't know exactly what
he knew. But I believe, now, that he already knew, that day, that this
sight wouldn't last long.*

---

Damia passed by the corner that afternoon too, and stopped.

"…Oh, there's something here today," Damia said. Not a surprised
voice. Just someone noticing, in passing.

"…Yes," I said.

"…Well, how about that. No rain either."

"…I know."

Damia wasn't curious beyond that. She stood a moment, brushed the dirt
with her foot a few times, and went on her way, saying only, "…Nice
day for it," as she left.

*I think that was the most honest response I saw that whole day. Damia
hadn't been born and raised in this field. To her, the corner was just
a stretch of ground she passed on her way to somewhere else. A good day
was just a good day. I didn't envy that lightness. It only felt
unfamiliar. To me, that something existed in that spot wasn't a thing
you could pass through in a day.*

---

I filled a sack nearly full that day.

Back home, when I showed it to Pikra, she went quiet for a long while.

"…Did it rain?" Pikra asked.

"…No."

Pikra looked into the sack again.

"…Then why is it like this?"

"…I don't know," I said. "I was just there, at that spot, today."

Pikra didn't look especially glad to hear that. I found that strange.

"…Aren't you happy?" I asked.

"…I am," Pikra said. But there was something careful in her voice.
"But I don't much trust something turning good for no reason. When
something turns good, there's always a reason behind it. Not knowing
that reason unsettles me."

"…Then how do you receive it?" I asked. "You say a good thing unsettles
you."

Pikra ran her fingertips once over the grain in the sack.

"…I receive it," Pikra said. "Receiving it without knowing why, and
refusing it because you don't know why, are different things. I've
never lived a life where I knew all the reasons. Even so, I ate what
there was to eat."

Somehow, that felt like comfort to me.

"…Will it be like this tomorrow too?" I asked.

"…I don't know," Pikra said. "But worry about tomorrow tomorrow. Today,
eat what's today's."

We ate well that evening, for the first time in a long while. Strangely,
that fullness made me uneasy. It felt, somehow, borrowed.

*I let that thought pass, that night. Later I would learn how exact
that feeling had been. Pikra had lived more than a generation among
this city's numbers. She knew, in her body, long before I did, that
there's always a price behind something good.*

---

Pikra was quiet a long while that night, by the hearth, working the
grain. I wasn't unfamiliar with that silence. I'd already learned, in
that house, that some people go quiet in front of good things too.

I thought again, that night, of my husband's words.

*What you spill isn't wasted. It's left behind.* I'd first heard those
words two autumns before, in a field. At the time, I'd thought he only
meant, go easy on your wrists.

That night, I think, I understood the meaning of those words fully,
for the first time. Spilling and leaving something behind come from the
same gesture. Only whose hand makes that gesture differs. My husband
had made that gesture with his own hand. That day, I still didn't know
that another hand, for the first time, had made the same gesture in
this field.

*I stop long over this sentence as I write it. I didn't know this, that
night. I only had a handful of grain in front of me, and pulled up an
old, dead man's words again. That those words described, exactly,
something that had actually happened that morning in some office in
this city — I wouldn't know that for a few days yet.*

---

I wrote that day down on paper, the way Mena had taught me.

**"It didn't rain, and there was grain at the corner. I nearly filled a
sack."**

I wrote no reason behind that sentence. I didn't know one. That day, I
was content with that alone.

*Now I can add another sentence beside that one. That day, in some
office in this city, a person pressed a confirm button. No one broke
the law. The procedure was exact. The Roll accepted the change exactly
as it was given. And that person's family score, still unknown to
anyone, was quietly changing direction.*

*I couldn't set these two sentences side by side, that night. I set
them side by side now, in this book. One I saw with my own eyes. One I
heard of later. But they were a single event, happening the same day,
in the same city.*

*I want to state one thing plainly as I close this chapter. That day,
no one broke the law. Lytron only changed a setting within his own
authority. The Roll accepted the change precisely, without resistance.
The administrator's office, Article 9, none of it was violated. I will
write this sentence many times more in this book. No one broke the law.
And that day, for the first time in years, grain fell at the corner of
this city's field.*

*I touched that sack once more that night before sleep. Every kernel
was rough and solid to the hand. I didn't know where it had come from.
I only knew, for certain, that it was truly there in my hands. That
night, for the first time in a long while, I waited for the morning
without worry.*
