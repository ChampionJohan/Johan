# Chapter 26. The One Who Writes It Down

> _Previously_ — I met Mena. Someone who wrote things down without
> judging anyone. The administrator hadn't lied, and there was still
> nothing at the corner. Mena said she would look into the Roll's
> reputation field the next day.

The next day, instead of going to the corner, I went to find Mena
first.

I wasn't entirely sure why. It was just that the carefulness in her
voice the night before had stayed with me. I didn't realize until I
reached the well that I hadn't even brought my water jar.

Mena wasn't there. Instead, she was standing in front of a low
building near the gate, a wooden sign reading "The Archive" over its
door. The door was smaller than the city's other buildings, and looked
old.

"…What is this place?" I asked.

"…This city's archive," Mena said. "A place where a person can look
into what the Roll has piled up. Every city has one."

"…Can we go in?"

"…I can," Mena said. "I'm a recorder. You come sit inside with me.
Quietly."

An older man came out from inside — someone who seemed to keep the
place. He gave Mena a small nod.

"…Back again," he said. "Staying long this time?"

"…A few days, maybe," Mena said.

"…Whatever you need, just ask. We have nothing to hide in this city,"
the man said.

I felt like I'd heard those words somewhere before. It was almost
exactly what the administrator had said at the corner.

*That was the first time I noticed it. People in this city worked in
different corners of it, and yet they all said the same thing. Nothing
to hide. What struck me was that it was true — and that made it sound
stranger still.*

The man stepped aside, and we went in.

---

Inside was narrow. A screen sat against one wall, with a single chair
in front of it. Drawers lined the opposite wall — the old-fashioned
kind, opened by hand.

Mena sat down and turned the screen on. Her fingers moved through
several screens, and then she looked back at me.

"…Ask if you're curious about anything. I'm working right now, so my
answers might be short."

"…That's fine."

Mena turned back to the screen. Her hand moved the same way it had
moved over paper the day before. Unhurried. Certain.

I sat beside her and looked at the screen. Names in rows. Numbers
beside the names, short phrases beside the numbers. I read a few.
*Commendation for diligence. Cooperative. Compliant with regulations.*
All good words.

"…This is all good things," I said.

"…That's the entry for people with high standing," Mena said. "Yours
will be different."

I tensed a little at that.

"…I have one too?"

"…You do." She scrolled a few times. My name came up. Standing zero.
Beside it, a single short line.

**"Newly registered. Below threshold for standing. Nothing out of the
ordinary."**

I read that sentence twice.

"…I have nothing out of the ordinary about me," I laughed.

Mena didn't laugh.

"…There's plenty. It's just not written down," Mena said. "The Roll
only calls something unusual by its own standard. Going to the corner
every day isn't unusual, by the Roll's standard."

"…Why not?"

"…Because nothing comes of it," Mena said. "The Roll sees people by
their outcomes. If there's no outcome, it treats everything before it
as if it never happened."

I thought about that for a long time.

---

"…How do you see it?" I asked.

For the first time, Mena took her hand off the screen.

"…I watch what people do," Mena said. "Whether it comes to anything or
not."

Mena pulled a sheet of paper from her case. My name was on it too.
Beside it was written:

**"Xene. Goes to the corner without fail, every day. Gives no reason.
Most days, comes home empty-handed."**

Reading that line, I felt something strange. It wasn't longer than the
Roll's sentence. But there was something more in it.

"…Why is this different?" I asked.

"…The Roll thinks I'm not there," Mena said. "I think I am. Even when
nothing comes of it, the fact that the person was there — that's still
true."

*I didn't fully understand that then. I do now. The Roll saw a person
by numbers. If the numbers didn't move, the fact that the person had
even stood in that place was erased. Mena was the opposite. Even when
the numbers didn't move, the fact that the person had been there was
never erased.*

"…Is Pikra in there too?" I asked.

Mena searched the name and found it. Pikra's number was low. Higher
than mine, but far below the city average. Beside it, a short line.

**"Standing reduced due to death of spouse and children. Renewal
approved."**

Something turned in me, reading that. Losing a husband and two sons —
here, it had been reduced to five words. *Standing reduced.*

Mena pulled another page from her stack of paper. Pikra's name was
there too. But beside it was a much longer sentence.

**"Glyke. Lost her husband first, then two sons in succession within
two years. Even so, told her two daughters-in-law three times to go
back. One stayed, one went."**

"…Is this the same person?" I asked.

"…Yes," Mena said. "The same person. But the Roll only writes what
this person lost. It doesn't write what this person did."

*That was the first time I understood, in exact words, the difference
between the Roll and Mena. The Roll wrote what was lost. Mena wrote
what the person had chosen to do in front of that loss. One was
subtraction. The other was a person.*

---

"…Why do you write?" I asked.

Mena stopped and looked at me. I'd tossed the question out lightly, but
her face didn't take it lightly at all.

"…Because if it's forgotten, it never happened," Mena said.

I echoed the words back.

"…If it's forgotten, it never happened."

"…Yes," Mena said. "People think that when a memory fades, the thing
itself fades. It isn't so. The thing stayed exactly as it was. It's the
memory that fades, not the thing."

"…But why does that have to be you?"

Mena smiled a little. The first smile of the day.

"…If I don't, no one does," Mena said. "The Roll only writes outcomes.
The administrator only keeps the code. The city only worries about the
city running smoothly. In between all that, there are people no one
writes down."

"…Like me?"

"…Yes," Mena said. "And like me."

---

I wanted to ask something more about that answer, but I didn't know
exactly what.

"…Are you someone no one writes down too?"

Mena turned the screen off for a moment.

"…Somewhere, probably," Mena said. "I never stay long in any one city.
No one's watched me long enough."

"…Doesn't that lonely you?"

Mena didn't answer right away. The silence felt long to me.

"…Sometimes," she said at last. "But instead of just enduring that
loneliness, I gained something from it."

"…What?"

"…Eyes that don't belong to anyone," Mena said. "If I were from this
city, I'd want to believe it was a good city. It's hard to live
otherwise. I'm not from here, so I don't have to believe that."

*Hearing that, I thought I finally understood why Mena carried that
plain, even face wherever she went. It wasn't indifference. It was a
face working hard not to take any side. I didn't know then how much
effort that took.*

"…So you're on no one's side?" I asked.

"…There's no side," Mena said. "I'm not someone who decides right and
wrong."

"…Then what are you?"

Mena thought a moment.

"…Someone who leaves behind the fact that something happened," Mena
said. "Whether it was good or bad — the person reading it decides that.
I don't stand in front of the judgment."

"…Isn't that hard? There must be times you want to judge."

For the first time, Mena smiled a little at that. A bitter smile.

"…Every time," Mena said. "Especially when it's someone I know. But the
moment I write down a judgment, I become the same as the Roll. The Roll
believes it's right, too, when it cuts a person down."

I turned that over for a long time.

"…So you don't even write that the Roll is wrong?"

Mena looked straight at me.

"…That's different," Mena said. "Calling something wrong when it's
wrong isn't judgment. It's fact. I write facts. I just don't lay my own
feelings on top of the facts."

At the time, that distinction felt only faintly there. Later I would
learn it was the most important distinction in this city.

---

Mena turned the screen back on. A different one this time. A list of
city names.

"…What's this?" I asked.

"…The reputation field," Mena said. "What I told you about yesterday.
Each city's Roll exchanges information with the others. Whatever
evaluation a person received elsewhere follows them here — it all
collects in this."

"…So?"

"…So there are things in here that never happened in this city," Mena
said. "Records that came from other places."

I asked, more or less idly,

"…Is anything you wrote in there too?"

Mena's hand stopped.

"…It's possible," Mena said. "I've done this work a long time. I don't
even know how far the things I've written have traveled."

*I didn't know then why that answer came out so thin. I do now. Mena
already sensed something, in that moment. She was about to confirm, for
the first time with her own eyes, how the work of a lifetime had
changed somewhere she couldn't see.*

Mena got up from her chair and went to the window. The field showed
outside it, the machines still turning. She watched the scene for a
while, then came back to her seat.

"…Xene," Mena said. "I've done this work a long time. There's one
thing you come to know, doing it that long."

"…What's that?"

"…What I write, the moment I write it, stops being mine," Mena said.
"Other people read it, copy it, summarize it, copy it again. I can't
control any of that."

"…Then why write it? If you can't hold onto it anyway."

Mena paused at the question.

"…Even if I can't hold onto it, I have to leave behind what was right
at the start," Mena said. "So that someone, someday, has something to
compare it against."

I didn't fully understand that answer then. Leaving behind what was
right at the start, and stopping something being twisted into wrong
later, sounded like two different things to me. I would come to
understand the difference far more clearly, a few days later.

---

"…Can I watch with you?" I asked.

Mena looked at me for a moment.

"…You can," she said. "But it might take a while. Once I'm in this, I
don't come out easily."

"…That's fine. I don't have to go to the corner today."

Mena smiled a little at that.

"…You go every day, you said."

"…Today I'll watch something else," I said.

Mena nodded and pulled her chair closer to the screen. Her finger
touched it. A list unfolded, long — city names, dates beside them,
case numbers beside the dates. Too many to count.

"…Are you reading all of it?" I asked.

"…Not all of it," Mena said. "Until a name I recognize comes up."

I sat beside her and watched her hand move down the list, line by line.
Her eyes ran over each row at a steady pace. She never hurried.

*I didn't find that slowness tedious. If anything, there was something
like respect in it. Mena never skimmed a single line — even when it
wasn't the name she was looking for.*

---

After a long while, Mena's hand stopped.

A single name sat in one line on the screen. The city wasn't this one —
a different city, a name I'd never heard. Beside it, in small letters,
were the words:

**"Source: Recorder Mena. Quoted."**

"…Is this you?" I asked.

"…Yes," Mena said. "I remember being in that city. A very long time
ago."

"…Strange, seeing your name here again."

Mena's face wasn't one of surprise. It was the face of someone
measuring something.

"…It isn't strange," Mena said. "I'd only ever heard, as a rumor, that
my records flow into other cities this way. This is the first time
I've seen it myself."

"…So now you've confirmed it, that's that, isn't it?"

Mena turned the screen again at that. The quoted sentence was short and
smooth. It looked flawless.

"…There's one more thing I have to confirm," Mena said. "Whether this
matches the sentence I actually wrote."

"…Could they be different?"

"…I don't know," Mena said. "That's why I have to look."

Mena opened her case. She took out a bundle of old paper. Her fingers
moved through it a little faster than before. She didn't show any
hurry outwardly, but I could feel the change in pace beside her.

*I didn't know exactly why that speed had changed, not then. I know
now. In that moment, Mena was already doubting something. It was the
kind of doubt only someone who had done this work a long time could
carry. I didn't know, that night, what that doubt would turn out to be
the next day.*

Mena found a sheet of paper that looked like the original. But by then
the day was already fading. The archive began to darken around us.

"…That's enough for today," Mena said, carefully returning the paper
to her case. "I'll look again tomorrow morning, in the light. This
kind of thing shouldn't be checked carelessly in the dark."

I felt something heavy in that. Mena might have stayed up all night
holding onto that paper. But she didn't. I don't know why, but not
checking it carelessly in the dark stayed with me a strangely long
time.

---

We left the archive together. The sun hung low. The shadows of workers
walking back toward the gate had stretched long.

"…Can I come again tomorrow?" I asked.

Mena looked at me for a moment.

"…You can," she said. "But Xene."

"…Yes."

"…Tomorrow might not be about confirming something good," Mena said.
"That's the feeling I have."

"…What kind of feeling?"

Mena stopped walking for a second.

"…A very long time ago, I saw something I'd written twisted in a
strange way," Mena said. "In another city. I thought it was something
that belonged only to that city."

"…And?"

"…Now I think I'm seeing the same thing in this one," Mena said. "I'm
not sure yet. I'll only know once I check."

Something cold ran through me, hearing that. I didn't know exactly why
at the time.

*That evening, for the first time, I saw the plainness fall entirely
from Mena's face. Only for a moment. It settled back into place almost
at once. But I saw the crack. I would come to know what that crack held
the next day.*

---

On the way home, I turned over, one by one, the screens I'd seen that
day. My name. Pikra's name. Lives arranged into numbers.

I passed by the field. The sun was setting, and the machines had
already stopped. A quiet field. The place that was so loud by day made
no sound at all in the evening.

In that quiet, I found myself remembering something my husband used to
say. *What you spill isn't wasted. It's left behind.* I set that phrase
beside the screens I'd seen that day. The Roll spilled nothing. So
nothing was left behind. Mena spilled nothing either. And yet
something was left behind. I couldn't put into words, then, what that
difference was.

*I can say it now. The Roll didn't spill, so that nothing would be
left. Mena, instead of spilling, copied it down by hand. The results
looked similar. Neither had lost anything. But in one case there was
nothing at all left in that place, and in the other, everything was
there, on paper.*

When I got home, Pikra was sitting by the hearth, working the stalks
she'd gathered that day.

"…You didn't go to the corner today, they say," Pikra said.

"…No. I went with Mena to the archive."

"…What did you see?"

I paused, choosing how to put it.

"…I saw your name too," I said. "It was in the Roll, and it was on
Mena's paper too."

Pikra's hands stopped.

"…What did it say?"

"…In the Roll, it said 'standing reduced,'" I said. "On Mena's paper
it said... the story about you telling your two daughters-in-law, three
times, to go back."

Pikra was quiet for a long while. She started working the stalks again,
her fingers moving a little slower than before.

"…So she goes around watching everything," Pikra said at last.

"…Yes."

"…And yet somehow, it's not frightening," Pikra said. "A person who
watches everything."

I laughed a little at that. I'd been thinking the same thing.

*Before I fell asleep that night, I turned that question over for a
long time. Why wasn't Mena frightening? I know now. The Roll decided
something after it watched. Mena decided nothing after she watched. She
just wrote it down. Eyes that decide nothing aren't frightening, no
matter how much they see.*

I lay awake late into the night. I looked out the window toward the
field. Nothing was visible in the darkness. But I knew that even in the
dark, that field was still letting nothing fall. Even at night, that
fact didn't change.

*I remember that night clearly, even now. I didn't know yet that what
Mena would discover the next day wasn't a problem belonging only to
this one city. I only meant to remember that day as the day I saw my
mother-in-law's name reduced to a few numbers. But that day was only
the beginning of something far larger than that.*
