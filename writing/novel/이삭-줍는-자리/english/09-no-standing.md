# Chapter 9. No Standing

> _Previously_ — At the gate, Xene finishes the new-registration
> process. Name, origin, parents, marital status — all of it goes into
> the screen. The mark in the center of the screen spins, the
> calculation beginning. Pikra takes her hand. The spinning stops.

A number came up.

**0**

I looked at it a long while. Thinking I might have misread it, I
looked again. It was still 0.

The person at the device stopped for a moment, looking at the screen.
I didn't miss that pause. Up to then, the person hadn't stopped once.

"…One moment," the person said. And signaled to someone else nearby.

---

A moment later, another person came.

This one walked unhurried. No trace of rushing at all. Their clothes
were different from the first person's too — a darker color, with a
small insignia on the collar.

"…What's the matter," the person asked. Their voice was low and even.

"…New registration, output value zero," the first person said.

"…Let me confirm."

The new person took over the screen. Fingers moved across it a few
times. I couldn't read anything from those movements.

"…That's correct," the person said. Still the same even voice. "Zero
is correct."

*I remember that moment still. The person said the number without
lowering their voice at all. No trace of apology. Just a plain
statement of fact. I found that stranger than any comfort would have
been.*

---

"…Miss Xene." The person looked at me and spoke. It was the first
time all day anyone had called me by my name that way. "May I explain
a little?"

I nodded. My voice wouldn't come.

"…A standing of zero isn't rare," the person said. "It happens when
there's no origin record, when a marriage occurred outside and isn't
recognized, and when there's no inherited value. The calculation isn't
wrong."

"…So what happens to me?"

"…Nothing happens," the person said.

I didn't understand what that meant.

"…Are you expelling me?"

"…No." The person shook their head. Very slightly. "We don't expel
anyone."

*This is a sentence I probably return to more often than any other in
this whole book.* We don't expel anyone. *I heard it many more times
after that. Every time, it was true. And every time, the fact that it
was true was the most frightening part of it.*

---

A few people waiting nearby glanced over at the exchange. No one
interrupted. Everyone simply waited their turn. I found that strange.
That someone receiving a zero wasn't, here, a particularly unusual
event.

"…Does this happen often?" I asked.

The person glanced at a corner of the screen for a moment. I saw a
small table there. A column of numbers.

"…This is the seventeenth today," the person said. As casually as
checking the time. "It varies by season, but ten to twenty a day,
usually."

I lost my words at that number. Twenty a day. Then, in a year. I
couldn't finish the calculation.

*That was the first time I understood I wasn't suffering some special
misfortune. That fact should have been a comfort. It wasn't. It
frightened me more. This city was already very much used to
manufacturing twenty people like me, every single day.*

I looked around. Here and there in the line, a few people took their
confirmation cards and quietly stepped away. I couldn't tell, from the
outside, which of them had received a zero. Everyone walked at the
same pace, the same gait. No one cried. No one raised their voice.

*That quiet struck me as the strangest part of all. I learned later.
Those people already knew that shouting would do nothing. A person who
knows it's pointless goes quiet. I read that quiet, that day, as
grief. Now I know it was resignation as well. The two wear similar
faces. They aren't the same thing.*

---

"…Expulsion isn't within our authority," the person said. "What we do
is renewal. If you have standing, we renew you. If you don't, we
don't. That's all."

"…What happens if it's not renewed?"

The person looked at me a moment before answering.

"…No rations will come. You can't apply for housing. You can't take
formal work, either," the person said, one item at a time, as if
reading a list. "Your name doesn't go onto the Roll. That's all."

"…That's the same as expelling me."

"…No," the person said again. No anger in it, no change in tone. "We
aren't telling you to leave, Miss Xene. You may stay here. We simply
have nothing to give you."

*I turned that sentence over many times, that night. Nothing to give
you. Not taking something away — giving nothing. The person knew that
distinction with total precision, and said it precisely. That
precision was what frightened me.*

---

"…What did I do wrong?" I asked. The question came out of me before
I'd meant to ask it.

The person looked at me for a moment. For the first time, the answer
was a little slow.

"…No," the person said. "You've done nothing wrong. Standing isn't a
measure of wrongdoing. It's a measure of what you have and what you
don't."

"…So this happened because I have nothing?"

"…That's right," the person said. Calmly. "No possessions, zero. It
isn't meant unkindly. It's simply arithmetic."

"…Can I see how it was calculated?"

The person looked at me a moment, then turned the screen so I could
see. I hadn't expected that.

A short list was on the screen.

**Origin contribution — 0**
**Inherited value — 0**
**Marital union value — not recognized**
**Residency history — 0**
**Total — 0**

I read it line by line. Each line had a number, and every number was
zero. None of the lines looked miscalculated. That was what made it
harder.

"…That's everything?" I asked.

"…That's everything," the person said. "Five fields. You had no value
in any of the five."

"…What about the marriage? I was married."

"…Marital union value is a field that combines two people's standing,"
the person said. "But it only applies to a marriage registered on the
Roll. Your marriage took place outside, and your husband died before
registration. There was no standing inside the Roll for it to combine
with."

Listening to that explanation, I watched the two years of my life
shrink into five lines. And every one of those five lines, zero.

*I still have that list memorized. I tried to forget it. It wouldn't
let go. Some numbers cling to a person that way.*

Hearing that, I felt, oddly, a little relieved. And that relief
shamed me at once. I was feeling relieved by the words of someone who
had just told me they would give me nothing.

*Now I know. That relief wasn't my fault. A person finds it easier to
bear being calculated without feeling than being told they are hated.
The Roll didn't know that and speak that way on purpose. It only
happens that speaking that way moves a person's heart that direction,
regardless.*

---

"…Can it change?" I asked. My voice shook.

"…Standing is a calculated value," the person said. "If the
calculation changes, the value changes. If you marry, or a relation
forms with someone who has standing, or the value comes through some
other channel."

"…And right now?"

"…Right now, it's zero," the person said. Very simply. "That's all
there is."

I looked at their face. Not angry, not sorry. Just the face of someone
doing their job.

"…This was done according to the law?" I asked. I didn't know why I
asked. I just wanted to ask it.

"…Of course," the person said. And for the first time, smiled a
little. A kind smile. "We don't do anything outside the law."

*I remember that smile for a long time. It wasn't an unkind smile. It
was truly a kind one. And yet every time I saw that smile, a chill ran
through me. That person genuinely believed they were doing something
right. And they were right — at least, before the law.*

Behind that person, on the wall, I saw a small placard, tucked among
other notices. "Roll Law, Article 2 — All residency standing is
determined by calculation, and shall be neither granted nor revoked
for any reason outside that calculation."

I read that sentence twice. I understood about half of it.

*Now I know. That article was the sentence holding up this whole city.
Neither granted nor revoked for reasons outside calculation. So the
person giving me nothing wasn't doing it from hatred — they were
keeping that article. And if I was ever to receive something, later,
it too would have to be arithmetic, not pity, not hatred. I read that
article that day without yet knowing what this city truly feared.*

---

"…Any further questions?" the person asked.

I said no. In truth there were many, but none of them would form into
words, right then. *Why me* rose to my throat and went back down. I
knew, even without being told, that it wasn't a question.

"…Then that's all," the person said. "Thank you for coming such a
distance."

*I turned that last remark over for a long time. Thank you for coming
such a distance. A courteous thing to say. Perhaps meant sincerely. But
that courtesy was the most frightening part of all. If the person had
been throwing me out, I could have hated them. But they didn't throw
me out. They simply gave me nothing, and said goodbye, politely.*

Pikra came and took my arm. She said nothing. I could tell, from the
strength in her grip, how worried she was.

We walked a few more steps into the city and stopped, just off the
main flow of people.

"…Are you all right?" Pikra asked.

"…Yes," I said. It wasn't a lie. It wasn't the whole truth, either.

Pikra studied my face for a long while.

"…I tried to stop you three times," Pikra said. Not an angry voice.
"Go, go, go, I said. And you didn't."

"…I don't regret it."

"…I know," Pikra said. "But I'm still sorry."

I shook my head at that.

"…It wasn't something you made me do. I chose it."

Pikra said nothing for a moment, then gripped my hand a little
tighter.

"…That's true," Pikra said. "You chose it. That doesn't make the
sorry go away."

*I didn't understand that, at the time. Why Pikra should be sorry for
something I'd chosen. Now I know. Some people, seeing another person
carry something in their place, don't let go of the sorry even knowing
it was that person's own choice. That sorry wasn't guilt. It was love,
turned into weight.*

---

I glanced back once as we passed through the gate. The person was
already looking at the next in line. Whatever had happened to me was
already, to them, something past.

At the far end of the line, I saw that couple again. The one with the
baby. The wife stood with the child in her arms, receiving something
onto her confirmation card. I didn't know what number they'd received.
Only that their faces looked a little brighter than ours.

I envied that. And the envy shamed me.

*I remember that retreating figure for a long time. Not a bad person.
I still think that, even now. That person had simply done their job
exactly, precisely as they'd set out to do it. But inside that
precision, there was no room for mercy to stand. Mercy had no field in
the calculation. There was no reason it should.*

Pikra kept walking, not letting go of my hand. Walking beside her, I
felt that something had gone missing, between the self I'd known this
morning and the self I was now. I didn't know, then, exactly what it
was.

*Now I know. What was gone wasn't standing. I never had standing to
begin with. What was gone was the self I'd been before I knew I would
be recorded nowhere in this city. That self doesn't come back.*

"…We don't expel anyone." Walking, I said the words over to myself
again, silently. However many times I repeated them, they sounded
strange. How could not expelling and giving nothing live inside the
same sentence at all — I didn't know the answer, that day.

*I would come to know the answer a little at a time, in the next
chapter — going deeper into the city, seeing how people like me
actually lived. The answer was inside the gate. It only took a few
days for me to learn to recognize it.*

Pikra walked on ahead. I followed, and felt, freshly, that I was
holding nothing in my hands. No confirmation card, no small tablet,
nothing. Nothing to receive meant nothing to hold. I kept telling
myself that was only natural, and still that day I learned, for the
first time, how heavy empty hands could be.
