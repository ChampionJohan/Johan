# Chapter 6. Pikra

> _Previously_ — Xene answers briefly: "I go where you go." She gives
> no reason. Glyke accepts the answer. Daphne has gone back to her own
> family. Xene and Glyke, now alone, keep walking toward the city. In
> the distance, they see a city-jurisdiction field for the first
> time — strangely quiet.

The first time I heard the request to change a name, it was the third
day of walking.

I wrote in Chapter 1 that outside, no one ever asks to have their name
changed. And the very person who'd said that did exactly that thing,
barely three days later. Sometimes a person does fastest the thing
they were sure they'd never do.

We walked the whole day again. The road kept widening and hardening.
It was no longer dirt but paved stone. Our soles hadn't adjusted to
that hardness, and we had to rest often.

*I'll set down the naming in this chapter. But first there's something
I should note. That third day, I saw Glyke stop walking, for the first
time. It wasn't from pain. She'd stopped because she'd seen
something.*

Stone roads wore a person down the longer you walked them. Dirt gave a
little under the foot and absorbed the shock; stone sent it straight
up through the legs. That day, for the first time, I learned what it
meant for it to be your bones aching, not just your legs.

More people passed us, day by day. Someone pulling a cart, someone
carrying a load, someone walking fast alone. None of them looked our
way. I found that strange. Outside, when a stranger passed, everyone
looked at least once. Here, no one did.

"…Why does no one look at us?" I asked.

"…No reason to," Glyke said. "There are too many strangers here for
strange to feel strange anymore."

I turned the phrase over. A place where strange isn't strange. I
couldn't decide whether that was a comfort or a fright.

*Now I know. It was neither comfort nor fright. It was simply nothing.
Strangeness not feeling strange means no one reacts to a stranger. A
place with no reaction has no welcome and no rejection either. It just
has nothing.*

We sat by the road at noon to rest our feet. Pikra looked at my foot
and clicked her tongue.

"…You've got a blister."

"…It's fine."

"…Fine, sure. Give it here." Pikra set my foot on her knee and
wrapped it in a strip of cloth. Her hands moved with practice. She'd
probably done the same for her own sons' feet, once.

As I felt her hands, I thought that these small things might be all
this woman had left to give me from now on. And somehow that didn't
feel like nearly enough at all.

---

A little past noon, there was a small grave marker beside the road. A
name was carved into the stone. An unfamiliar name.

Glyke stopped in front of it. Looked at it a long while.

"…Someone you know?" I asked.

"…No," Glyke said. "A stranger."

"…Then why look so long?"

Glyke answered after a while.

"…There's a name," Glyke said. "A dead person, and their name
remains. I find that remarkable."

I thought of the settlement's pile of nameless stones.

"…Is that how it is everywhere in the city?"

"…Probably. If you have money," Glyke said. "Even without money, at
least the name is kept. That's the law."

I felt relieved, hearing that. At least a name remains.

*I would learn how naive that relief was, later. A name remaining, and
what that name comes to mean — those are two different questions. I
didn't yet know what the word standing does to a name.*

We passed the marker and walked on. After a few steps, Glyke spoke
again.

"…Xene, do you know what your name means? Your own name."

I was a little startled by the question. "…No. It's just my name."

"…I don't know what mine means either," Glyke said. "I don't know who
named me, or why. It was just there, when I was born."

"…That's odd, come to think of it."

"…It is," Glyke said. "The word you use most, in your whole life, and
you spend your whole life not knowing what it means."

*I remember this exchange for a long time. At the time I took it for
a passing remark. Now I know. The meaning of names is revealed only
very late in this book. That day on the road, we were planting the
tiniest seed of that story, without knowing it.*

---

That evening we lodged in something like a roadside shed. It seemed
built for travelers passing through. Old straw was heaped inside.

Glyke lay down on the straw and stayed a long while without sleeping.
I had my eyes closed beside her, but I wasn't really asleep either.

"…Xene," Glyke called.

"…Yes." I opened my eyes.

"…Asleep or not."

"…Not asleep."

Glyke sat up. In the dark, I couldn't make out her face well.

"…I have a favor to ask."

"…Yes."

"…Once we're in the city, don't call me Glyke."

I didn't understand and stayed still a moment.

"…Then what should I call you?"

"…Pikra," Glyke said.

---

The instant I heard that name, my tongue felt as if it had stiffened.
I didn't know what it meant, but the sound itself was foreign.

"…What does it mean?" I asked.

"…You don't need to know the meaning," Glyke said. "Just call me
that."

"…But why all of a sudden—"

"…It isn't sudden," Glyke said. Her voice was firm. "I've been
thinking about it the whole way. Glyke is the name he called me by.
The name my sons called me by. There's no one left to call me by that
name. I don't want to be called by it anymore."

"…But I still know you as that name—"

"…That's exactly why I'm asking," Glyke said. "If you don't call me
by that name, I can consider it truly ended. But if you keep calling
me by it, I'll keep having to answer to it. Every time I answer to it,
I'll know all over again that the people who used to call me by it are
gone."

I had no answer to that.

*Now I know. This wasn't abandoning a name. It was changing the weight
a name had to carry. Glyke had been the name of someone who was loved.
To go on using that name where the love no longer existed was to
confirm, every time, that the love was absent.*

That night I thought of my husband's name. I've never once written it
in this record. I decided that from the start. But that night, for the
first time, I asked myself why.

*I won't give the full reason here either. But writing this chapter, I
realized something. By not writing that name, I was doing the opposite
of what Pikra was doing. Pikra was letting go of an old name to be
called by a new one. I hid the old name away so that no one could ever
call it at all. The methods were opposite. What we were trying to do
may have been the same. Some way of managing the weight a name
carries.*

---

"…You really won't tell me what Pikra means?" I asked.

"…No."

"…Where does the word come from, then?"

"…It's an old word," Pikra said. "My grandmother's generation used
it, I'm told. I don't know it exactly myself. I heard the elders use
it, sometimes, when they were angry."

"…Then why do you want it as your name?"

"…The sound fits," Pikra said. "You don't need the meaning. You hear
the sound and you know. This is my sound now."

I found that explanation strange, and understood it too. Some words
reach you through their sound before their meaning does.

"…Why?"

Glyke — I should write Pikra from here on, though that night I still
couldn't say the name aloud — was quiet a long while, then said,

"…If you knew, you'd look at me with pity," Pikra said. "I don't want
that."

"…I wouldn't look at you with pity."

"…You would. Once you knew. So just call me by the sound."

I never once managed to say that name aloud that night. My mouth
wouldn't form it.

Instead, I asked,

"…Then what do I get called? If you're changing your name..."

"…No, your name stays yours," Pikra said. I'll use that name for the
rest of this chapter, even just in writing. "I don't want to change
your name. It's mine I want to change."

"…Why not mine too?"

Pikra was quiet a moment.

"…You haven't lost anything yet," Pikra said. "Your name is still only
carrying the weight your name can carry. Mine isn't. Mine is carrying
weight it can't carry anymore."

Hearing that, I suddenly felt like a person who'd lost nothing. Even
though I'd lost my husband. That thought felt strange.

"…I lost something too," I said quietly.

Pikra reached over and took my hand at that.

"…You did too," Pikra said. "But Xene, the size of what was lost is
different. I lost everyone who made my life. You still have your life
left. I won't pretend that's the same."

I couldn't answer that. It sounded right, and it sounded wrong, both
at once.

*I think now that the distinction was right. Grief has size. Admitting
that isn't cruel. Flattening it all into the same thing is crueler.
Pikra already knew that, that night.*

---

The next morning, I tried it.

"…M-mother." I started, then stopped.

"…That's fine as it is," Pikra said. "Keep calling me Mother. It's the
name I want changed."

"…Right." I took a breath. "……Pi—Pikra……."

The sound came out awkward. As if it hadn't come from my own mouth,
but someone else's.

Pikra heard it and laughed quietly. A sad laugh, but a laugh.

"…Everyone's like that at first," Pikra said. "You'll get used to
it."

I practiced the name all that day as we walked. Silently, in my mouth,
not aloud. Pikra. Pikra. However many times I repeated it, it still
felt like someone else's name.

Around noon, without thinking, I said,

"…Gly—" and stopped myself.

Pikra looked at me. Not a scolding look. Just a waiting one.

"…Sorry," I said.

"…It's fine," Pikra said. "I'm still not used to being that name
myself. We're both learning it new."

That put me a little more at ease. I wasn't the only one failing at
it. She wasn't used to her own new name either.

By afternoon I'd gotten a little better. Once, I got it right, right
on time — "Pikra" — and Pikra answered. In that moment I felt I'd
crossed something.

"…There," Pikra said quietly. Almost to herself.

"…What is?"

"…Nothing." Pikra shook her head. "Just — there."

*Now I know. In that moment Pikra was confirming that her name had
finally been accepted by someone new. Changing a name isn't something
you can do alone. It needs someone to call it. That afternoon, I put
my hand to that completion for the first time.*

I noticed that afternoon that Pikra's walk had changed a little. Not
in any obvious way. But something in it was straighter. Before, she'd
kept glancing back as she walked; from that afternoon on, she only
looked ahead.

"…Are you uncomfortable somewhere?" I asked.

"…No, why?"

"…Your walk seems a little different."

Pikra thought for a moment, then said,

"…Maybe it is," Pikra said. "Once you take a new name, it feels like
you have to walk as the kind of person who carries it."

I didn't fully understand what she meant, but I got the gist. A name
wasn't just a sound you were called by. It changed how the person
carrying it stood, how they walked.

*It took me a very long time to get used to it. Even months later I'd
still catch, for a moment, before that name. I don't catch anymore.
Now it comes out as naturally as if it had always been her name. That
fact strikes me as strange, sometimes. A person can get used to
anything. Good things and sad things both.*

---

Late that afternoon, we finally saw the city in the distance.

I'll write that moment in the next chapter. In this chapter I'll set
down only one thing first. Seeing the city for the first time, the
first thought that came to me wasn't its size, or its splendor.

I looked at the person beside me. Pikra. I was already calling her by
that name aloud. In three days. She stood narrowing her eyes toward
that city. I saw fear and longing pass across her face at the same
time. It was the first time I'd seen two feelings fight like that on
the same face.

*Names change that fast. People change that slowly. Walking toward
that city, I felt, dimly, for the first time, that it takes far longer
for a person to change than for a name to.*
