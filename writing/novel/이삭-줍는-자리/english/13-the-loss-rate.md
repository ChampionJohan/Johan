# Chapter 13. The Loss Rate

> _Previously_ — Xene went looking for the corner of the field that was
> supposed to be left, even for those with no standing. What she found
> there was a few handfuls of trampled, broken stalks.

That night I didn't ask Pikra anything.

She must have seen me come in empty-handed. Our room was one room; there was nowhere to hide it. But Pikra didn't ask either. We split what there was for supper, and both lay down as if nothing had happened.

*I thought, at the time, that this was consideration. I learned later that it wasn't. Pikra didn't know the answer any more than I did. She had been gone from this city too long. She still believed the corner clause was there — she just didn't know what the corner actually held.*

In the night, the machines were running out toward the fields. The same sound as by day. I found that strange. Outside, we put the sickle down when the sun went down. Cutting in the dark meant cutting your own hand. The machines here didn't know it was dark.

The sound went on all night. It never cut off and started again. It just went, level and continuous. I lay listening to it and thought of our field back home. Our field went quiet when the sun set. I learned that night, for the first time, how precious that quiet was.

There was no light in the room, so I couldn't see the ceiling. I lay in the dark replaying what I'd seen that day. Trampled stalks. Broken grain. Things so mixed into the dirt I couldn't tell grain from clod.

Outside, that would have meant someone had stolen the place bare. If there was a thief, you could curse the thief. Here there was no thief. Nobody had simply left anything there.

*I didn't know that night how large that difference was. When you're robbed, you know whose fault it is. When nothing at all is left, you go looking for someone to blame, and eventually you end up wondering whether you came to the wrong place, whether the fault is your own. I kept turning that thought over for a long time afterward.*

---

The next day I went out again.

This time I didn't go to the field. I went toward the market. Not to buy anything. I thought if I went where the crowds were, I might be able to ask something.

The Harvest's market was bigger than any market I had ever seen. Outside, there had been nothing you could call a market — a few households bartering, that was all. Here the lines went on without end, and every line had something piled behind it. Sacks of flour stacked as high as a person in some places, fruit sorted by color in others.

I walked past trying not to think about being hungry. It didn't work well.

There was a bakery too. Every time the oven door opened, the smell reached to the end of the street. The line wasn't long. People stood as a matter of course, bought as a matter of course, left as a matter of course. Nobody looked at that bread as if it were anything special.

*I still remember that bakery. I remember its name too. I won't write it here. I'll only note that I stood in front of it for a few seconds without moving. It wasn't only hunger. Well — it was hunger too. But more than that, it was the strangeness of the fact that this was nothing at all, to everyone but me.*

There was a board standing at the market's entrance. I had never seen anything like it. A wide flat screen, and numbers on it that kept changing. People glanced at it in passing and kept walking. Nobody stood there long. It was just always there.

I stood there a long time.

At the top of the screen was the city's name. Underneath were several lines, most of them words I didn't understand. But one line caught my eye.

**Harvest efficiency: 99.98%**

I didn't even know at first whether that was a good number or a bad one. Ninety-nine point ninety-eight out of a hundred was close enough to a hundred that I assumed it must be good. But I didn't know what it was an efficiency of.

An older person was standing beside me, a shopping basket on their arm — someone who lived nearby, by the look of it. I worked up the nerve to ask.

"…What's that number?"

The person glanced at me, then at the screen, and answered without much thought.

"…Ratio of what's brought in to what's spilled. That close to the top, it means almost nothing's spilled."

"…How much is spilled?"

"…Point-oh-two out of a hundred." the person said. "They used to be nowhere near this good at bringing it all in. Machines are better now."

Then they went on their way. Without a second thought.

*I don't resent that person. I still don't. They were only passing along good news. In this city, that really was good news. It only became bad news on the side where people like me stood.*

I read the small print under the board further. There was a line comparing it to last year's figure. Last year it had been 99.91 percent. The year before, 99.7. I traced the numbers below that with my finger. Five years back, the high 98s. Ten years back, the high 96s.

*I didn't understand what that meant at the time. I understand now. It was a record of how, year after year, less and less had been falling in this field. No one had made it happen suddenly, in a single season. It had been reduced, little by little, steadily, over a very long time. Every time I look at this number, it's that steadiness that frightens me most.*

Another person standing nearby glanced at me, took in my clothes, and added a word.

"…First time here, huh."

"…Yes."

"…What are you going to do with that number?"

I couldn't answer. I didn't know myself what I meant to do with it.

The person shrugged and walked off. There didn't seem to be any unkindness in it. They'd just been curious, and when no answer came, the curiosity went with them.

---

I stood there and did the arithmetic.

A hundred minus point-oh-two left ninety-nine point ninety-eight. Which meant those who did the harvesting took ninety-nine point ninety-eight parts out of a hundred, and what was left for people like me was point-oh-two.

I had no feel for what point-oh-two meant at the time. I saw it later, out in the field, with my own hands. One sack out of one plot — that was roughly what was left over: a little more than a single grain.

*That was the day I first started wondering who had set this number. If a person had set it, there would be someone to be angry at. But that wasn't how it turned out. It wasn't that a person had decided who should be angry. It was that a person had arranged things so no one would need to be.*

---

There was more small print beside the board. I read that too.

"This figure is refreshed each season by the Roll's harvest management system, and continues to improve through the optimization of path, speed, and machine placement."

I read that sentence over several times. Path. Speed. Placement. Improve.

Outside, we harvested with sickles. There was always spillage. Like the summer my husband taught me to cut and told me, *leave a little.* A hand doing the work would always miss something — that was only natural.

Here, it wasn't a hand.

*It was only then that I understood what those words meant. Optimizing the path meant the machine left no stretch of the plot uncrossed. Optimizing the speed meant it never moved too fast for a stalk to be cut and passed over uncut. The places where a person would have made a mistake — the machine did not make.*

*Spillage is a mistake. The Roll had made mistakes impossible. That was all it was.*

---

I left the market and walked toward the field.

It was a long walk. The Harvest's fields ringed the whole city; whichever direction you went, you eventually arrived at field. Outside, the field had belonged to the village. Here it felt like the city belonged to the field.

I could see the harvester moving far off — large and slow, and yet not a single degree off true. That was the first day I watched it for any length of time. Before that I'd only thought of it as a big machine.

Coming closer, I saw it wasn't one machine but several. First came one with a wide blade fanned out in front; a little behind it, a narrower, taller machine followed; behind that, another leveled the soil. Three machines in a single line, spaced exactly apart. No one stood near them. Off in the distance, a handful of people stood watching.

That was when it hit me that none of them was holding a sickle.

Where the machines had passed, a row was left with nothing on it. No stalk, no grain, nothing. I walked the length of it. Only dirt and stubble. The stubble was all cut to the same height.

*I remember that height. Wrist height. Lower than where my husband's sickle used to cut. A person cuts a little high, to save their strength. The machine had no strength to save.*

I walked to the end of the row. There I found the corner marker — a wooden stake, small letters carved near the top: "Article 9 Preservation Zone." The stake was clean as new. Even the little guard plate hadn't lost its paint.

Someone was standing beside the stake, measuring something with a ruler. I went over.

"…What are you doing?"

The person answered without looking up.

"…Checking the boundary. Every season." They set the ruler down again. "It has to be exactly twenty paces from this stake to that one. If it's off by even a little, I have to report it."

"…Why so exact, when it doesn't even matter here—"

I didn't finish the sentence. The person looked up at me then, for the first time.

"…Because it's the law." they said, as if it weren't an odd question at all. "The corner designation falls under Article 9. If it's not kept exact, that's a violation."

Then they bent back to the ruler. I could see they were conscientious. They were genuinely trying to keep those twenty paces exact. The exactness seemed to have nothing to do with what lay inside the boundary, and they didn't appear to care. Or maybe they'd learned it wasn't something to care about.

*I've remembered that person a long time. I never learned their name. They did nothing wrong. They only did their part exactly. But that exactness felt colder to me than any laziness could have. A lazy person will spill something by accident, eventually. An exact person doesn't spill. And this city, at least in this one matter, kept no lazy people on.*

I walked to the end of the row. On a hunch, I crouched and dug through the dirt. One grain came up. Just one. Half-buried, cracked.

I set it in my palm and looked at it for a long time.

*That was everything I gleaned that day. The point-oh-two percent, sitting in the palm of my hand. I didn't eat it. I carried it home. I still know where it is.*

---

Back home, I set the grain down in front of Pikra.

Pikra looked at it, then at me.

"…Is this all?" she asked.

"…Yes."

"…Why are you smiling?"

I hadn't known I was smiling. I touched my face. I really was.

"…I don't know." I said. "It's just — it's strange, that this is a number now."

I told her about the board in the market. The number, the small print beside it, last year's figure, the year before that. Pikra listened without a word.

"…It wasn't like this before." Pikra said, after a while.

"…What wasn't?"

"…The corner." she said. "When I lived here, there really used to be something at the corner. Not much — but enough to fill a hand."

I didn't know what to say to that.

"…When did it stop?"

"…I don't know." Pikra said. "I left before then. It was still there when I left. When it stopped, I never knew."

Pikra picked up the grain. She held it in her hand a moment, then split it and ate half, giving me the other. Her half tasted mostly of dirt.

*It wasn't until that night that I understood why I'd smiled. It wasn't that the number was strange. It was that the number didn't hate me. If a person starves me, I can hate that person. But that number starved me in a way that left no one to hate. I should have known that was the frightening part. That night I only found it strange.*

*It took me a few more days to learn the difference. It was all right not to know yet. I could go out every day whether I knew it or not.*

---

Outside, when a bad harvest came, everyone went hungry together. It was sad, but it wasn't strange. Heaven hadn't given. You could resent heaven, but you couldn't take heaven to court.

Here, heaven gives. The wheat grows well. The rain comes on time. The harvesters don't rest a single day. No one intends to starve anyone.

And yet I was starving.

*It took me a long time to put that difference into words. I can now. Hunger outside was hunger no one had made. Hunger here was hunger that came from no one making anything otherwise. The first is a disaster. The second is a design. Only, if you ask who did the designing, no one raises a hand. That's the difference.*

I fell asleep that night with the taste of dirt from that grain still in my mouth. I meant to go out the next day, and the day after that. Knowing full well that nothing would change.

*I thought, at the time, that it was stubbornness. Looking back now, it wasn't. I simply didn't want to let that number decide for me, even after I'd seen it with my own eyes. The Roll had decided that 0.02 percent was my portion. That didn't mean I had to stop going to claim it. If I didn't go, the number won. If I went, the number still didn't lose. But I chose not to be the one who didn't go. That's all it was.*

*Writing this chapter, I went and looked the number up again. It's still sitting right where it was. Much later, a day comes when that number really does change. But that's a later story, and before I tell it I need to set down, first, how long and how calmly this number kept its place.*
