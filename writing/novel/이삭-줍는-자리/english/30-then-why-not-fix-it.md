# Chapter 30. Then Why Not Fix It

> _Previously_ — The quotations Mena found overnight came to seven. All
> the same direction. No explanation becoming no remorse, silence
> becoming guilt. He said he'd known about this for a very long time.
> Xene decided on the question she'd ask Mena the next day.

That morning, before I'd even reached the archive door, I ran the
question through my head several times.

*Then why not fix it.*

I thought it was an obvious question. I wasn't so naive, at sixteen, as
to not know how hard it is to set right something you know is wrong.
And yet in that moment it felt simply obvious. If you'd found seven
wrongs, the next step was to fix seven wrongs.

Mena was there. She looked a little better than the day before. A face
that had slept some. The case sat closed beside her. I felt a small
relief seeing the lid shut. At least this morning, no new sentence
seemed about to leap out of it.

The old man was drawing water in the yard. He saw me and gave a light
wave. It felt different from yesterday's wave — yesterday's had been
careful; today's was just an ordinary greeting. This was a city that
didn't carry yesterday's weight into today. Whether that was fortunate
or something to regret, I still don't fully know.

"…Mena," I said, from the doorway. "Then why not fix it."

Mena looked at me. Not a surprised look. If anything, it seemed she'd
been expecting that exact question.

"…Sit down," Mena said. "It'll take sitting down, to talk about this."

---

I sat beside her. The table was cleaner than yesterday. All the paper
had gone back into the case. I guessed that meant Mena had made some
kind of peace with it, overnight.

"…What do you want to fix?" Mena asked first.

"…Those sentences," I said. "They were twisted, weren't they? Just set
them back the way they were."

"…Where would you fix it?" Mena asked again.

I couldn't answer right away. Thinking about it, there was no
"where."

"…This city?" I finally managed.

"…This city's Roll only quoted my sentence," Mena said. "This city
didn't twist it. This city only took what came from another city and
used it as it was."

"…Then that other city."

"…That other city is the same," Mena said. "I traced five of the seven
back yesterday and this morning. I couldn't find a starting point
anywhere. Everyone says they received it from the city before them."

---

"…Then the very first city," I said. "It must have started
somewhere."

Mena nodded.

"…That's right. It started somewhere," Mena said. "I know one of
those. It's the one I lived through myself. But if I went to that
city, what would I be able to do?"

I couldn't answer that.

"…Go and confront them," I said. "Tell them they got your words
wrong."

"…Say I confront them," Mena said. "Say the city says, 'Yes, we were
wrong,' and fixes the sentence. Then what about all the judgments
already handed down on the basis of it? What about the other cities
that already quoted it? I couldn't even finish counting how many times
that sentence has been re-cited."

*That was the first time I saw the true size of this problem. I had
thought of a wrong, until then, as something like a point — something
you find and erase. This wasn't a point. It was water already spilled.
Where do you erase water from?*

---

"…Then from now on," I said. "Just make sure it doesn't happen
anymore."

"…How?" Mena asked.

"…Make a rule," I said. "That whoever quotes something has to check the
original first."

Mena smiled a little for the first time. A weary smile.

"…Xene, that's a good idea," Mena said. "But who makes that rule?"

"…The administrator."

"…This city's administrator can only make rules for this city," Mena
said. "He has no power over another city's administrator. This city
could make a rule to always check originals, and it would mean nothing
if the other cities didn't follow it."

"…Then what if all the cities decided it together?"

Mena looked at me steadily.

"…For that, every city would have to gather in one place," Mena said.
"I've never seen such a gathering. Cities exchange Rolls with each
other. They don't meet to decide anything. Exchanging is easy. Meeting
to decide is hard."

---

Listening, I thought suddenly of the phrase "Book 3." The thing Mena
had only called "that other city, a long time ago" the day before. I
grew curious about it for the first time.

"…That thing that happened in that city," I asked, carefully. "What
happened then? Was there no place to fix it there either?"

Mena looked out the window for a moment.

"…Someone else tried to fix it, back then," Mena said. "Not me. Someone
else. Very hard."

"…What happened?"

"…That person ended up at the same place, in the end," Mena said. "The
place where there's no place left to fix it. That city's judgment
system had never once broken the law. Not a single time. So there was
no way to fault the system itself."

Something felt familiar in that.

"…That," I said. "Isn't that the same story as this city?"

Mena looked at me. A little surprised.

"…How did you think of that?"

"…This city's Roll has never broken a law either," I said. "The corner
clause is being kept, isn't it. And yet there's nothing at the corner.
The administrator said it himself. We've never broken the law."

---

Mena looked at me for a long moment. Something else was in her eyes.

"…That's right," Mena said finally. "Exactly that. I've had a feeling
of this for a few days now, and just now, Xene, you put it into words
before I did."

"…So is it all the same thing? Nothing at the corner, and your words
being changed?"

"…The shapes are different," Mena said. "But the frame underneath is
the same. No one breaks the law. And yet someone, always, is left
empty-handed. It's true for the owner of that sentence. And it's true
for you."

I felt a chill hearing that. I had never once thought, until that
moment, that the empty hands at the corner and Mena's shaking hand
might be the same story.

"…So does the Roll, and the judgment system Mena went through in that
city, do exactly the same thing?" I asked.

"…Not exactly the same," Mena said. "The Roll measures people. That
city measured guilt. They measure different things. But the manner of
measuring is alike. Both are extremely precise, both work hard never
to be wrong, and both, within that precision, leave someone out."

"…It's not on purpose, is it."

"…That's right," Mena said. "It isn't on purpose. Which is exactly why
it never gets fixed. If it were on purpose, you could change the mind
behind it. If it isn't, there's no mind to change at all. No one thinks
they've done anything wrong."

I thought of Lytron again, hearing that. He truly didn't think he'd
done anything wrong. Neither did the administrator. I already sensed,
dimly, that Lytron wasn't the only person in this city like that.

*That was the first time I fully felt what this book was really
asking. Whether not breaking the law and being good are the same
thing. I had no answer to that question then. I don't have a complete
one now, either. But I would spend the years after that learning how
to go on living while holding that question in my hand.*

---

"…So there's really nothing at all we can do?" I asked. My voice, even
to my own ear, carried something like resentment.

Mena shook her head.

"…It isn't that there's nothing we can do," Mena said. "It's that
there's nowhere to fix it. Those are different things."

"…What's different about them?"

"…If there's somewhere to fix it, you go there and fix it, and that's
the end," Mena said. "If there's nowhere to fix it, you have to do
something else. I don't know exactly what that is yet. But I know one
thing. Keep leaving it, exactly, accurately, behind. So that someone,
someday — someone with more power than I have — can come and see it."

"…When would that be?"

Mena thought for a moment. The moment felt long to me.

"…I don't know," Mena said. "I'm not the one who gets to decide that.
I'm only someone who leaves this accurately behind now, so that
whatever there is to see, when that day comes, is there to be seen."

"…What if it takes too long? What if that person never comes?"

Mena looked at me a moment. Sorrow and stubbornness sat together in
her face.

"…I'll still leave it behind," Mena said. "If I don't, then even if
that person comes, there'll be nothing for them to see. I don't know
when they'll come. But I don't want to send them away empty-handed
when they finally do."

Strangely, that made me think of the corner. Nothing in that field, and
yet I went out there every day all the same. Mena kept writing, knowing
she couldn't fix it. I understood, for the first time, that those two
things were the same kind of stubbornness.

I still wasn't satisfied with the answer. But at the same time, I knew
Mena wasn't saying it to soothe me. It was what she actually believed.

I stood up.

"…Where are you going?" Mena asked.

"…To the administrator," I said. "I'll ask him myself."

Mena didn't stop me. She only nodded.

"…Go ask him," Mena said. "I'm curious too. What he'll say."

---

The administrator was at his office by the gate. I hesitated a moment
outside, then went in.

The administrator recognized me. No surprise showed. He offered me a
seat, without ceremony.

"…What can I do for you."

"…Mena's own words are getting twisted into something bad, over and
over, in different cities," I said. "Seven times now. It's here in
this reputation field too. Can't this be fixed?"

The administrator tapped the screen a few times. It seemed he'd found
the entry I meant, and looked it over for a moment.

"…I've confirmed it," he said. "This entry came from another city's
Roll. We didn't generate it."

"…Can it be deleted, then?"

"…That isn't within our jurisdiction," he said. "We only reference the
data we receive. We have no authority to alter it. You'd need to
request that from the originating city."

"…I don't even know which city that is."

The administrator looked at me for a moment. For the first time,
something like it crossed that plain face. Not sympathy. More the
expression of a man hearing a question he'd already heard many times
before.

"…That happens often," the administrator said. "Data grows less
traceable the more hands it passes through. Our city has committed no
violation. We're presenting what we received, honestly."

*I thought of what I'd heard at the corner, a few days before, hearing
that answer. We have nothing to hide. We've never broken the law. I
felt, in that moment, that though those words had come from different
mouths, they still sounded, somehow, like a single voice.*

"…So this city has no responsibility at all?" I asked.

"…Not legally, no," the administrator said. "I'm not in a position to
say anything beyond that."

I had no answer to give him. The administrator wasn't a bad man. He was
simply a man who drew, exactly, the line between what he could say and
what he couldn't.

---

On my way out of the office, I ran into Lytron. He stopped when he saw
my face.

"…Something happen?" he asked.

I told him briefly what had happened with the administrator. Lytron
listened to it all and was quiet a moment.

"…I'd have said the same thing, in his place," he finally said.

I felt a flicker of anger at that.

"…You too?"

"…Don't be angry," he said. "I'm not saying the administrator's
wrong. I know this city's systems. Tracing where data came from is
outside our authority. I don't know anyone who could do that either."

I felt it as evasion and asked again.

"…Then who's the most powerful person in this city? Can't even they do
it?"

Lytron thought a moment.

"…Not even my father can," he said. "That much I know for certain. No
matter how high our family's score is, we have no authority to touch
another city's Roll. Authority isn't something you buy with a score.
There's simply no such position at all."

"…So no one can do it?"

Lytron thought again.

"…Unless there's someone who can view every city's Roll," he said.
"But there's no such person. Each city manages only its own Roll. They
receive what's sent to them, but no one holds the authority to look
into another city's Roll."

"…What about Mena? Mena goes between cities."

Lytron looked at me for a moment at that.

"…Mena has no authority either," he said. "As far as I understand it,
Mena is someone who watches, not someone who fixes."

I found that strangely exact. Someone who watches, and someone who
fixes. That was the first time I heard that distinction stated so
plainly.

*That day I understood that Lytron hadn't meant it unkindly. He was
simply someone who knew this city's structure precisely. And I learned,
that day, that the more precisely someone knew it, the faster they
arrived at a dead end in front of a question like this.*

---

I thought of the field for a moment. And what Lytron had said the day
before came back to me. He'd said something like the administrator too.
That the man hadn't been wrong either. That it wasn't his.

*That was the first time I felt that everyone I'd met in this city —
the administrator, Mena, even Lytron — stood, in the end, before the
same wall. No law broken. And yet not good. I felt, that day, for the
first time, that this wall was the hardest wall in the whole book. As
if I had reached out and touched it with my own hand.*

Mena had begun putting her papers back in order. Her hands were
steadier than yesterday's.

"…Xene," Mena said. "I've carried this question for a very long time.
Then why not fix it. I think a person who asks that question is better
off than one who doesn't. Not stopping that question is the one
resistance I have left."

"…Resistance?"

"…Yes," Mena said. "Not fixing it, but also not accepting it as
something natural. I believe there's a very important difference
between those two."

I held onto those words for a long time.

---

That evening, I told Pikra everything that had happened that day. The
administrator, Lytron, what Mena had said.

Pikra listened all the way through and was quiet for a long time.

"…Aren't you angry?" I asked. "Everyone keeps saying it isn't their
fault."

"…I'm angry," Pikra said. "But Xene, I spent that anger a long time
ago."

"…What do you mean, spent it?"

Pikra set down what she'd been holding.

"…When I was young, I asked the same thing you're asking," Pikra
said. "Then why not fix it. I don't remember who I was talking to
then. But I remember what they said back. 'There are wrongs you don't
even know who to ask about.'"

I was startled at that.

"…You heard the same thing."

"…I did," Pikra said. "At the time, it sounded like an excuse. Now it
doesn't. Now I think it was just a plain statement of how this world
is actually built."

"…Then what do we do? Just put up with it?"

Pikra shook her head.

"…Putting up with it and going on living are different things,"
Pikra said. "I don't tell you to put up with it. I tell you to go on
living. You'll learn the difference between those two, someday."

"…What was he like?" I asked. The question came out before I meant
it to. "Did he carry something he couldn't fix, too?"

Pikra's hands stopped. She looked at me for a long time.

"…He was a little different," Pikra said. "When he saw something he
couldn't fix, instead of getting angry, he changed whatever he could
reach with his own hands. He couldn't change the whole world, so he
gathered his own little plot of field a little differently. Left
something behind, spilled something."

Hearing that, something ached in my chest. Longing for that man came
over me again.

"…Could I live that way too?" I asked.

Pikra smiled for the first time. A real smile.

"…You already are," Pikra said. "Going out to the corner every day —
that's exactly it."

I lay in bed that night and thought of Pikra's smile again. A real
smile. Only then did I realize how long it had been since I'd seen
one.

*I thought about it long into the night. Putting up with it, and going
on living. I couldn't quite grasp the difference then. I know a little
of it now. Putting up with it is doing nothing and simply enduring.
Going on living is doing today's work while still carrying the thing
you can't fix. Mena arranged her papers again that day. Pikra worked
her stalks again that day. I went out to the field again that day. None
of us fixed the wrong. And yet none of us stopped, either.*

*Since that day, I've asked myself this question, over and over. Then
why not fix it. I still haven't found a clean answer to it. But I know
now what it means not to stop asking. That's the thing Mena taught me
that has stayed with me the longest.*

*I fell asleep that night having decided to ask Mena a different
question the next day. Not how to fix things, but how to leave things
behind. I had no idea, then, how far that question would take me.*
