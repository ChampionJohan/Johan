# Chapter 3. I'm Going Back

> _Previously_ — Having lost her husband and two sons in order, Glyke
> declares that winter that she will return to the city. Daphne decides
> to go back to her own family. Xene knows she has a family's house to
> return to as well, but that whole spring, she never once thinks of
> it.

I asked Glyke, more than once, why she wanted to go back, after that
night. The answer was different every time. At first that struck me as
strange. Now I know. Every different answer was true. A person's heart
doesn't move for just one reason.

*I'll set down those reasons in order, in this chapter. Not that there
was an order to them. I'll only set them down in the order I heard
them.*

Even now, people sometimes ask me, "Why did you go into the city?" I
always answer with one of these three reasons. Which one I pick
depends on my mood that day. All three are true, so none of them is a
lie.

---

The first reason I'd already heard. *I don't want to be left alone.*

But the day after I heard it, I noticed there was more to it than
that. Glyke had spent the morning picking things up one by one around
the house. She'd lift a bowl, set it down; touch an old blanket, fold
it.

"…What are you looking for?" I asked.

"…Nothing," Glyke said. "Just touching things."

"…Why?"

"…Everything here is going to stay here," Glyke said. "There's not
much I can carry. So I'm touching it now, while I can."

Hearing that, I looked again at the things in that house — the hearth,
the water jars, the sickle he'd used. All of it worn smooth by use. It
was only then that it truly hit me — none of it could go to the city.

*I return to this scene often. After I came into the city, I had to
relearn what it means to own something. In the city, without standing,
even what you have isn't fully yours. But the things Glyke was
touching that day had been hers since before the word standing
existed. I didn't understand the difference then. I do now.*

The second reason I heard a few days later, while we were milking the
goats in the yard.

"…Xene, do you know what happens here when someone dies?" Glyke
asked.

"…You bury them. Here."

"…That's right, you bury them," Glyke said. "But you don't mark it.
One stone on top, that's all. A few years on, no one even remembers
whose stone it was."

Hearing that, I thought of the pile of stones in the corner of the
yard.

"…Is the city different?"

"…In the city they carve the name," Glyke said. "There's a thing
called a headstone. They cut the name into the stone. That way, a
hundred years on, you can still tell whose place it is."

"…Why does that matter?"

Glyke's hands stopped.

"…I thought it didn't matter," Glyke said. "But after losing them, I
understood. If a name doesn't remain, even the fact that they existed
starts to blur. I don't want that — the fact of them — to blur."

*That answer made sense to me, at the time. It's strange that it did.
I had never lost anyone like that, not yet. And still I understood, or
felt I did. Maybe there's another road to understanding grief besides
grieving yourself. Watching from beside someone might be one of them.*

That night I went out alone to the pile of stones in the yard. Moonlight
let me make out their shapes — small stones, large ones, square ones,
broken ones. Stacked with no order to them.

I touched one with my hand. It was cold. I wondered who had set this
stone down first, in the place he'd fallen from the roof. Glyke, or
someone from the village. I'd never asked.

*Looking back, it's strange. I wasn't a curious person, ordinarily.
But that night I truly was curious. Standing in front of that pile of
stones, I had a thought for the first time. Without a name, these
stones would just become stones, in time. Stones no one remembers as
anyone's place.*

---

The third reason came latest, and shortest.

It was the day we packed, in spring. I was watching what Glyke packed.
Not much. A few clothes, a few bowls, and one small bundle of cloth.

"…What's that?" I asked.

Glyke unwrapped it. Inside were small stones. A few taken from the
pile in the corner of the yard.

"…I have to take at least these," Glyke said.

"…That's it? That's the reason?"

Glyke looked at me. After a long while, she said,

"…Xene, honestly, I can't put the whole reason into words. Not
wanting to be left alone, that's real. Wanting a name to remain,
that's real too. But the truth is just — my feet are going that way.
If you go looking for reasons, more keep coming. But my feet are
already moving."

I didn't quite understand that, at the time.

*I know now. It was the same for me. Whenever people later asked me
why I followed, I gave a different answer each time. All of them were
true. But the truth was simply that my feet went that way.*

---

In the last few days before we left, I looked over every corner of
that house once more. No one told me to. My feet just moved that way.

The threshold I'd sat on the first day I came to that house. The
corner of the yard where my husband taught me the sickle. The corner
of the room where Daphne and I had bitten our blankets and cried at
night. I went and touched each of those places, one by one.

Glyke did something similar. Once I found her standing a long while in
the shed, though there was nothing much there to see.

"…What are you looking at?" I asked.

"…Nothing," Glyke said. "I'm just looking at the time I spent
standing here."

I didn't quite follow that, at the time.

*Now I know. A place holds the time a person spent there. You can't
see that time, but standing in that place, you feel it again. Glyke
spent nearly thirty years in that shed. She was leaving that time
behind. I was only leaving behind two years, and still there were
places my feet wouldn't come away from.*

The morning we left, I went into our room one last time. The
belongings were already packed; the room stood empty. I stood there a
moment.

*I said nothing in that room. No prayer, no farewell. I just stood
there. I think, looking back, that was the only farewell I had to
give that room. Standing without a word.*

---

Word got around the village, and people came one or two at a time.
Most of them hadn't come to talk us out of it. Most of them had simply
come to say goodbye.

The old man — the one who'd come every day for those four months —
came last.

"…Live well in the city," he said.

"…I don't know if *living well* is even possible there," Glyke said.

The old man laughed. It was a sad laugh.

"…I don't know either," he said. "I've never once been to the city.
But every time I've seen people come from there, they always seemed to
have more than us and be in more of a hurry."

"…A hurry, how?"

"…They're always measuring something. Measuring time, measuring
people," the old man said. "Nobody measures anything here. I don't
know what's left over once you're done measuring."

*I thought back on this conversation often, after I came into the
city. The old man was right. The city measures. What the old man
didn't know was this: measuring itself isn't the wrong thing. The
problem is what happens after the measuring. The city measures, and
then gives nothing to anyone who doesn't fit the standard. That's the
frightening part.*

The old man turned to go, then pulled me aside.

"…Xene."

"…Yes."

"…Why are you going?" he asked. "You're not one of us, not
originally. You were born here — you could stay here."

I couldn't answer right away.

"…No reason," I said.

The old man looked at me a long while, then nodded.

"…No reason is the heaviest answer there is," he said. "A light
person gives you a reason. A heavy person says no reason."

I didn't know if that was a compliment. I still don't, really.

*Now I understand it this way. A choice you can give a reason for is a
choice you can calculate. If you can calculate it, you can calculate
it again, later, and go back. A choice you can only call* no reason
*has no room left to go back to. The old man probably knew that.*

---

The night before we left, I had my last talk with Daphne. I didn't
know then that it would be the last.

"…Xene," Daphne said. "Are you really going all the way to the
city?"

"…Yes. Following Mother."

"…I might too," Daphne said. "But I'm scared. You've heard the
stories. They say people from outside get ranked low."

"…I've heard."

"…And still?"

"…I don't know," I said. "I just want to be near Mother."

Daphne looked at me a long while, then said, "…You're strange, Xene.
Not kind. Strange."

I didn't take it badly. If anything, it felt true.

*Looking back now, Daphne had it exactly right. I didn't follow
because I was kind. Kindness is a matter of enduring or bearing
something, and I wasn't enduring or bearing anything. I simply thought
it was what I was meant to do. The two are different things.*

---

The next morning, the three of us set out together, our packs on our
backs.

Daphne still hadn't made up her mind, but she came with us anyway. The
road to the city ran alongside the road to her own village for a long
stretch, she said. They'd walk together to the fork and decide there.

A few people from the village came out to see us off. The old man was
among them. No one cried. Instead, everyone pressed something into our
hands — a loaf of dry bread, a pinch of salt, a spool of thread. Glyke
took it all, saying thank you some twenty times.

We walked all that day. The road, familiar at first, turned strange
around noon. I had never before been more than half a day from our
settlement.

*I don't remember that day in much detail. That we walked, that the
sun rose and set, that our feet ached. That's about it. But a few
scenes remain.*

Around noon we rested by a dry streambed. Daphne shared out the bread
she'd been given earlier. As the three of us ate, Daphne said, out of
nowhere,

"…I think I've been on this road once before. When I was little."

"…When?" I asked.

"…Following my mother. We went to see the city. Just the once,"
Daphne said. "But I didn't like it at all. Everyone was in such a
hurry."

Glyke laughed quietly at that. "…That's just how it is there."

"…Then why are you going?"

Glyke walked on without answering.

I watched her back, and asked Daphne quietly,

"…How old were you then?"

"…Around eight. Why?"

"…Just curious. What do you remember most?"

Daphne thought for a moment, then said,

"…That people didn't call each other by name," she said. "Here
everyone calls everyone by name. There, they call out something like
numbers. That scared me."

I had nothing to say to that.

*I would remember this conversation again, a few days later, at the
city gate. What Daphne had seen wasn't numbers. I learned, later. It
was standing rank. That an eight-year-old's eyes had found it strange
too — I still find that remarkable, thinking of it now.*

By afternoon, fields began appearing on either side of the road. Far
more evenly divided than the fields back home. The furrows ran
straight as though drawn with a ruler.

"…Are those city fields too?" I asked.

"…Not quite yet," Glyke said. "That's just the edge of the city's
reach. The real city fields are further along — straighter than this.
Quieter, too."

"…What does it mean for a field to be quiet?"

Glyke thought a moment.

"…You'll see when we get there."

*I didn't think much of it at the time. I thought it was just a
manner of speaking, a field being quiet. Days later I learned exactly
what it meant. There was hardly any birdsong in the city's fields, or
insect sound either. The machines were so precise that whatever lived
there couldn't seem to find a place to settle.*

As evening came and the fork drew near, Glyke stopped walking.

"…Let's rest a while here," Glyke said.

We set our packs down under a large tree by the road. I can still
draw that tree exactly, even now. An old tree, its trunk split into
two. The road split into two there as well. At the time, I thought it
was only coincidence.

*Now I don't think it was coincidence. In this world, every place
where a road forks seems to carry some mark of a fork — a tree, a
rock, a river. No one made the mark. It's only that, in a place like
that, even the world itself seems to take a forked shape.*

The sun hung low. Daphne set her pack down and sat first. I sat down
too. Glyke didn't sit. She stood.

I felt something strange, then. Glyke had been silent the whole way
here. But I sensed, in that moment, that her silence wasn't the
silence of exhaustion — it was the silence of someone preparing
something.

Glyke looked back and forth between the two of us, then, after a long
while, opened her mouth. Her voice was different from usual.

"…Girls," Glyke said. "From here, each of you goes your own way."
