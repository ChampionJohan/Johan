# Chapter 41. Entered

> _Previously_ — After Lytron's standing fell, the way people treated him
> shifted, little by little. The order of greetings. Where he sat. How he
> stood in line. For the first time in his life, he had to accept the
> word "no" and simply stand there with it.

Someone came from the Roll office.

It was four days after the day of Lytron's signature. No one came in
person. A small slip of paper arrived. A notice of completed
registration, it said.

I took the paper and looked at it for a long time. My name was on it.
Xene. Below it, a city Roll number. Below that, a date of registration.
Below that, a standing score.

*I still have this piece of paper. The color has faded and the corners
have folded. I never threw it away. I couldn't.*

---

Pikra looked at the paper and said nothing at first. She just took it
from my hand, studied it for a long while, and handed it back.

"…We should go confirm it," Pikra said.

"…Confirm it?"

"…On the screen," Pikra said. "Not the paper. You have to see how the
Roll itself has it written to know it's real."

That sounded strange to me. It was already written on the paper. I
didn't understand why we'd need to look at a screen as well.

*I know now. In this city, what was real was always on the screen's side.
The paper was only a copy of what the screen said. It wasn't that the
paper came first and the screen confirmed it after — the screen was the
original, and the paper was its shadow. Pikra already knew that.*

---

We went to the office.

The administrator received us. Even as always.

"…You've come to confirm the registration," he said. He already knew.

He tapped the screen and turned it toward us.

There was a name. Xene. Beside it, a number.

I looked at that number.

It wasn't zero.

---

I looked at that number for a long time.

It wasn't a large number. What Lytron had given wasn't the whole of his
family standing — only enough to clear the registration threshold. The
administrator had explained that already. "It's enough. Enough to bring
Miss Xene above the registration threshold."

So this number wasn't a generous one. It was barely enough.

*And still I looked at it for a long time. Whether it was generous or
not didn't matter to me then. All that mattered was that it wasn't zero.
I had lived sixteen years without ever once seeing a number beside my
name that wasn't zero.*

"…Would you like to see more?" the administrator asked.

"…Yes," I said.

I don't know how long I stood in front of that screen. The administrator
didn't hurry me. Someone else must have been waiting, and he never
showed a trace of it to me.

There was more on the screen. A field called history. I opened it and
found my whole life summarized in a few lines. Place of birth, marriage,
bereavement, migration. Four lines. Sixteen years, into four lines.

*Looking at those four lines, I felt something strange. I should have
been angry, and I wasn't. I thought, instead, that the shortness was
honest. The Roll hadn't inflated my sixteen years. It had set them down
exactly as they were, in four lines. The problem was only that the
number beside those four lines carried a weight far heavier than the
lines themselves.*

My hand stopped at the third line. *Spouse — deceased,* it read. A date
beside it. I knew that date. I knew it without needing to count.

"…Does this count too?" I asked. My voice shook a little.

The administrator looked at me for a moment.

"…It counts," he said quietly. "I'm sorry. This is how the Roll is
built. It doesn't look at what a person went through. It looks at the
value that experience left behind."

I felt, in that moment, that the man genuinely was sorry. And at the
same time, I knew that sorrow changed nothing at all.

*That was the day I saw, exactly, how the Roll reads grief. It doesn't
read grief as grief. It reads it as value. I loved that man; that's why I
called him my husband. The Roll called him by a single number. That love
and that number were written side by side on the same line — that was
the hardest thing to bear, that day.*

---

Below the number were other fields.

Residency standing. Ration standing. Labor standing. Each marked
*valid.* I read the word, one field at a time, without speaking it
aloud.

*I'd seen these fields before. At the gate. They had all read *invalid*
then. The administrator had explained it to me then, too. Not
expulsion. Refusal to renew. No one was driving me out. Only, nothing
was being given.*

*The words hadn't sounded like comfort then. They sound a little
different now. Between giving nothing and giving something, there is a
distance no words can fully hold. I saw that distance, with my own eyes,
in front of this screen, for the first time.*

"…What changes now?" I asked.

The administrator tapped the screen a few more times.

"…You can apply for housing," he said. "Until now you were listed under
Miss Pikra. Now you can apply in your own name. Rations will come to you
separately, too. There are jobs only registered people can apply for —
you'll be able to see that list now, as well."

I didn't grasp, right away, what each of those things meant. I would
come to know them, one by one, in my own body, later. I no longer had to
stand behind Pikra in the ration line. There was a share now that came
out under my own name. My name could be on the door of the house — I
wasn't putting it there yet, but that it could be there at all made a
difference.

*I felt something strange, each time one of those things happened. I
would be lying to say I wasn't happy. But there was something larger than
happiness underneath it. I didn't know, then, exactly what it was. I
know now. It was the feeling of owing something. I hadn't earned that
number. I had received it. And what's received always carries the face
of the one who gave it.*

---

Before he closed the screen, I asked one more thing.

"…How long is this number valid for?"

"…The same as any other standing," the administrator said. "It's
reassessed at the regular renewal. Except—"

He paused.

"…Except what?"

"…A transferred value is calculated a little differently," the
administrator said. "It wasn't built up from your own history. How you
live in this city from here on will matter more to how long this number
holds than it would otherwise."

Something went cold in me at that.

"…So this is," I said, "a number I have to keep proving?"

The administrator looked at me. For the first time, something cautious
crossed his face.

"…Honestly," he said, "yes. But that's true of every standing in this
city. You're not uniquely disadvantaged."

*I took that for comfort, at the time. I know now it wasn't comfort at
all — it was simply a fact. The administrator hadn't meant to console me.
He had only stated it precisely. Everyone in this city is always
proving themselves. I had only known that since I was born. Lytron was
only just learning it, that day.*

---

We left the office.

Pikra was waiting outside for me.

"…How was it," Pikra asked.

"…It wasn't zero," I said. No other words came to me.

Pikra nodded. She neither smiled nor wept. She only took my hand once,
and let it go.

"…Come on," Pikra said. "To the field."

"…Today too?"

"…I know you want to go out," Pikra said. "You don't have to, anymore."

I said nothing to that.

*That day I went out to a field I no longer had to go out to. Before,
I'd gone because I had to. From that day, I went because I wanted to. I
couldn't have explained that difference in full, then. I still can't
explain it completely, even now. I only know it appeared, very quietly,
somewhere inside me.*

---

Lytron was at the field.

He watched us approach and didn't come toward us. He just stood where he
was. I knew he was keeping his distance on purpose.

"…Did you confirm it?" he asked first.

"…Yes."

"…Not zero?"

"…No," I said. "Not zero."

He nodded. It wasn't quite a smile, but very close to one.

"…Good," he said. That was all.

I felt something more should have followed that short word. He said
nothing more. I asked nothing more either.

*I understood only later why he hadn't come closer. He didn't want to
show me what he'd lost, standing right in front of what I'd gained. On
the day I received, he didn't want to bring what he'd given up along
with him. I think that was a consideration all his own. One no one had
asked of him.*

"…This is strange," I said. I held it back and finally said it. "I
should be happy today. My heart keeps turning wrong instead."

"…Why?"

"…You gave it," I said. "I received. You gave it. That keeps catching at
me."

Lytron glanced toward the field for a moment.

"…Is that strange?" he asked, back. "I think it's the natural way of it,
actually. Giving and receiving is supposed to be that — one gives, one
receives. If both give, that's not giving and receiving anymore, that's
—"

He stopped. He seemed unable to find the right word.

"…That's just standing still," he finished. "Neither one moving."

I felt I understood what he meant, and didn't fully. I understand a
little more now. I think, that day, he wanted to tell me not to feel
sorry for receiving. But he couldn't say it directly, and said it
sideways instead. The moment was awkward for him too.

---

The next day, I went to the ration office alone for the first time.

Before, I'd always gone behind Pikra. I had no share of my own; we ate
from what was given to her. That day I stood in line under my own name.

The clerk called my number. The call was unfamiliar enough that I
answered a beat late.

"…Miss Xene," the clerk called again.

"…Yes." I answered, only then.

He entered my number into the screen. Checked something for a moment,
then handed me a sack.

"…This season's share."

I took the sack. It wasn't heavy. But the weight in my hands felt
strangely unfamiliar. I had always been the one sharing in what someone
else received. This was the first time I'd received something with my
own hands, under my own name.

*I stood there a long time, holding that sack. There was someone in line
behind me, and I still couldn't move. Someone nudged me lightly on the
back before I came to myself.*

"…I'm sorry," I said, stepping aside.

The clerk said nothing more. He didn't look at me specially, didn't look
at me strangely. He simply called the next person.

*I have never been so glad of anyone's indifference. I hadn't wanted the
man to recognize me. I had only wanted to be treated the same way as
everyone else. That day, for the first time, I was. Not specially at
all.*

On the way home I met "him." He was putting away his tools at the edge
of the field.

"…I received something for the first time today," I said, holding up
the sack a little. "In my own name."

He stopped his hands and looked at me.

"…How does it feel," he asked.

I thought for a moment.

"…Strange," I said. "Good, but strange."

"…Why strange?"

"…It's not because I did anything," I said. "I just received it. Someone
paid for it instead."

He nodded. And went back to his work.

"…There are people who find it strange to receive," he said. "People who
think it isn't real unless they earned it themselves."

"…Am I one of them?"

"…A little," he said. It didn't sound unkind, said with a smile.

"…Is it wrong to find it strange?"

"…Finding it strange and refusing it are different things," he said.
"You can find it strange and still receive it. That's actually the most
honest kind of receiving there is."

I didn't fully understand that, that day. I came to understand it, a
little at a time, over the years that followed.

*I remember that moment still. He didn't teach me something new that
day. He only put into words something I had already been feeling. I
think it was moments like that, stacked one on another, that led me to
write this record.*

---

That night, I took out the notice again.

Name, number, date, standing. That was all that was written on that one
sheet of paper.

*I felt, that night, how thin that paper truly was. A single sheet. And
behind that thin sheet lay one signature, a few seconds, and a number a
man had kept his whole life. I folded that paper and put it against my
chest. From that day I carried it with me always.*

*Even now, years later, I still take it out sometimes. The color has
faded further. The words remain. Name, number, date, standing. I know
that not one of those four was made by me. My name was given by my
parents; my number was set by the Roll; the date was only the day it
happened to be; the number was given to me by someone else. There is
nothing of my own doing anywhere on this paper. I think this is the way
this city let me in.*
