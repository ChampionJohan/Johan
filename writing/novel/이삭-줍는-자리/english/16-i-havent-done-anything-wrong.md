# Chapter 16. I Haven't Done Anything Wrong

> _Previously_ — Xene met Lytron. He was not unkind to her. He simply
> couldn't understand why she came every day to a place with nothing in
> it. Pikra said his was a high-standing family.

This is a chapter I wasn't there for.

Most of what's written here, I didn't live through myself. Some of it I heard, much later, from Lytron directly. Some of it I learned only sideways, while Mena was researching this city's records. I hesitated a long time over whether to write it at all. Setting down something I never saw felt like it went against how I learned to keep this record.

*Mena once said to me, "If you only write what you saw, you end up with a story that has only the person who saw it in it. But this world isn't a place where only one person lives." This is the chapter where I first felt the truth of that.*

So this chapter isn't my memory. It's a day I pieced together afterward. But nothing in it is invented. I made up none of the conversation. What I set down here is only what Lytron told me himself, and what Mena confirmed as fact.

---

Lytron's morning began earlier than mine.

His house stood in the innermost part of the city, the district farthest from the field. I passed through that neighborhood once, much later, and remember it — wide streets, quiet. Every house had a high wall, and behind every wall, the sound of running water. It was a neighborhood with no reason to conserve it.

Lytron woke before dawn and checked his terminal first thing. Reading through the overnight zone reports was always his first task. Loss rate, machine uptime, labor assignment. If the numbers looked like yesterday's, he was at ease. If anything was even slightly off, he went looking for the cause.

*I was a little surprised, hearing this. I'd assumed Lytron woke each morning and started his day at leisure — the son of a high-standing family, after all. He didn't. He too had numbers to check every morning. The only difference between us was that when those numbers went bad, he didn't go hungry for it.*

His house had many rooms, I was told — though I never learned exactly how many. Lytron himself didn't seem to have counted. I found that strange. Our room was one room, and I had every corner of it memorized. A house with several rooms would have some that went mostly unused, and I could only imagine what it would feel like not to need to count them at all.

There was a small irrigation channel running through the yard. It was ornamental — not dug out of any need, but simply because it was pleasant to look at. Hearing that, I went quiet for a while. Back at our settlement, people had come to blows over a single water channel. Here, letting water simply run for no reason at all was enough to make a yard.

---

At breakfast, his father would ask for the day's report.

"…Yesterday's loss rate."

"…0.02 percent. No change."

"…Good work."

That was often the whole of the conversation. Lytron's father wasn't a man of many words. Though on days the numbers turned bad, the words multiplied.

*I've imagined that short exchange many times over. What "good work" must have meant to Lytron. I've never once heard those words myself, so at first I felt a pang of envy. I see it a little differently now. That phrase was praise and standard at once — it also meant that on a day the numbers turned bad, that phrase wouldn't come.*

---

Lytron's family had done harvest management work for three generations.

His great-grandfather's generation had already had a hand in this city's fields, I was told. The family's standing score had been built up over those generations. Not gained by theft. Not gained by deceiving anyone. Earned steadily, over a long time, by doing the work well.

*I write this part carefully. I don't want anyone reading this book to hate Lytron for it. That family really had worked hard. Generation after generation rising early, generation after generation reading the numbers closely, generation after generation trying not to make mistakes. They hadn't reached their position by doing anything wrong. They had simply done well.*

*But I also came to understand, later, that this "doing well" stood in exact opposition to what was left for someone else. In a structure where one side's doing better always meant less remaining for the other, I doubt that family had ever once thought of it in those terms.*

Lytron had an older brother. His brother already managed a different zone and lived independently. The two weren't on bad terms — they ate together on holidays, sometimes walked the fields together. What the brother usually told him came down to the same thing: *Keep the numbers right, and everything else falls into place.*

Lytron believed it. It had been true for him, so far, exactly as he'd been told.

*I thought about this a long time. "Keep the numbers right and everything falls into place" — that was true for those two brothers. In their world, it really was. The trouble is that a world where that sentence isn't true was laid directly over the same city, the same ground. Writing this chapter, I came to see most clearly of all how those two worlds could sit on the same soil and almost never see each other.*

---

Lytron was seventeen. A year older than me.

Already, at that age, he was handling zone management support work. The Roll set children of high-standing families to real work early. The reason was simple: they would sit in bigger seats later, so best to learn it now.

Lytron didn't dislike the work. He was good at it, actually. He had a feel for reading numbers, and he was quick at adjusting machine placement. His superiors trusted him. His reputation among his peers wasn't bad either — conscientious, courteous, never one to treat anyone carelessly, people said.

*Hearing this reputation, I didn't believe it at first. But over time I came to understand it was true. Lytron really was that sort of person. What I'm trying to do in this book isn't to erase that fact. It's the opposite — to let it stand as it is.*

Lytron had a few friends, all children of similarly placed families. On their days off, they gathered and shared meals together. I once heard the list of dishes at one of those gatherings — it was more than I'd eaten in a whole year. Lytron didn't think of it as anything special. Just what he usually ate.

Among his friends, he was sometimes teased for being too conscientious, too meticulous. *You like numbers more than people,* they'd joke. Lytron only laughed at it. He never denied it. He had no reason to deny it.

*I think this is the most important part of this chapter. Lytron was someone who could hear that he liked numbers more than people, and laugh. Because he didn't think it made him a bad person. I think that's the face of harmlessness. A bad person bristles at a remark like that. A harmless person laughs it off. Because he feels no reason to bristle.*

---

That afternoon, Lytron made his rounds of the zone. It was the day he met me.

The rounds followed a set order: check machine condition, check zone boundaries, check labor assignments. Somewhere in that order was the corner zone. Since it fell under Article 9, it was on the list of things to be checked regularly.

*So the fact that I met Lytron that day wasn't a coincidence, I learned later. It was a coincidence, in a sense — that I was there that day, and that it happened to be inspection day, was a coincidence. But the round itself wasn't. It was his job. I had been in that spot at the same hour every day for twelve days, and Lytron passed through it following a fixed order. Two repetitions simply collided.*

Lytron logged me in that day's inspection record like this: "One unregistered individual within zone, repeated access confirmed. No irregularities."

*I saw that sentence later. "No irregularities." I laughed, seeing it. Whether I laughed because it was sad, or because it was absurd, I still don't quite know. That day had been the biggest thing that happened to me — and in his record it was a day with nothing irregular in it at all.*

Once the round ended, Lytron went back to the office and drew up a summary of what he'd checked that day. Loss rate summaries by zone, machine uptime, changes in labor assignment. That report was written every single day, and every single day it went up to his superiors. Lytron liked that work. He said it felt good when everything fit together exactly, when not a single number was off.

"…What's good about that?" I asked him once, later.

"…Hard to explain." Lytron said. "When something fits together exactly, it feels like I've done something well."

I said nothing to that. For him, it must really have been an achievement. I don't think that achievement was a bad thing in itself. Only that it seemed strange to me that I had been standing right where that achievement completed itself.

---

That evening, back home, Lytron said a few words to his father about the day — the round, the machines. Among it, a brief mention of me.

"…There's someone who comes every day, out by the corner."

"…Unregistered?"

"…Yes."

"…Report it if it's something. Otherwise, don't worry about it." his father said. "That's just what that spot is for."

And that was the end of it. In that room, I'd been a story that lasted a few seconds.

*I never feel angry, picturing that scene. I know that sounds strange to hear. But it's true. His father said nothing unkind. Even "don't worry about it" wasn't said out of dislike. It was simply that he'd judged it an unimportant item in his son's day. I think the judgment was wrong. But I don't want to paint the man who made it as a villain.*

---

That night, Lytron went over the day's round record once more in his room. He said he'd paused, briefly, at the entry for my access log.

He told me this much later.

"…I felt strange that night." he said.

"…Strange how?" I asked. Much later.

"…Hard to explain." Lytron said. "Looking at your access log, it suddenly hit me — for the first time — that there was a person behind that number. Before then it had just been an entry."

"…And then?"

"…And then no conclusion came of it." Lytron said. "I just felt that, for a while, and then forgot about it the next day, reading some other report."

*I heard this confession much later still. By then a great deal had already changed. I thought it belonged in this chapter. Lytron felt something, very briefly, that night. And then he forgot it. I don't hold that forgetting against him. If I'd been his age, with that many reports to check in a day, I probably would have forgotten too.*

That forgetting wasn't especially cruel. After that night, Lytron woke the same way, made his rounds the same way, wrote his reports the same way. Nothing changed. And that nothing-changing, I learned later, is the most common thing in this city. Most people feel something, carry that feeling through a single night, and then set it down the next day because there's work to do. It isn't malice. A day simply passes that way.

*I don't want to write this as a bad thing. If I'd been able to live that way, I would have too. Only I never got the chance to live that way. My days had nothing else filling them that I could forget under. So I had no choice but to return, every day, to the same spot in my mind. Maybe that was the biggest difference between him and me. He had the room to forget. I didn't.*

---

I want to close this chapter with something Lytron said to himself. This too I heard later.

Around that time, he sometimes asked himself: had he done something wrong. The answer was always the same.

"…I haven't done anything wrong."

That was true. Lytron truly hadn't done anything wrong. He hadn't taken anything from anyone. He hadn't deceived anyone. He hadn't broken the law. He hadn't lied. He had only done his own work, in his own place, conscientiously.

*I'll write this sentence again more than once in this book. For now I'll only set down this much: that it was true. And that even while it was true, there could still be a day when someone like me sat with two grains of wheat in her palm and had to laugh. That both of these things could be true at the same time — I held onto that longer than anything else while writing this book.*

I rewrote this chapter several times. When I first wrote it, I painted Lytron a little worse than this. I thought it would land the book's point more clearly. Reading it back, I saw it was a lie. In the end I cut all of it and settled for only this much.

I tried, more than once while writing this chapter, to make myself hate Lytron. I couldn't manage it. However hard I searched for a place to put that hatred, all I found there was a seventeen-year-old conscientiously checking his numbers. Hating that person wasn't different from hating conscientiousness itself.

*I know that's exactly not what this book is aimed at. This book isn't aimed at Lytron. It's aimed at the comfort he never once thought to question. And I am someone who never had that comfort — and so didn't even know it was comfort, when I had none.*
