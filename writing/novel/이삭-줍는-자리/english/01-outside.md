# Chapter 1. Outside

Outside, there's no such word as standing.

I didn't know the word until I was sixteen. I'd heard it — people from
the city used it sometimes. But I didn't know it was a word you
attached to a person. I thought it was a word you attached to things.
This wheat has standing, that wheat doesn't. Something like that.

By the time I learned it was a word for people, I was already standing
at the city gate.

---

Our settlement had no name.

It was never a problem. A name is only useful when someone far away
comes looking for you, and no one ever came looking for us from far
away.

There were twenty-two houses. I could count all twenty-two, and I knew
the name of everyone who lived in them. I knew whose door got knocked
on at night, and I had a rough idea of who wouldn't make it through
winter.

*I can still count those twenty-two houses in order, even now. For a
while after I came into the city, whenever I couldn't sleep, I counted
them. It was the only list I had.*

We planted a little wheat, kept a few goats, drew water from the
channel. When the harvest failed, we went hungry. Hunger was a bad
thing, but it wasn't a strange thing.

The strange thing was the city.

They said no one in the city ever went hungry. I didn't believe it for
a long time. How could no one go hungry in a year the wheat failed?
Wheat was something heaven gave, or didn't.

*Later I learned it was true. In the city, truly, no one goes hungry.
And I learned why that's a frightening thing to say.*

People from the city didn't look at us with pity. That surprised me.
I'd expected them to feel sorry for us, but they didn't. They looked
at us the way you'd look at **something that needed explaining**.

Once, one of them stood looking at our field for a long time, then
said:

"…Why do you spill so much here?"

I didn't know what he meant.

"…You drop it while you're reaping. There, and there." He pointed.
"Add it all up and that's a sack, easy."

"…We don't pick up what falls," I said.

"…Why not?"

I couldn't answer. I'd never once thought about why. You didn't pick
up what fell. Everyone did it that way. My grandmother had done it
that way, and hers before her, probably.

He tilted his head and wrote something down.

*I didn't wonder for a long time what he wrote. Now I can guess. He
probably wrote: loss rate.*

---

I entered that household when I was fourteen.

Outside, marriage comes early. You need hands to work, and you never
know when someone might be gone. Some people marry for love, but
mostly those two reasons come first.

The household had a mother and two sons. The father, they said, had
died some years before. I married the elder son, and not long after,
the younger married a girl from another house.

The mother's name was Glyke.

*I said that name several times a day for two years. I didn't know it
would change into another name. I thought names didn't change. That's
how it is outside. Outside, no one ever asks to have their name
changed.*

Glyke wasn't a talkative person. But she was a person who laughed. She
laughed even on days with nothing to laugh about. At first I thought
it was forced, but living with her, I learned it wasn't. She really
did find things funny.

There was a year we lost the whole crop. A late frost came. In three
days the field turned black.

I sat at the edge of the field and cried. I was fourteen.

Glyke came and sat beside me. After a long while, she said,

"…This year's wheat had a temper on it."

I looked at her, not understanding.

"…Everyone else waited, and it came out early, all on its own," Glyke
said. "Being in a hurry never pays, anywhere."

And she laughed.

*That was the first time I laughed. Someone had once told me you go
ugly when you laugh through tears, but Glyke never said that. She just
laughed with me.*

*I think it was that laugh, not the marriage, that made me one of
that household.*

My own family's house was two doors down. So even married, I hadn't
gone far.

My mother said this, the day I married:

"…That mother is a good person."

"…I know."

"…What you know and what I know are different," my mother said.
"I've seen that woman laugh after losing something."

That day, I let the remark pass.

*Two years later I would remember it again. And a few months after
that, I would watch the laugh stop. My mother said she'd seen the
woman laugh after losing something. She never told me how much a
person has to lose before it stops.*

---

I won't write my husband's name here.

There's a reason I won't. I'll set it down later. For now I'll only
note this: in this record, I will not call him by name, not once.

He had big hands. So big that whatever he held looked small. A water
jar looked small in them. A sickle looked small in them.

He spoke slowly. He'd say a word, wait a long while, then say another.
At first I couldn't stand the gaps and kept filling them myself. Then
at some point I just started waiting.

*I learned waiting from him. I became a person who waits, for the rest
of my life. A person who watches nothing fall in the corner of a
field and still waits for next year. I know where that came from.*

We lived together two years.

Some people would call two years short. I don't. Two years is enough
to know a person completely — which side he slept on, what sound woke
him, which finger moved first when he was angry.

I knew him completely.

In the autumn of our first married year, he taught me to use a sickle.
Until then, in my own family, I'd been the one who bound sheaves, not
the one who cut.

He watched me for a while, then said,

"…You're doing it too clean."

"…What's wrong with that?"

"…Clean like that, your wrist gives out," he said. "Spill a little."

"…That's a waste."

He waited a long while before answering. As he always did.

"…What you spill isn't wasted."

"…Then what is it?"

Another long while passed.

"…It's left behind."

*I took it for a joke, that day. I thought he just meant to spare my
wrist. He was good at that — saying something that sounded like
nothing, that turned out, later, to be everything.*

*Two years later I would use that line again, standing in front of a
field where nothing fell. By then I knew he hadn't been joking. There
was still, in that world, a person who knew that spilling and leaving
something behind were the same act.*

---

The day he died was a day nothing happened.

That's the strangest part. I wish there'd been something — an omen,
some unusual word. There wasn't.

He went out in the morning. A section of the channel bank had
collapsed, he said, and needed fixing. Three of them went — him, his
brother, and a neighbor.

At midday the neighbor came running back alone.

*I won't set down what happened after that. I could. I'm choosing not
to. This record isn't being written for that.*

*I'll set down only this. After that day, I could never again say the
words a day nothing happened. There is no such day. There's only the
evening, when you learn what the day held.*

---

His brother lived four more months.

The injury from the bank never healed. At first it seemed like it
would. By the second month he was walking. By the third he was back in
bed, and by the fourth he died.

So this is how Glyke lost them. A husband, two sons. In order, across
several years, one at a time.

*I used to think losing people one at a time was easier than losing
them all at once. I don't think so now. Losing them all at once, you
fall apart once. Losing them one at a time, you have to get up again,
every time. Getting up is harder than falling.*

Glyke got up all three times.

The third time took a long while.

After the brother died, his wife stayed on with us for a while. Her
name was Daphne. A year younger than me.

The three of us were left in that house. Three women. Two of us had
belonged to that household for less than two years.

At night we didn't talk much. Not for lack of things to say — I think
it was that once we started, we wouldn't know where to stop.

Once Daphne woke crying. She bit the blanket to keep from making a
sound.

Glyke sat up. But she didn't go to her. She just sat there.

At the time I wondered why she didn't go.

*Now I know. It wasn't that she couldn't. It was that she chose not
to. Glyke knew that if she went to her, Daphne would stop crying. And
she knew that night was not a night that should be stopped.*

*Everyone in that household knew things like this — when to reach out
a hand and when not to. I learned it here. In the city, no one knew
it.*

---

It was some two months after the brother died.

A person came from the city. Someone who came twice a year or so, to
do what he called a Roll renewal check. Even living outside, if you
were originally from the city your name stayed on the Roll, and it had
to be confirmed the name was still valid.

That was the first time I properly heard the word Roll.

He took out a device and entered Glyke's name. He looked at something
for a long while. Then he nodded, wrote something down, and left.

Glyke watched his back for a long time.

"…What did he say?" I asked.

"…He didn't say anything," Glyke said.

"…So it's fine, then?"

Glyke looked at me. And for the first time, she didn't laugh.

"…No," Glyke said. "The number went down."

I didn't know what she meant.

"…What number?"

"…Mine," Glyke said.

*I really didn't understand what that meant, then. People had
died — why would the number of the one left behind go down? The ones
who died were them. Why was Glyke the one being cut?*

*Now I know. That's how this world counts a person. The Roll doesn't
count a person alone. It counts a person **together with what belongs
to them.** So much for a husband, so much for a son, so much for
hands that work. So Glyke hadn't only lost three people. The number
attached to those three had vanished along with them.*

*The grief came from outside. The arithmetic came from the city.*

That night I asked Glyke,

"…What happens when the number goes down?"

"…Nothing happens," Glyke said.

"…Then why worry?"

Glyke looked at me.

"…That nothing happens is exactly the problem," Glyke said. "If
someone takes something from you, at least you can say you were
robbed. When someone just gives you nothing, there's nowhere to say
it."

I didn't understand.

"…What's the difference?"

Glyke thought for a moment, then said,

"…When you married into this house, what did I give you?"

"…A great deal."

"…I gave it because I had something to give," Glyke said. "Once I
have nothing to give, I become a person who's done nothing for you.
Not a person who's wronged you. A person who's done nothing. But from
where you're standing, receiving, it looks exactly the same."

*I would think back on this countless times after I entered the city.
The Roll never wrongs anyone. It simply does nothing. Glyke already
knew that, before she ever went back in.*

---

That winter, Glyke sat a great deal.

Not that she stopped working. She did everything that needed doing.
But when the work was done, she sat in the doorway and looked in one
direction. Always the same direction.

I knew which direction it was. Toward the city.

I never said anything. Asking would have meant she had to answer, and
I didn't want to make her.

One evening, Glyke spoke first.

"…Xene."

"…Yes."

"…I've been thinking about where I want to be buried."

I set down what I was holding.

"…Don't say things like that."

"…No, listen," Glyke said. "This isn't a sad thing to say. It's a
thing you say when you're getting ready."

I couldn't say anything.

*I didn't believe, then, that it was a thing you say when you're
getting ready. I believe it now. Glyke really was getting ready. I
don't think she understood how frightening it is, to be near someone
who is getting ready.*

Glyke looked toward the city again.

"…My people are all there," Glyke said. "All three of them."

I knew what she meant. All three were in the city cemetery. Even
someone who died outside, if they were city-born, was carried back
and buried there. It was something like an old agreement between the
outside and the city.

"…I'm the only one left here," Glyke said.

And she laughed. This time she laughed, but it was a different laugh
than before.

*I remember that laugh. It was the last time Glyke ever laughed as
Glyke.*

*After that, she asked to have her name changed.*
