# Chapter 19. The One Who Gleans Beside Me

> _Previously_ — Xene began to break the ice with the hired hands. She
> got to know Damia. There was a quiet man there too. She never learned
> his name. She thought nothing of it.

It was about ten days later.

Through those ten days, stopping by the storehouse before heading to the corner had become my fixed routine. Greet Damia, exchange a few words, nod to the quiet man, go on to the corner. That order almost never changed. Even the weather stayed much the same — hard sun, dry wind, the smell of dust every evening.

But that day, the quiet man was heading not toward the storehouse but toward the corner.

His assignment must have changed, I thought. That was all I made of it.

*I haven't written those ten days in any detail here. There was little enough to write. The fact that every day was much like the last is, I think, the most accurate picture of what my days were like then. I never found that repetition tedious. Being able to repeat at all was, itself, a rare thing, after I'd left the outside.*

---

The corner was never officially a work zone. But now and then it needed tending — clearing the ditch, straightening the marker, redriving a boundary stake. When there was work like that, one of the hired hands would be assigned.

That day, it was him.

I sat as always at one side of the corner and began sifting the dirt. He worked over by the ditch. Our spots didn't overlap. I didn't pay him any particular mind and kept at what I was doing.

*I don't remember that day as a special one. Looking back now, this whole chapter is special. But to me, that day, it was just an ordinary day. Nothing different, except that there happened to be one more person there.*

At one point, in the middle of leveling the dirt, he reached a hand toward me. I flinched. But what he was pointing at wasn't me — it was the ground by my foot.

A grain lay half-buried in the dirt beneath me. I'd have walked right past it.

"…There." he said, briefly.

I bent and picked it up. Before I could say thank you, he'd already turned back to the ditch.

*I thought, that day, that he simply had a sharp eye. Someone who handles dirt every day might well spot a single grain before anyone else. I thought nothing more than that.*

---

Sifting the dirt for a long while, I noticed at some point that he had drifted a little closer to me.

He hadn't said anything. The distance had simply narrowed naturally, as he tended the ditch. I felt uneasy at first. But he never looked at me — just kept at his own work. I settled again and went back to mine.

About half a day passed that way, and near where he'd been working, I began finding more grains.

More than usual.

---

I didn't think it strange.

Working a ditch turns up dirt — that was only natural, I thought. Whatever had been buried had come up with the churned soil. That seemed the obvious explanation. I believed it. I had good enough reason to.

*I'm careful writing this part. I didn't notice a thing that day. Truly, I didn't. Whoever reads this chapter later will already know. I want to say this to them in advance: I didn't know, then. If I write that I knew, that would be a lie.*

That evening, I gathered a little more than usual. More than two cupped palms. Even after sharing with Pikra, a few grains were left over.

"…Bit more today." Pikra said.

"…Must be from working the ditch." I said.

"…Must be." Pikra said.

We didn't press it further. Pikra wrapped the extra in a small cloth and set it aside for the next morning's share. We were learning, around then, to stretch a day's worth over two.

---

He was at the corner the next day too.

I wondered if his assignment had changed again. I didn't know whether that was common or not. I was curious, but I didn't ask. He was still as quiet as ever. He did his work. I did mine.

That day too, more grains than usual turned up near where I sifted — especially near where he'd been.

That was when I first thought, briefly, that it was strange. But the thought didn't hold. I was hungry, and a hungry person doesn't examine strange things too closely. I just gathered them.

*I've gone back over that moment many times since. Why didn't I press further? The answer is simple. Nothing good could have come of it. If it wasn't a coincidence, asking might have made him stop. I wasn't calculating that carefully at the time. But some instinct told me that looking too closely at a good thing breaks it.*

---

He still hardly spoke.

If I asked something, he gave short answers. "…Yes." "…No." Rarely more than that. At first I found it uncomfortable. A few days on, I found it a relief instead. There was no pressure to keep a conversation going.

"…Do you come here often?" I asked him once.

"…Sometimes." he said.

"…What brought you here today?"

"…Fixing the ditch."

That was the whole conversation. He went back to his work.

*Reading those short answers again now, I smile. There's no lie in any of them. "Sometimes" was true. "Fixing the ditch" was true. He never once lied to me. He simply never told me what I hadn't asked.*

---

Around then, Damia said something to me.

"…You're looking better these days." she said, when we crossed paths near the storehouse.

"…Am I."

"…Eating well?"

I didn't know what to say, so I just smiled. It wasn't as if I was eating well. But it was true things had improved.

"…Corner working out for you lately?" Damia asked again.

"…A bit better these days."

Damia nodded and went back to her work without pressing further. I watched her go and felt that her idle question had made the day a little lighter. Someone being curious about my day, at all, was a rare thing for me then.

*I think Damia's question was just a passing pleasantry. We weren't close enough yet for her to press into my circumstances. I didn't say more than that either. I didn't think it rude, at the time. Some questions are complete just by being asked, without needing an answer in full.*

---

A few days on, I noticed a difference between the days he came and the days he didn't.

The days he came were, on the whole, better. The days he didn't were like before — very little, or nothing at all.

I put it down to the assignment. Days the corner needed tending, things got more disturbed, so naturally more turned up. I repeated that explanation to myself more than once. Not because I was trying not to doubt it. At the time, it really did seem the most plausible one.

*I understand now that it wasn't that I tried not to doubt. It's more accurate to say I felt no need to doubt at all. It was enough, at the time, that my days had gotten a little better. Why they'd gotten better was a question for later. For a hungry person, later questions always get pushed back.*

---

Pikra seemed to notice too.

One evening, she paused for a moment over what I'd laid out — more grain than usual. She said nothing, and went back to dividing it.

I saw that pause. I knew she'd seen it too.

"…Better lately." Pikra said, as she divided it. Not a prying tone.

"…Yes."

"…Good."

That was all. Pikra never asked why it had gotten better. I never explained. We divided the grain in half that evening, as always, ground the fragments and shared them in water, as always.

*Looking back on that evening now, I wonder why Pikra didn't ask. Whether she didn't know, or knew and chose not to ask. I still can't say for certain. Pikra was someone who knew when not to ask, her whole life through. Whichever it was, that evening, she chose not to.*

---

Once, an unusual amount of grain had gathered around where he'd been working.

I was gathering it when I happened to look up. He'd already moved off to another spot. Not looking at me.

"…There's a lot here." I said.

He glanced my way. Only briefly.

"…Is there." he said.

His expression stayed the same. Not startled, not concealing anything. Just — *is there.* That was all.

*I can still picture that expression exactly. An expression holding nothing at all. I read it, at the time, as indifference. I read it differently now. It wasn't indifference — it was the face of someone who had decided not to answer. I couldn't tell the difference then.*

---

I learned something from him, too.

At first I just brushed my fingers over the dirt carelessly. Then one day I happened to watch the way he sifted it. Fingers laid flat instead of stiffly upright, sweeping like a rake. Done that way, the grains caught against the fingertips far more easily.

From that day I started sifting the same way. Within a few days my hands had taken to it. My gathering grew a little faster.

"…This works better." I told him once.

"…Yes." he said. Nothing in his manner suggested he'd taught me anything. I didn't make a show of having learned it either. We simply both became people who sifted that way.

*I still use that technique now. Fingers laid flat, sweeping the dirt like a rake. For years I never had occasion to explain to anyone who taught it to me. Some memories don't need explaining. There are things a hand simply knows.*

---

Once, I nearly saw something.

Looking up, I caught his hand moving from near his waistband toward the dirt. It was a very brief motion. I didn't quite make out what it was. The next moment he was already gathering his tools, his hands empty.

"…Drop something?" I asked.

"…No." he said.

I accepted the answer without question. There was no particular reason to doubt it. A person's hands move around, working. I thought of that moment once, that night, and forgot it the next day.

*I'm setting this scene down again now. I nearly saw something that time. Truly, only nearly. I didn't see it. Writing this book, I didn't want to inflate what I might have seen. I confirmed nothing. Not writing something unconfirmed as though it were confirmed — I believe that's one of the principles this record has to keep.*

---

Around then, working beside him at the corner came to feel natural to me.

We'd never made any arrangement. It was simply that he came to be assigned to the corner more and more often, and I was there every day regardless, and at some point we became two people doing separate work in the same spot.

We still spoke little. But the silence stopped being uncomfortable. I found that strange. With Damia, silence felt like something that needed filling. Not with him. Quiet could just stay quiet, and it was fine.

*I still don't know what to call this feeling. Comfort seems too large a word. I'll only set it down as this much: that there was now someone beside whom it wasn't strange to say nothing at all.*

---

That evening, I told Pikra a little more about him.

"…There's someone who's been at the corner with me lately."

"…A hired hand?"

"…Yes. Doesn't say much, but he just works quietly nearby."

Pikra looked at me a moment.

"…Is he all right?"

"…Yes. He doesn't seem like a bad person."

Pikra nodded. She didn't ask anything further.

That night, lying down, I went back over those last few days. Nothing much had changed. I still went out every day, still got my hands into the dirt, still shared with Pikra in the evening. Only now, somewhere in that day, there was one more quiet person. He'd said hardly a word, but those few words had lifted a little of the day's weight.

*Looking back on this stretch of time, I think it matters that I didn't feel, then, as though I were gaining anything remarkable. I only felt that the day was a little less lonely. I never wanted more than that, never suspected more than that. I don't know now what kind of beginning that might have been. At the time it was just one day after another.*

*I didn't mention that night, either, that there'd been more grain around his spot again. It didn't seem like something worth saying. I still believed, then, that it was only the ditch work turning things up. There was no need to put into words a thing I already believed.*

*Writing this chapter now, I think it's fortunate, in a way, that I didn't know then. If I had known, I wouldn't have been able to accept it. I believed it was chance, so I simply took it. And that, I think, was exactly what he wanted.*
