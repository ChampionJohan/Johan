# Chapter 25. Someone Passing Through

> _Previously_ — On the day the rain stopped the machines, grain was left
> in the corner of the field for the first time. I filled a sack that day.
> And I understood it was the sky's doing, not the city's mercy.

The first time I saw her, it wasn't at the corner.

It was at the well near the gate. I was on my way to draw water, and she
was sitting there writing something. A flat wooden board across her
knees, paper spread on top of it, a pen in her hand.

It wasn't the first time I'd seen someone write. Most city people used
handheld terminals — small enough to fit a palm, letters arranging
themselves at a touch. But someone writing by hand, on paper, I had
hardly seen in this city at all.

*I thought, at the time, that she was strange. Paper was a precious
thing here. Writing by hand was slow, too. And yet she sat there as if
she had nowhere else to be.*

Her clothes didn't look like a city person's either. City people mostly
wore clean clothes — the lower your standing, the less true that was.
Hers were neither. Worn, but neat. Worn from long use, not from
carelessness.

At her feet sat a thick leather case, bound with cord in several places.
One of the cords was a different color from the rest, as if it had
recently been replaced.

She looked up. Our eyes met.

"…Come for water?" she asked.

"…Yes," I said.

"…There's room here. You can share it."

I set down my water jar and sat beside her. I had no particular reason.
There was something in her voice that put a person off their guard. I
hadn't sat this easily beside a stranger since coming into the city.

---

"…What are you writing?" I asked.

She tilted the paper slightly so I could see. I read the letters. A few
names, and beside each one a short line.

One line caught my eye.

**"One sack a day at the corner. Only on rainy days."**

I knew at once it was about me.

"…Me?" I asked.

"…Yes," she said. "I saw you a few days ago. Filling a sack at the
corner."

"…Why write that down?"

She thought a moment, then set her pen down.

"…That's my work," she said. "My name is Mena."

I thought I'd heard the name somewhere before. But I couldn't place it
then.

*I had no idea, in that moment, how many times that name would come to
my lips later on. I only thought I'd met a strange woman writing
something on paper by a well.*

"…I'm Xene," I said.

"…I know," Mena said. "I saw it on the gate registry."

I was a little startled.

"…You look at that too?"

"…I do. It's my work," Mena said. "Who's come into this city, and with
what standing. I keep all of that."

"…Why?"

"…I'll tell you later." Mena smiled. "For now, go and get your water.
It's a waste to let that jar sit empty."

I laughed. It was strange how right that sounded.

---

When I came back with the water, Mena was still there. Watching someone
else now — an old man coming out through the gate. He had a pack on his
back and a slow step. Mena watched him a long while, then wrote
something down.

I sat beside her again and watched her hand move. Not fast. Not hurried
either. Just certain. Each stroke made a small, steady sound as the pen
tip pressed into the paper. The sound never varied.

"…You don't know that man either," I said.

"…I don't know most of them," Mena said. "I'm not from this city."

"…Then where are you from?"

She smiled a little at the question.

"…Nowhere, really," she said. "I go between cities. I don't stay long
in any one place."

"…Doesn't it wear you out? Walking all the time?"

"…It does," Mena said. But her voice didn't sound worn out. "But if you
stay too long in one place, there's something you stop being able to
see."

"…What's that?"

"…How the same thing happens differently, and also the same, in city
after city," Mena said. "If you only ever live in one, you think
everything that happens there belongs only to that city."

*I didn't fully understand what she meant, not then. I had lived outside
the city walls before. But I had never met anyone who moved between
cities the way she did. Later I would learn that what she meant, that
day, had to do with this city's Roll.*

The old man passed through the gate and vanished into the city. Mena
watched until his back disappeared from sight.

"…Do you know what'll happen to him?" I asked.

"…No," Mena said. "I'm not the kind of person who knows that."

"…Then what kind of person are you?"

"…The kind who knows he passed through here today," Mena said. "That's
all I know. But that's enough."

---

A few days later, I met Mena again on my way to the corner.

She was standing at the edge of the field, the wooden board still in
her hand. This time she was watching the whole field — the machines'
paths, the workers moving sacks, the men weighing grain at the scales.

"…What are you watching?" I asked.

"…The field," Mena said. "How it turns."

"…Nothing ever falls in it."

Mena looked at me.

"…I know," she said. "That's why I came to watch."

Something about that struck me as strange. Since coming into the city
I'd hardly met anyone who knew that nothing was left at the corner.
Everyone knew the corner clause was being kept. No one asked why, if it
was being kept, there was nothing there.

"…Lytron knows too," I said. "But he won't say why either."

"…Who's Lytron?"

"…The harvest-management assistant."

Mena wrote the name in a corner of her paper. I watched her hand move.
No judgment attached itself to the name. No good person, no bad person.
Just a name, and what he did.

"…Mena," I said. "Why did you come here?"

She looked up at the sky for a moment.

"…I heard a rumor," Mena said. "That this city's Roll is unusually
precise. A precise Roll always catches my interest."

"…Why?"

"…The more precise something is, the more exactly it leaves something
out," Mena said. "I wanted to see what that was."

I thought that was a frightening thing to say. But there was nothing
frightening in Mena's face. Just a plain, even calm — the look of
someone asking how wide a field was.

*That was the first time I had any real sense of what kind of person
Mena was. She hadn't come to fix something in this city. She had come to
see what the city had left out. Fixing and seeing were different things.
I didn't know the difference then. I would come to know it very
clearly.*

---

That evening, Lytron passed by our cabin and stopped.

"…Who was that, earlier?" he asked.

"…Mena," I said. "She writes things down."

"…Writes what?"

"…People."

Something odd crossed his face.

"…The Roll writes people down too," he said.

"…It's different," I said. I had no words then to explain exactly how.
I just knew that it was.

Lytron stood there a moment, shook his head, and walked on. I watched
him go and thought: someday Lytron will meet Mena too. I wondered what
his face would look like then.

*I had no idea, then, how soon my curiosity would be satisfied.*

But he turned back after a few steps.

"…Is she registered in the Roll?" he asked.

"…I don't know," I said. "I never asked."

"…That's strange," he said. "Can someone who isn't registered really
move this freely through a city?"

"…Mena seems to."

Lytron's face went thoughtful for a moment. I saw something uneasy in
it. He was someone who liked everything kept in order. An unregistered
person moving about freely was, to him, like an unfilled box.

"…I'll check on it," he said. And this time he actually left.

*I heard that so lightly, at the time. Lytron saying he'd check on
something was nothing unusual. I didn't know yet where that checking
would lead him.*

That evening, standing over the water jar, I turned the conversation
over again. Lytron had found it strange that an unregistered person
could move so freely. I didn't think that reaction was his fault. He'd
been born into a world that was kept in order. Of course something
unordered would unsettle him. I only didn't yet guess where that
unsettled feeling would eventually take him.

---

The next day, at noon, I met Mena at the corner again. This time she
wasn't alone. The administrator stood beside her, explaining something.

I watched them from a little distance.

"…This is the designated corner," the administrator said, gesturing.
"Under Article 9, we leave it exactly, every year."

"…May I look at what's left?" Mena asked.

The administrator smiled. There wasn't the slightest discomfort in that
smile.

"…As much as you like," he said. "We have nothing to hide."

Mena walked to the corner and crouched down, sifting the soil with her
fingertips. Nothing came up but a few handfuls of trampled, broken
stalks.

"…What's the loss rate?" Mena asked.

"…0.02 percent," the administrator said. "As far as we know, the lowest
figure in the region."

"…Are you proud of it?"

The administrator paused at the question before he answered.

"…Not proud, exactly. Accurate, I'd say," he said. "We've never broken
the law. Not then, and not now."

Mena said nothing to that. She only nodded and wrote something down.

*I still think back on that scene often. The administrator hadn't lied.
The Roll really hadn't broken the law. And yet there was nothing at all
in the dirt on Mena's fingertips. That day, for the first time, I saw
with my own eyes that both of those things could be true at once.*

When the administrator had gone, Mena called to me.

"…Xene," she said. "That man didn't lie."

"…I know," I said.

"…That's the more frightening part," Mena said. "If it were a lie, you
could catch it. If it isn't a lie, there's nothing to catch."

I didn't fully understand what she meant then. Later I would return to
those words more than once.

---

That afternoon, the man who quietly gleaned among the workers passed
near us. Mena's eyes followed him a moment.

"…And him?" Mena asked.

"…He gleans alongside me," I said. "One of the hired hands."

"…His name?"

"…I don't know. I never asked."

Mena nodded, and instead of a name, wrote a short line: *Name unknown —
gleans alongside her.*

I felt something strange, watching those words settle onto the page.
Mena never pretended to know what she didn't. When she didn't know, she
wrote that she didn't know.

The man glanced our way for a moment. He seemed to know someone had
been watching him, writing something down. He didn't get angry, wasn't
curious either. He just gave a small nod and went back to his place. A
trampled stalk snapped under his foot.

I heard the sound and looked at Mena. She seemed to have heard it too —
turned her head that way for a second, then back to the paper. She
wrote nothing. I didn't understand, then, why that was worth noticing.
Mena wasn't someone who wrote down everything she saw. She wrote what
was worth writing. What the standard for that was, I didn't ask that
day.

*I didn't know then why that was so unusual. In this city, nothing was
ever simply let pass unknown. The Roll always filled every blank. There
was no box for "unknown." With Mena, there was.*

Mena hoisted the case back onto her shoulder and asked me,

"…What are you doing tomorrow?"

"…Going out to glean. Same as always."

"…Tomorrow I think I'll look inside the Roll," Mena said. "There's
something called the reputation field, apparently. Where people's
histories collect."

"…Why there?"

Mena looked at me for a moment. For the first time something heavy
passed through her eyes.

"…Because things I wrote long ago might be somewhere in there," Mena
said. "I've never checked what's being done with them now."

I didn't understand, then, why she said that so carefully. Her voice
was the same as always. But her fingers were gripping the wooden board
just slightly tighter. I noticed it by chance. And it would be the next
day before I understood that the chance had been no accident at all.

*I didn't know, then, what face that carefulness would turn into by the
next day. I only knew that Mena would be somewhere in this city
tomorrow, watching something.*

---

That night, setting down the water jar, I told Pikra about the woman
I'd met.

Pikra was sitting by the hearth, working over dried stalks — a handful
gathered that day at the corner. Many of them had been trampled and
broken, and it took time to pick out the whole ones.

"…I met someone new today," I said.

Pikra looked up without stopping her hands.

"…What kind of person?"

"…Someone who writes. She watches people at the well and writes things
down."

Pikra listened a long while, then said,

"…Mena?"

"…Yes. You know her?"

Pikra shook her head.

"…No. I think I've only heard the name," Pikra said. "There's supposed
to be someone like that. Going between cities, writing things down.
Has been for a very long time."

"…Doesn't that scare you?"

Pikra smiled — a smile I hadn't seen much of lately.

"…No. If anything, it's a relief," Pikra said. "Someone who writes
isn't someone who takes."

"…What's the difference?"

Pikra thought a moment.

"…The Roll takes a number from me," Pikra said. "Someone who writes
takes nothing from me. They just sit beside you."

*I let that pass, that night. Now I know. Pikra already knew, even then,
that what made this city frightening wasn't what it took away. It was
what it never gave. And a person who only writes is neither of those
things.*

---

The next day, I went out to the corner earlier than usual.

Mena was already there. Not at the field, but on the low bank beside
it, watching the morning workers come in.

I sat beside her. Neither of us said anything.

We sat a long while like that, watching the field come to life. The
machines starting up, the scales chiming, the line of workers forming.

Then Mena said quietly,

"…It's loud."

"…Yes," I said.

"…With all this noise, there's not one sound in this field that's grain
falling."

I thought about that for a long time.

*After that, every time I went out to the field, I listened. The
machines, the scales, the voices. And there really was never a sound of
anything falling. Mena was the first person who had ever confirmed that
for me — by sound.*

Mena stood and brushed the dirt from her clothes.

"…I'll look over that way today," she said. "You go on as you always
do. I'm only someone passing through."

That phrase stayed with me strangely. Someone passing through. And yet
a few days later, that someone passing through would find something no
one in this city had ever seen.

Watching her hoist the leather case onto her shoulder, I tried to guess
how many names must be inside it. I couldn't guess.

I watched her walk away along the bank for a long time. Her step was
neither slow nor fast. It was the step of someone who knew exactly
where she was going, and yet was in no hurry at all.

Carrying my water jar back to the cabin, I counted through, one by one,
the people I'd met that day. The administrator. Lytron. The man. Mena.
Four people, watching the same field, and all four had seen something
different. The administrator saw a law that had been kept. Lytron saw
one unregistered name. The man saw a few unspoken stalks of grain. Mena
saw dirt with nothing in it.

It struck me as strange that none of them were seeing it wrong. Each of
them had seen exactly what they'd seen. And yet even added all together,
their accuracies hadn't put a single more grain on that ground.

*I thought, for the first time, that day: if people looking at the same
spot could see such different things, could any one of them be called
wrong? I fell asleep that night without an answer.*

At the cabin door I stopped a moment and looked up. A few stars were
scattered thin across the sky. Not so different from the sky I'd known
outside the settlement. That, at least, seemed to be something even
this city couldn't touch.

*He had told me once that what you spill and what you leave behind were
the same thing. I turned that over again that night. Maybe the names
Mena was writing down were something like that, too. Nothing changed —
things were only left behind. I didn't yet know why that was necessary.
But strangely, it eased my mind a little. Somewhere, someone had written
down that I had been at the corner that day.*

*I didn't know, then, that the woman I'd met at the well would be the
one who made it possible for me to write this record at all. I only
thought, that night, that her case must be heavy.*
