# Chapter 5. Your People

> _Previously_ — At the fork in the road, Glyke tells her daughters-in-
> law three times to go back. Daphne weeps, kisses them both, and
> leaves for her own family. Xene stays. Glyke grows angry — if she
> follows, her standing will fall to zero. Xene sees the fear beneath
> that anger. Glyke takes her hand and asks, "Why won't you say
> anything." Xene tries to answer.

I didn't say much, standing there.

People still ask me, even now, why I answered so briefly. It was such
an important moment — why didn't I say more? I always give the same
answer. There wasn't more to say.

Once, someone I met here in the city asked me, "You made a decision
that big and didn't explain yourself? Didn't anyone ask?" I told him
Glyke had asked. And that I hadn't answered. He looked at me as if
that were a strange thing.

*I understand that look. In this city, an explanation is proof itself.
No explanation means no grounds, and no grounds means nothing to
trust. I would learn that rule the hard way, later. But at the fork
that evening, I didn't know it yet. I only spoke the way I knew how
to speak.*

*I was most careful, writing this chapter. There was a temptation to
write this scene large — as though I'd said something momentous. But
that would be a lie. I didn't say anything momentous. I said something
short.*

Up to that very moment, I hadn't decided in advance what I would say.
Not while I watched Daphne's back walking away, not while Glyke's hand
held mine. I wasn't choosing words in my head. My mouth simply opened,
and the words came.

*I've found this strange, many times since. Shouldn't the more
important the words, the more you need to prepare them? For me it was
the reverse. Every prepared answer came attached to a reason. Only the
unprepared answer came with no reason attached. And a reasonless
answer, in the end, was the one that never wavered.*

---

"…I go where you go," I said.

Glyke looked at me. Her face waited for more.

I said nothing more.

"…That's all?" Glyke asked.

"…Yes."

"…Why?"

I didn't answer that question either. Not because there was no reason.
If I gave the reason, the choice would become a choice made for a
reason, and a choice made for a reason can waver once the reason is
gone. I didn't want to make a choice that could waver.

Glyke started to ask again, then stopped. Whatever she'd meant to say,
she closed her mouth on it, and looked at me a long while instead. I
didn't look away. There was no reason to.

*I can describe that silence more precisely now. I hadn't failed to
say the reason, then. I had decided not to say it. Those are two
different things.*

I didn't know, at the time, how important that distinction would
become across this whole book. Not being able to say something, and
choosing not to say it. The Roll makes no distinction between the two.
To the Roll, silence with no explanation is all the same silence. But
to a person, the two are entirely different things.

Glyke looked at me a long while more, then asked again.

"…Have you decided where to stay, too? I might not even be able to
feed myself, once we get there. With you along."

"…That's all right."

"…What's all right about it. Were you even listening to what I
said?"

"…Yes."

"…Then answer me."

"…I did," I said. "I go where you go."

---

Glyke let go of my hand. She stepped back a few paces.

For a moment I thought it was a refusal, and my heart dropped. It
wasn't. Glyke simply wanted to see me from a little further off.

For a long while neither of us said anything. The wind moved the
branches. I was afraid of that silence. Wondering if I'd said
something wrong, I began to open my mouth again.

But just then, an expression I'd never seen before crossed Glyke's
face. Not anger, not grief. Something like acceptance. Like the face
of someone who had finally finished a calculation they'd long put off.

*I still haven't found the right words to describe that expression
exactly. But after seeing it, I understood something. This woman had
never meant to win against me, not from the start. Stopping us three
times wasn't because she truly wanted to stop us — it was because she
needed to fulfill the duty of trying. And a person who has fulfilled
their duty is, after that, free to accept.*

"…You really are stubborn," Glyke said at last. There was no anger in
her voice.

"…Yes."

"…I never taught you to be this stubborn."

"…I learned it from you," I said.

Glyke laughed shortly at that, as if it were absurd. There were still
tears in her eyes.

"…When did I ever."

"…All the time," I said. "Not shrinking the field. Standing in the
shed. Taking the stones. That's all the same thing, isn't it."

Glyke had no answer to that.

*That was the first time I felt like I had won. I hadn't spoken to
win. Maybe it wasn't a matter of winning or losing at all. I was
simply handing back what I'd learned from her.*

I thought, in that moment, of what my husband had told me too. That
what you spill isn't wasted, it's left behind. I'd taken it, at the
time, for a joke told in a field. That evening it sounded different.
Leaving something behind isn't a loss. It's the making of a place
where something remains. I thought, right then, that staying beside
Glyke was exactly that kind of act. Not a loss — the making of a
place.

*These two things — what I learned in a field, and the stubbornness I
learned in that house — seemed to arrive together, at that fork in the
road. I didn't connect them, at the time. Only now, writing this down,
do they connect.*

---

Glyke came close again and took my face in both hands. Her hands were
rough.

"…Xene," Glyke said. "I have nothing to give you. I'm not taking you
because I have something to give. You understand that?"

"…I understand."

"…In the city, people will treat you carelessly. They might not even
call you by name — just a number."

"…I understand."

"…When it's cold, no one will help you. You won't starve, but you
won't be full either."

"…I understand."

"…There, I might not be able to protect you either. My own standing
is on the floor."

"…I understand."

"…Xene, this isn't me taking you because I can do something for you.
It's the opposite. I'm going to become someone who can do nothing for
you."

"…That's all right."

"…Why is everything all right with you. Don't you know any words
besides all right."

I nearly laughed at that. I held it back.

"…And still you'll come."

"…Yes."

Glyke looked at me a long while. Then she lowered her hands and
nodded.

"…Then let's go," Glyke said. Her voice had completely changed. It
was no longer a voice trying to stop me.

*I've pictured this moment many times since. Some days it feels like
consent. Some days it feels like surrender. I think now the two are
the same thing. Ceasing to let go of someone, and taking someone in —
in the end, it's the same motion.*

---

We slept on the road that night, near the fork. We had no strength to
go further.

We built a fire and shared out the dry bread. Daphne's absence felt
strangely large. Neither of us looked at that empty space. The habit
of dividing our load three ways was still in my body — I caught myself,
more than once, holding out bread toward where Daphne would have sat,
then stopping.

Many stars were out. The same stars we'd watched back at the
settlement, and yet they felt different, seen from this unfamiliar
road.

"…Aren't you cold?" Glyke asked.

"…I'm fine."

"…*Fine* again," Glyke said, laughing a little. It was the first time
she'd laughed since that evening.

I was glad of that laugh. I'd thought I might never see it again. It
wasn't the same laugh as before, but a laugh was still a laugh.

I added another branch to the fire. Sparks rose and faded out.

"…Mother," I asked. "Will Daphne be all right?"

"…She'll be fine," Glyke said. "She has somewhere to go back to.
People like that are usually fine."

"…What about me?"

Glyke looked at me. "…You'll be fine because you have nowhere to go
back to."

I looked at her, not understanding.

"…When you have somewhere to go back to, you keep looking back," Glyke
said. "You won't look back anymore. There's nothing behind you to
look back at. That'll make you harder, in the end."

*I took that as comfort at the time. Now I hear it differently. It was
comfort and an exact prophecy, both at once. I really have never
looked back, not once, since. Whether that made me hard, or whether it
was simply that I had nothing to look back at, I still don't quite
know.*

"…Xene," Glyke said, across the firelight.

"…Yes."

"…Can I ask you one thing."

"…Yes."

"…What's your real reason. You didn't have to say back there. Can you
tell just me, now?"

I looked at the fire a long while.

"…I don't know," I said. "I honestly don't know. It just felt
stranger, the thought of not being wherever you are."

Glyke listened to that and nodded quietly. She didn't press further.

*I think this is the most precise account I can give, in this whole
book, of my own choice. I didn't go for a reason. It only felt
stranger not to go. Some choices are made that way — not by a force
pulling forward, but by the force of not going another way.*

---

I lay awake late into that night. Glyke fell asleep first. I sat up a
long while, watching the fire die down.

*I'll set down here that I wavered, once, that night. Only for a
moment. As the fire was nearly out, I found myself thinking, for a
moment, of which direction Daphne's road had gone. The thought crossed
me that I could have gone that way too. It was very brief. I didn't
hold onto it. I knew that holding it would only make it grow.*

*I set down that wavering because I don't want to say I never
wavered. I did waver. I simply didn't follow where it led. Those are
different things.*

Before dawn I slept a little, then woke to the sound of Glyke packing.
The sky was still dark.

"…Sleep more," Glyke said.

"…No, I'll get up."

We set off again without much of a breakfast. My legs were stiff.

---

The next morning, we shouldered our packs and started walking toward
the city again.

It was just the two of us on the road now. The space Daphne had walked
was empty. I was aware of that empty space all day.

The road widened as we went. More people passed us. Unfamiliar faces,
all of them. They glanced at us and kept walking. I kept lowering my
head at their unfamiliar looks.

"…Lift your head," Glyke said. "No need to look down."

"…People are looking."

"…Let them look. We haven't done anything wrong."

I lifted my head at that. I didn't know, then, how often I'd think
back on those words in the days to come.

Around noon, Glyke said, out of nowhere,

"…Xene."

"…Yes."

"…Thank you."

That was all. I didn't answer. It wasn't a thing that needed
answering.

That afternoon, we saw a field far off for the first time. Larger and
straighter than anything we'd passed the day before. The furrows
seemed to run all the way to the horizon.

"…That's where the city's jurisdiction begins," Glyke said.

I looked at that field for a long while. It was strangely quiet. Even
with the wind blowing, there was no sound but the wheat itself moving.

"…No birds," I said.

Glyke said nothing and kept walking. I didn't know, then, what that
silence meant.

*Now I know. Glyke already knew that field. How much it had changed
from the one she'd left thirty-some years before — she must have
taken it in at a glance. I couldn't read her expression, then. Only
later did I call it back to mind, and read it properly.*

*I've named this chapter* Your People. *Those weren't words I spoke
that day. I read them, much later, in a certain book. And when I read
them, I knew that what happened at that fork was exactly those words.
I went where my mother went. Which meant her people had become my
people. I didn't know the words then, but I was already living them.*

*I cried a long while after reading that book. The woman in it spoke
briefly too, the way I did. Gave no reason, the way I didn't. Reading
it, I thought that the choice I'd made that day might not have been
mine alone to make. Maybe someone had already walked that road, and I
only walked behind her. I didn't know that, then. There are roads you
can walk without knowing.*
