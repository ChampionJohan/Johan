# Chapter 45. The Standard

> _Previously_ — Mena told me what the two names meant. Xene — stranger,
> and at the same time, guest. Lytron — the price paid to get someone
> back. I lay awake that night, unable to say whether the two names had
> met by chance.

The administrator came to find me not long after that.

He had never come to us first before. We had always gone to him. I was
briefly startled, seeing him standing at our door.

"…What brings you here?" I asked.

"…I have something to explain," he said. "Do you have a moment?"

---

We sat on the bench by the door. The scalding heat of summer had already
taken a step back. The wood of the bench was cool to the touch, and a
few persimmons were ripening red in the corner of the yard. Pikra came
and sat with us.

"…Miss Xene's case," the administrator said, "has come to remain in the
Roll as a record."

"…A record?"

His tone was, as always, the same. A voice reading off a document,
nearly without inflection. There was something in that flatness that
caught at me. What he was speaking of had been the heaviest season of my
life, and the moment it left his mouth, it became a single entry.

"…Precedent would be the more precise word," the administrator said.
"This is the first case in which the standing-transfer procedure was
actually used. Since the founding of the Roll."

I didn't understand the words at first.

"…Why does that need to be explained to me?"

"…Because this case will now serve as a reference," he said. "If anyone
else wishes to use this procedure, the office will now be able to show
them this case. How the procedure actually proceeds. What paperwork is
required. What happens after the transfer."

---

"…What am I supposed to do?" I asked.

"…Nothing," he said. "I only came to inform you. And this is also to
ask your consent. Whether you agree to have this remain as a case
record."

Something struck me strangely, hearing that.

"…And if I don't agree?"

"…The name can be withheld," the administrator said. "The case itself
will remain, but your name won't be attached to it."

I thought for a moment.

"…The name can remain," I said.

---

Pikra looked at me.

"…Why?" Pikra asked.

I didn't have an answer ready. I needed to gather my thoughts.

"…I think," I said, slowly, "I don't want this to have happened to me
alone. If someone else ends up in this same place someday, I think it's
better if this exists."

The administrator looked at me steadily.

"…May I ask why?" he asked.

I thought a moment, then said,

"…Medon," I said. "He didn't do it. But maybe the next person who stands
where he stood — because of me — might decide differently."

---

The administrator was quiet a moment, hearing that.

"…That's an interesting reason," he said. "The Roll wasn't designed that
way. A case is kept for procedure's sake, not to persuade anyone."

"…Then it won't persuade anyone," I said.

"…Probably not," he said. "The Roll doesn't change a person's mind. It
only shows a case. Whatever the person who sees it decides to do with it
— that's theirs."

*Those words stayed with me. The Roll, once again, promised me nothing.
It only left a door a little easier to see. Whether to walk through it
remained, still, entirely, each person's own.*

---

A few days later I went to the office myself and looked at the record.

My case was summarized on the screen. Name, standing before the
transfer, value received, the one who transferred it, the date
processed. Below it, in small print: *First recorded use.*

I looked at that phrase for a long time.

The sentences on the screen were short and dry. A name, a number, a
date. I thought of Mena, looking at them. She had told me once that a
single line she'd written had traveled through other cities and come
back meaning something entirely different. I'd thought, at the time,
that was someone else's problem. Now my own name, too, had been reduced
to a few words and set down on a screen. That those words might someday
travel through other mouths, in other contexts, changed from what they
were — for the first time, that thought frightened me.

*I felt something strange, at that moment. A few months earlier, I hadn't
even existed in the Roll. I didn't have a name. Now my name had become
the Roll's standard. I know exactly what happened in between. I hadn't
done anything remarkable. I had only gone out to the field every day,
and someone had given up what was theirs.*

---

The administrator showed me one more document.

"…This is still a draft," he said, "but we've drawn up a new
information sheet on the standing-transfer procedure, based on this
case. There was almost no guidance on it before."

I read the sheet. Procedure, required documents, expected outcome. At
the bottom, one line read:

*Reference: the Xene case (Registration, Chapter 41).*

I laughed, reading that. I hadn't expected my story to end up as a
footnote in an official document.

---

"…What will this change?" I asked the administrator.

"…I don't know," he said. "I'll be honest with you. I can't promise this
procedure will see much use. There may well be more Medons than not."

"…Then why go to all this trouble? Making up an information sheet and
all."

The administrator thought for a moment.

"…Because it's my job," he said. "I'm a man who keeps the law
precisely. But if a door the law has left open sits unseen and untended,
that's a failure on my part too, I think."

I was a little surprised by that. For the first time, I saw something
human inside the administrator. He wasn't only a cold man. In his own
way, within precision, he was doing his best.

---

About a month later, a stranger knocked at our door.

A woman. A few years older than me. Her clothes were worn, her face
tired.

"…Are you Xene?" she asked.

"…Yes."

"…I'm sorry to bother you," she said. "I heard about you, at the
office. That you'd had your standing transferred to you."

Something occurred to me at that.

---

I brought her inside. Pikra brought tea.

"…My mother-in-law," she said, "has low standing. I have a little more.
I wondered if I could do what you did. But no one seemed to know how."

"…Did you go to the office?"

"…I did," she said. "They only said the procedure was complicated.
Nothing more. Then I heard about you. The first case."

I felt something strange, hearing that. That my story had traveled all
the way to someone's door — it still didn't feel real.

---

"…I'll tell you what I know," I said.

I explained what had happened, step by step. The application, the
signature, the threshold. She wrote it down on paper.

"…But," I said, carefully, "you're giving up your own standing. It
isn't an easy decision."

She nodded.

"…I know," she said. "That woman raised me. My husband lost his father
young, and she raised him alone."

*I couldn't help thinking of Lytron, hearing that. He hadn't raised me.
We hadn't even known each other very long. And still he'd given up what
was his. This woman, at least, had a clear reason. I envied that a
little, and at the same time understood, more than ever, how much
harder Lytron's act had been to explain.*

---

After she left, Pikra spoke.

"…There'll be more," Pikra said. "People like her."

"…Why do you think that?"

"…Word doesn't stop once it starts," Pikra said. "Whether you want it
to or not, you've become a certain kind of name in this city now. The
one who knows the way."

That felt heavy, hearing it.

"…I'm not someone who knows the way," I said. "I'm just someone who
received."

"…That's the same thing," Pikra said. "Only someone who's received can
show another person the way. Someone who's never received doesn't even
know the door exists."

---

A few days later, I heard, through the office, that the woman had
actually gone through with the procedure. I didn't know the details.
Whether it had succeeded, or whether she'd stopped partway, like Medon
— I never learned.

*I never did hear the rest of that story. That was how this city was.
One person's choice opened a door for another, but whether that person
walked through it was never reported back. I found that frustrating,
and, at the same time, somehow right. Not every choice needed to be
reported back to me.*

I told Lytron about it that evening.

"…What you did became a precedent," I said. "My case."

Lytron looked briefly surprised.

"…Me?"

"…None of this exists without you," I said.

He shook his head.

"…I only did one thing," he said. "Becoming a precedent isn't up to me
to decide. That's for the Roll — or the people who come after — to
decide."

I thought that was exactly like him. He was never a man who measured
the size of what he'd done.

---

"…Can I ask you something?" I said.

"…Yes."

"…What would Medon say, if he saw this record?"

Lytron thought for a moment.

"…I don't know," he said. "But I don't think seeing it would change his
mind. He already knew everything he needed to know, that day, when he
decided."

"…Then what use is this record?"

Lytron looked out over the field.

"…It probably isn't left there to change Medon," he said. "It's for
someone else. Someone who doesn't even know this procedure exists yet.
Like me. If the administrator hadn't mentioned it, in passing, I never
would have known it existed either."

---

I thought again, hearing that, about the way this city worked.

*The Roll persuades no one. It only records. What's recorded remains for
the next person to see. And what that person does with it is always,
always, left to them. I understand now that this is how this city
works. There's no compulsion. Only a door made a little easier to see.*

*I found that both sad and fortunate. Sad, because even with this
record, the next Medon might still not walk through the door. Fortunate,
because with this record, the next Lytron will at least know the door is
there.*

---

I went out to the corner late that night. "He" was still there.

"…Did you hear?" I asked. "My case became a standard in the Roll."

He nodded.

"…I know," he said.

"…You knew that too?" I asked, smiling. He always already knew things
no one had told him.

"…I heard it from the administrator, this time," he said. Almost like
a joke. He rarely spoke that way.

I sat down beside him.

---

"…It's a strange thought," I said. "My story is a line in this city's
rulebook now. And I'm still just myself. Going out to the field,
gleaning grain. Nothing's changed, it feels like, and at the same time
something's completely changed."

He stopped his hands and looked at me.

"…That's not strange," he said. "That's how a big thing always is. To
the person living through it, every day feels the same as the last.
Only later, looking back, do you see that a line's been drawn somewhere
between those days."

"…Where does it get drawn?"

"…No one knows," he said. "No one knows, in the moment the line is
drawn, that it's a line at all. You only see it later."

*I let that pass by that night. I hear it differently now. That might
not have been a thing he only said about this city. Every time he spoke
that way, I felt his words came from somewhere larger than this one
city. I thought, at the time, that it was only my imagination.*

"…One more thing I wanted to ask," I said. "Does a standard like this
exist in other cities too? Someone like me?"

He didn't answer for a long time. His hands kept moving.

"…There probably is," he finally said. "This probably isn't something
that only happened in this city."

I found that answer strangely comforting. I couldn't explain why.

---

I lay awake that night, thinking for a long while about the fact that a
name that had never been in the Roll had become the Roll's standard.

*When I first came to this city, I had zero standing. I existed, but I
wasn't counted. Now my name remains, a single line, in this city's
rulebook. In between, I hadn't done very much. I had only gone out to
the same place, every day. It wasn't I who had paid the price.*

*And still I think I understand, a little now, why my name became the
standard. It didn't become the standard because of anything I did. It
became the standard because of what someone did for me. I want to set
that down exactly, in this book. This standard isn't mine. I was only
the place where that price came to rest.*

*I wonder, sometimes, whether that footnote — "Reference: the Xene
case" — will still be there in this city's rulebook, years from now,
decades from now. Whether someone will read it and go looking for my
name. I don't know the answer. But I know exactly what lay behind that
footnote. One signature. A few seconds. And the quiet ordinary days of
one person that went on, after those few seconds ended. A footnote
can't hold all of that. That's why I write this record.*
