# Chapter 43. The Corner

> _Previously_ — I tried to call Pikra Glyke again. Pikra refused. "The
> bitter didn't go away. I just learned I could live with it still
> there." She had chosen, of her own will, to go on being Pikra.

Lytron reset the loss rate before summer's end.

From 0.02 percent to 3 percent. Seen as a number, it was a small
difference. The administrator once showed us that figure. "That's all
it is," he said. Truly, that was all.

*I heard that phrase — that's all it is — many times in this city. That's
all a signature is, that's all a number is, that's all a loss rate is. I
know now. The largest things in this city always hid behind the words
that's all it is.*

Once that number changed, the corner of the field changed.

---

The first day, I went out to the corner as always.

I didn't expect much, as always. It was habit. My body was simply used
to going out. All I knew was that I'd come back with a few handfuls of
trampled stalks.

That day was different.

There was grain in the corner. Real grain. Unbroken, standing on the
stem, the ear full. Scattered here and there.

I stood a long time without moving.

*I remember that moment exactly. What came before the surprise was
suspicion. Had someone dropped it by accident. Had someone put it there
to mock me. It took time before I believed the grain was real.*

---

I bent down and picked one up.

It was real. The kernels were ripe, the stalk still alive. I held it in
my hand and looked at it for a long time.

I filled a whole sack that day. The first time since the day it rained.
That day, the machines had stopped. This time they hadn't. The machines
still ran. The workers still moved as always. But somewhere in that
motion, a gap had opened that hadn't been there before. A gap the size
of one falling ear of grain.

*I understood that difference exactly, then. The rain had been the
sky's doing. This was a person's doing. Filling that sack, I knew that
inside it was something someone had set down with their own hands.*

---

I brought the sack home that night and spread it out and looked at it
for a long while.

Pikra came and looked with me.

"…You gleaned a lot," Pikra said. She didn't sound surprised. She
seemed to already know.

"…Something's changed," I said. "At the corner."

"…I'd heard," Pikra said. "Something about Lytron."

"…You knew?"

"…I guessed," Pikra said. "He's been strangely quiet since he gave up
his standing. It was the quiet of someone with something left to do."

I was surprised at that. Pikra had noticed before I had.

*I felt again, that moment, how much deeper Pikra's eye for reading
people ran than mine. She had lived her whole life that way. Telling
apart the one who has something to give from the one who has nothing
left, the one still willing to give from the one who has already given
everything. I still haven't fully learned that eye.*

Word got around within a few days.

There was grain at the corner, the word went. No one believed it at
first. Then a few came to check. Then more came.

I watched those people. People with low standing or none at all. The
old, those with children, those whose bodies weren't strong. They came
to the corner and gleaned.

*I felt something strange, watching that scene. I had stood alone at
that corner for a long time. Not anymore. I couldn't tell whether that
meant my loneliness had ended, or my place had simply disappeared.*

I once saw the administrator pass by the corner. He stopped and watched
the people gleaning for a long while. I was nervous he might do
something about it.

He said nothing. He only watched, and then walked on.

I asked him about it later, when I ran into him at the office.

"…Why didn't you say anything, that day at the corner?"

The administrator thought for a moment.

"…There was nothing to say," he said. "Article 9 says a corner must be
left. It doesn't specify how much. What Mr. Lytron changed in the
settings wasn't a violation. His family standing paid the price for it.
I have no place in that matter."

"…Doesn't it bother you," I asked, "that so many people are suddenly
coming?"

"…Whether it bothers me is unrelated to my job," the administrator
said. "My job is only to see whether the law is being kept. It's being
kept."

*I confirmed, once more, why he frightened me. He didn't judge by
feeling. He didn't judge by good or bad. He judged only by whether the
law was kept. And by that measure, he was always right.*

---

An old man was gleaning beside me.

"…Used to be nothing here at all," he said, half to himself. "There's a
bit, lately."

"…Yes," I said.

"…Someone must have changed something," he said. And asked no further.
He didn't seem curious about the reason. It seemed enough for him just
to know there was something to gather today.

*I felt hurt by that indifference, at first. He didn't know what Lytron
had paid. Didn't seem to think he needed to know. I think differently
now. It was right, in fact. That was what the corner clause had always
been. The giver's name was never meant to remain. Leviticus never said
that either. It only said to leave it.*

---

I no longer needed to go out to the corner.

My own ration came separately now. Registered as I was, I had a right
to it. I was no longer someone who lived by gleaning at the bottom.

And still I kept going.

Pikra found it strange, at first.

"…You don't have to go anymore," Pikra said.

"…I know."

"…Then why do you go."

I couldn't answer right away. I didn't know the exact reason myself.

---

I carried the question with me for a few days.

One day I met "him" at the corner. He was still there. Among the other
hired hands.

"…Why come out, when you don't have to anymore?" he asked. The same
question as Pikra's.

"…I don't know," I said. "It's just familiar here."

"…Is that all?"

I thought a moment.

"…No," I said. "I don't want to forget what I was here."

He stopped his hands and looked at me.

"…What were you?" he asked.

"…Someone who was nothing," I said. "I only learned what that meant
after I came here. I don't think I should forget it."

*That was the first time I made that answer for myself. No one had
taught it to me. I kept gleaning, even after being registered. Not out
of need, but for some other reason. That day was the first time I put
the reason into words.*

---

He nodded.

"…That's a good reason," he said.

"…Why do you keep staying here?" I asked. A question I'd always wondered
about.

He smiled a little. He didn't answer. He just started moving his hands
again.

*I let that silence pass by, back then, the way I always did. Now I find
myself missing that silence, sometimes. I learned, being with him, that
some questions carry farther left as questions than answered.*

---

The corner stayed changed, from that autumn on.

Not fully generous. 3 percent was still a small number. Not enough to
feed a whole family. But it was different from before. Before, it had
been close to zero. Now it wasn't zero.

*I think that difference is a large one. People often ask whether it can
really matter that much. 0.02 percent to 3 percent. It might not look
like a large number. But I can say this, having stood at that corner
myself. Between having nothing at all and having a little, there is a
distance no number can hold.*

The administrator said nothing about the change. I understood why,
later. The Roll had broken no law. The loss rate wasn't a value the Roll
set — it was a value a person set. Lytron's resetting it hadn't been
illegal. It had simply been a loss. His family standing took that loss
exactly as it was.

*I would confirm this fact again and again, throughout this book. The
Roll never broke the law, not once. The corner clause had been there
from the beginning. It had simply been designed so that nothing would
be left there. What turned that design back wasn't the Roll — it was one
person. And that person paid the price with his own body.*

---

One day I saw Medon passing near the corner.

He stopped and watched the people gleaning. I tensed for a moment,
afraid he might recognize us.

He found me. He didn't come closer. He only nodded slightly, from a
distance. I nodded back.

*I don't know what he was thinking in that moment. I never asked. I only
remember that the time he stood there watching wasn't a short one. He
must have seen, that day, for the first time, someone else walking
through the door he had refused.*

He walked on before long. He didn't look back.

I told Pikra about it that night.

"…I saw Medon. Near the corner."

Pikra said nothing for a moment.

"…He must know now too," Pikra said, finally. "What he didn't do."

"…Do you hate him?"

"…No," Pikra said. "I told him, that day, in that room. I don't resent
you. That was true then, and it's true now."

*Listening to that, I felt that what Pikra carried toward Medon was a
little different from what I carried. I still felt some leftover
disappointment, whenever I thought of him. Pikra didn't seem to. She had
already accepted, in the way of someone who has lived long, that right
and good are not the same thing.*

---

A few days later, I met a girl at the corner.

She looked a year or two younger than me. She didn't know how to glean
and was struggling. She kept pulling the whole stalk and breaking it.

"…Try it like this," I said, coming closer to show her. "Leave the
stalk. Just take the ear. Gently."

The girl copied what I did. She failed a few times, and then got the
knack of it.

"…Thank you," the girl said.

I felt something strange, hearing that. Not long ago, I had been the one
who knew nothing at this corner. Now I was the one showing someone else.

*That day I found one more reason, the true reason I kept gleaning at
this corner. I could stay here and show something to those who came,
knowing nothing, just as I once had. That wasn't a place the Roll had
given me. It was a place I'd found for myself.*

---

As autumn deepened, more people came to the corner.

I didn't know most of their names. They didn't know mine either. We
were only people who happened to bend down in the same place at the same
hour.

One day I saw a child among them. About eight years old. He'd found one
ear of grain and was carrying it to show his mother. His mother stroked
his head.

*Watching that, I thought of Lytron. He didn't know that child. He never
would. That child, too, would go his whole life never knowing where the
grain in his hand had come from. That didn't sadden me. I thought,
rather, that it was right. That the name of the one who paid the price
doesn't remain — that was the oldest rule of the corner clause.*

I told Lytron about that child that evening.

"…Did you see it?" he asked.

"…Yes."

"…That child doesn't know what I did," he said. Not in a hurt tone.

"…Do you want them to know?" I asked.

He thought for a moment.

"…No," he said. "I think it's right to leave it as it is. Not knowing."

*I knew that answer came from the same place as the day he'd given up
his standing. He had paid a price and still didn't want that price to be
known. I had never seen that kind of mind before then.*

That night I recalled the corner again. The day I first came and
gathered a few handfuls of trampled stalks, and now, this day, when I
filled a sack and went home. The same place. A completely different
place.

*I still remember these two corners side by side, even now. One where
the law was kept exactly and nothing at all was left. The other where
the law was kept just as exactly and something was left. The law didn't
make that difference. The law was exactly the same in both corners. What
made the difference was what one person decided to give up, from what
was his.*

*I think that fact explains this whole city. The Roll didn't change. The
regulations didn't change. Only one thing changed — a single person's
choice. And that one thing changed a single corner into an entirely
different place.*
