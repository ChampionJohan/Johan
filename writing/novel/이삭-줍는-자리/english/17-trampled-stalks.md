# Chapter 17. Trampled Stalks

> _Previously_ — Xene learned Lytron's day only afterward. He hadn't
> done anything wrong. And that fact stayed true even while she was the
> one coming home empty-handed, day after day.

Even after the day I met Lytron, I kept going out.

Nothing changed. The corner stayed exactly as it had been. Trampled stalks, grain broken up in the dirt, an occasional whole one. I grew steadily more practiced at picking out what could be eaten.

*This is the first time I use the word "practiced" in this chapter. Being practiced doesn't mean getting better. I want to be clear about that here. I grew practiced at hunger. Not that the hunger went away — only that I grew better at enduring it.*

---

The walk to the corner was the same every day.

Out of the lodging, along the main road, then left where the field began. About half an hour on foot. At first that half hour felt like a waste. Later it came to feel like the better part of the day — walking, I didn't have to think about anything.

The field was vast. Too vast to take in at a glance. The machines moved in formation. The first time I saw them, I'd thought them frightening. Now I just looked at them. What frightened me wasn't the machines — it was what was left behind them. Or rather, precisely: what wasn't.

The corner sat at the field's edge, against a drainage ditch. A single stake stood there: "Zone Under Article 9." I passed that stake every day. The first few days I stopped in front of it and read the letters. Later I stopped reading. There was no longer any need.

*I could still walk that path today. With my eyes shut. Half an hour, left, the stake, the ditch. Some roads a person's feet remember. I became someone whose feet remembered that one.*

---

For the first several days, others came besides me.

An older woman, and a man who brought two children. All of us ran our palms through the dirt the same way. We didn't speak to each other. We shared the same ground and never once met each other's eyes.

After about a week, the older woman stopped coming. The week after that, the man with the children stopped too. I don't know where they went. I didn't ask. It didn't seem like asking would have brought an answer.

*I guessed, later. They must have done the arithmetic. That walking half an hour to gather less than half a palmful wasn't worth it, when that half hour could go toward something else. Not a wrong calculation. I was simply the only one who never made it.*

*I still can't explain exactly why I kept going out. It wasn't a thing arithmetic could explain. Maybe that was precisely why I kept going.*

---

What I actually gathered at that corner never added up to a full day's food.

I once measured it exactly. On my best day, I gathered enough to fill two cupped palms. Even that was half dirt and broken husk. Sifted down to the grain itself, it was less than a fistful.

On my worst day, there was nothing at all. Some days there were. I don't remember well what we ate on those evenings. Probably just water, before sleep.

*I want to set these numbers down exactly. An amount that never came to a full day's food. Some days, nothing at all. This was the whole of what Article 9 put into my hands. The law was being kept. I confirmed that fact more than once. If the law had been broken, that would have been easier to bear.*

---

Once, I saw two crows at the corner.

They'd arrived before me. Pecking at the dirt. At first I was glad — if there was enough there for birds to peck at, there must be something left for me too.

Up close, it wasn't so. The birds couldn't peck long before moving on either. A few pecks in one spot, and if nothing came of it, they flew to the next. I followed them and checked the places they'd left. Nothing there either.

*That was the day I first understood that this corner wasn't stingy with people alone. It was stingy with birds too. I understood why, later. The Roll hadn't been aimed at people. It had simply eliminated spillage. But eliminating spillage meant that people and birds alike came away with nothing, equally. The Roll didn't starve me out of hatred. It simply hadn't thought about anyone, or anything, at all.*

The birds didn't stay long. I never saw a crow at that spot again. They must have found somewhere else. I had nowhere else to go.

---

Back home, I shared it with Pikra.

The ritual never changed. I spread what I'd gathered on the floor. Sorted the whole grain from the fragments. Divided the whole grain in two. Ground the fragments and stirred them into water to drink.

We did it without a word. At first I divided it and Pikra received her share. A few days on, Pikra began doing the dividing herself. Her hand was more exact.

"…How do you divide it so evenly?" I asked once.

"…Practice." Pikra said.

I didn't ask further after that.

*I learned later. During the years she lost her husband, and then both her sons, Pikra had already divided small portions among survivors many times before. There was a reason her hand had grown that exact.*

Sometimes Pikra didn't finish her own share. At first I didn't notice. One night I woke and found her quietly pushing what was left in her bowl toward mine. I pretended to be asleep.

*From that night on, I got in the habit of never quite emptying my own bowl. I left a little behind, so Pikra wouldn't see. We shared, that way, by each pretending to have what we didn't. I don't think that was a lie. It was the only way we knew, in those days, to look after each other.*

---

One evening, the grain came out to an odd number.

Three. It couldn't be split evenly. I caught my hand before it could reach naturally for two.

"…Pikra, you take two."

"…No." Pikra said. "You're the one who went out and gathered it."

"…Even so."

We sat a moment with the three grains between us, saying nothing.

In the end Pikra picked up one and broke it in half. We each took our half, ending up with two and a half apiece.

*I still remember that breaking hand. She pressed it with a fingernail and split it exactly in two — neither half larger nor smaller. I watched, that night, how carefully a person can try to be fair over the smallest possible thing.*

---

Around then, I noticed my hands had changed.

They had once been hands that held a sickle. Now they were hands that searched the dirt for a single grain. My fingertips had cracked. Only natural, digging through dirt and dry stalks every day.

Standing up sometimes blurred my sight. Not for long. Just a moment. I knew why. I only pretended not to.

*I tried to write this passage plainly. I didn't want to look pitiable. I wasn't a pitiable person, then. I was someone who got up every morning and walked that same road again. My body was, in fact, changing. That fact doesn't make me pitiful. I choose to believe that.*

---

Around then I got in the habit of hiding what I gleaned.

I sewed a small pocket inside my clothes and carried what I gathered there as I walked. No one had ever said anything to me about it. Still, I didn't want to be seen. If I carried it in my hand, someone might notice, so I hid it against my body every time, without fail.

*Looking back later, I found that habit strange. I hadn't done anything wrong. What I gathered was what the law allowed. And yet I hid it. No one had ever told me to. I thought a long time about where that habit had come from. I understand now. A person with no standing learns, on her own, without being taught, to carry even what she's been permitted to have where no one can see.*

---

Trampled stalks couldn't be eaten. Even so, I gathered them up every time.

We used them for fuel — to heat water. Not much, but better than nothing.

One day, burning that trampled straw, a strange thought struck me. These stalks had once stood upright. The machine had laid them down as it passed. Lying flat, half-buried in dirt, by the time I found them they were already crushed apart.

*Every time I saw them, my husband came to mind. Those stubbles cut off at wrist height. A sickle's cut and a machine's cut had a different grain. A sickle cuts on the slant. A machine cuts straight down. I could feel the difference now with my fingertips. That was the first skill I learned in this city.*

---

Around then I began a counting of my own.

I gathered small stones and lined them up like tallies — grains gleaned that day, compared to yesterday's. Good days went on the right, bad days on the left. The right side almost never grew.

*I realized later what an odd habit this was. I had come into this city and, for the first time, become someone who counted. But what I counted was the exact opposite of what the Roll counted. The Roll didn't count how much I had. It only counted whether I had standing. I counted the opposite — not standing, but what my hands actually held. No one would ever read that tally. I kept counting anyway.*

Those stones didn't last. Later I moved on to something else. But the habit stayed. Even now, when I count something, those stones are the first thing that comes to mind.

---

Around then I began watching the people who worked the field.

The hired hands. Some drove the harvesters directly; others followed behind, checking their condition. They were different from people like me. Not entirely without standing — they had some standing, however low, enough to take work for a wage.

I envied them. If you were paid, at least you were sure of one day's food.

The hired hands came in early morning and left toward evening. Those handling the machines didn't speak — the noise was too loud for words. Only in their breaks did they gather briefly, drink water, exchange a few words.

I got in the habit of watching those breaks from a distance. I didn't yet have the nerve to go closer. I just watched where they sat, who sat beside whom. That was all I could manage, at that point.

*I didn't know then how shallow that envy was. The hired hands' lives weren't comfortable either. They were only better off than mine. I picked up the habit, around then, of measuring people by that standard — better than me. Even now, I can't say that habit has entirely gone.*

---

On the way home, I began to notice other things.

Some days my route home passed through an inner alley of the city. On those days I'd see what had been set out near back doors. Some of it spoiled, some of it perfectly fine, simply discarded. One day, half a loaf of bread was sitting there, whole, right on the road.

I stood in front of it a long while. Hesitated over whether to take it. In the end I didn't. I don't fully know why, even now. Gleaning at the corner was fine — that was the place the law had marked out. But this was a place no one had marked out at all. I couldn't explain the difference at the time.

*I can explain it now. Gleaning at the corner was a right. Gleaning from the alley was closer to begging. I was a person with no rights left, but I still wasn't willing to beg. That was the last shred of pride I had left, at the time. Looking back it's an absurd distinction — the hunger was exactly the same either way. But I held onto that distinction anyway.*

There was something discarded, and something that never fell, both together in this city. I saw that first in that alley. Nothing fell in the field. But something was thrown away in the alley. The two things were happening, at once, inside the same city.

---

One evening, Pikra asked me something for the first time.

"…Do you see people out there?"

"…Yes. There are a lot who work there."

"…Talked to anyone yet?"

"…Not yet."

Pikra thought a moment, then said:

"…Talk to them. Can't hurt."

I found that unexpected. Pikra had always told me to be careful — never before to reach out.

"…Why?"

"…Might be better than gleaning alone." Pikra said. Then, after a pause, she added, "When I picture you sitting there by yourself every day, I feel a little—"

Pikra didn't finish the sentence.

*I still wonder, sometimes, what that unfinished sentence would have been. I think Pikra believed she had no right to be angry, no right to worry — not after having tried three times to stop me from coming with her at all. So the sentence never came all the way out. I never finished it for her. Some sentences are right left unfinished.*

---

That night, I decided that the next day, in the field, I'd try speaking to whoever I could.

It wasn't a grand resolution. Pikra's words had simply stayed with me. That it might be better than gleaning alone.

I didn't decide beforehand who I'd speak to, or what I'd say. I just thought that in the morning, whoever happened to be nearby, I'd at least say hello. That was all.

*I don't want to write this decision as though it were momentous. In the next chapter I get a little closer to the people working the field. One of them later becomes very important. But I didn't know that that night. I just fell asleep thinking, tomorrow, maybe I'll say hello to someone.*
