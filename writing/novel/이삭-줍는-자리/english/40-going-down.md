# Chapter 40. Going Down

> _Previously_ — Lytron transferred his standing to Xene. The procedure
> was one signature, a few seconds. The numbers changed on the screen —
> that was all, quiet as though nothing had happened. "He" heard the news
> and said he had "hoped" for it.

I had only ever known the word *going down* from the field.

A stalk goes down. Grain goes down. Wheat leans one way when the wind
blows. I didn't hear that word used for a person until I came to this
city. A score goes down. A family goes down.

*I thought those two kinds of going down were different. Wheat goes down
and stands up again, once the wind stops. I learned, that autumn, that
this was wrong. A person's going down doesn't stand back up just because
the wind stops.*

I can't write this chapter through Lytron's eyes. I was never inside him.
I only set down what I saw from the outside. What he lost, only someone
watching him from outside could count precisely. The one who loses rarely
knows, at first, exactly what they've lost. I learned that too, that
autumn.

When I entered this city with zero standing, I didn't know precisely what
I had lost. There was simply nothing. Never having a thing from the start
and having a thing and then losing it are different. Lytron was the
second kind. So he had to feel the size of what he'd lost, new, every
single moment. I never had to. I had no *before* to compare against.

---

For the first few days, nothing seemed to have changed.

Lytron still went to the Harvest management office. Still sat in the same
seat. I thought he was fine.

But the way people treated him was shifting, little by little. It began
with the smallest things. The order of greetings changed. Where people
used to bow their heads first when he passed, now sometimes he had to
bow first.

"…It's nothing," he told me. He said it before I'd even asked. "I'll get
used to it."

*I wanted to believe that. I would learn later that *nothing* was, in
fact, something.*

---

Pikra noticed the change before I did.

"…Doesn't he seem different lately?" Pikra said, one day.

"…He seems tired."

"…No, not that," Pikra said. "His walk's changed. Before, he walked like
a man who decided where he was going before anyone else did. These days
he walks like he's watching people's eyes."

I hadn't noticed it. Once she said it, I looked again, and it was true.

"…Is that a bad thing?" I asked.

Pikra thought for a moment.

"…Not bad," Pikra said. "But it's unfamiliar to him. Anything unfamiliar
is hard, at first."

*I turned Pikra's words over for a long time. Watching people's eyes. I
had known that since I was born. Which door to knock on for food, which
look meant a bad mood that day. Lytron was only now beginning to learn
that language.*

---

About a week later, I saw him standing in a line outside the office.

A line for confirming rations. He'd never had to stand in a line like
this before. His family name alone used to be enough to have things
processed on the spot.

Not anymore.

He waited his turn like everyone else. When the person ahead moved
slowly, he simply stood and waited. He didn't grow impatient. That
struck me as stranger than if he had. He should have been angry. He
simply stood there.

"…First time in a line?" I asked, coming up beside him.

He looked at me and smiled. A bitter smile.

"…Not the first," he said. "But this time is a little different."

"…How?"

"…Before, I could have stood in a line. I didn't have to, but I could
have, if I wanted. Now I have to stand in it."

I thought I understood the difference. Doing something because you could
choose to, and doing it because you had no choice.

*What he learned in that line, I think, I had already known, probably
since I was born. Not a line you stand in because you want to, but a line
you must stand in. I already knew what that was. He was only now
beginning to learn it.*

---

His seat at the Harvest management office had changed too.

Before, it had been by the window, a good seat. Now it had been moved
inward. No one had ordered it moved. Seats were simply arranged by tier.
The rearrangement had just, naturally, sorted him into his proper place.

"…Who told you to move?" I asked.

"…No one," he said. "Seating goes by rank. It always did. I just never
had to notice it. When my rank was high, there was nothing to notice."

*Something important was in those words. The Roll's order had always been
there. Lytron had lived on top of that order his whole life without ever
seeing it. Order is invisible from above. You have to come down to see
it.*

---

One of his colleagues had never spoken to him first before. It had
always been Lytron who opened a conversation. That was rank.

Now that colleague spoke first.

"…Are you all right?" the colleague had asked, he told me later.

"…What do you mean?" he'd said back.

"…No, nothing," the colleague said. And smiled, awkwardly.

Lytron told me the moment had been strangely difficult.

"…I understood, for the first time, what it feels like to be pitied," he
told me. "I never knew, my whole life."

I said nothing to that. I couldn't. I knew that feeling very well. I only
didn't want to hand him that knowledge in that particular moment.

*That was the first time I felt he and I were looking at the same thing,
from some place. The place he had come down to and the place I had
always stood weren't identical, but they were, for the first time, close
enough to see each other.*

---

Something had changed at the market too.

There was a stall he always went to. The owner used to smile and pick out
the best of whatever he had. He'd cut the price, more than once, I heard
later.

I happened to pass that stall one day and ran into Lytron there. The
owner still recognized him, still greeted him. But he no longer offered
his hand first. He simply watched Lytron choose, until Lytron had paid
the full price.

"…It never used to be like this," he said quietly, coming out of the
stall. It sounded less like something said to me than said to himself.

"…What do you mean?"

"…He took the full price," he said. "That's the first time I've paid
full price at that stall."

I didn't know what to say to that. It wasn't unjust. It was, in fact,
exactly correct. He had simply paid the price of the thing.

*I searched his face, that moment, for any sign of resentment. There was
none. He wasn't resentful. He was only bewildered. It was the face of a
man in the moment when something that had been ordinary his whole life
suddenly wasn't ordinary anymore. I already knew that face. I had worn
it myself, my first days in this city.*

"…It's a strange thing to say," he said, "but I don't dislike that man
for it. He's just doing what's ordinary. I was the strange one. I lived
my whole life thinking something that wasn't ordinary was ordinary."

I thought that was the most exact thing he'd said in all these weeks.

---

What changed most was his house.

Lytron's father, from then on, hardly spoke to him at all. Not angry, not
scolding. Only what was needed, said and no more.

"…Do you still eat together?" I asked.

"…Yes," he said. "But no one says anything. You just hear the dishes."

"…That must be hard."

"…Yes," he admitted, plainly. "Much harder than I expected."

*I understood, for the first time, that what Lytron had lost wasn't only
a number. He had lost some place in his own father, too. A price isn't
only ever counted one way. There are prices the Roll never registers.*

---

One day, out at the field, I saw him at the ration office, requesting
something. Borrowing the key to a grain storehouse. Once, his name alone
would have gotten it handed to him instantly.

The clerk asked for paperwork.

"…I'm sorry," the clerk said. "There's a verification requirement now."

Lytron stood there while the paperwork was prepared. A few minutes. It
must have felt long, to him.

I watched from a short distance away. There was no irritation on his
face, no anger. Only the face of someone encountering something unfamiliar
for the first time.

*I remember that moment exactly. He was standing, for the first time in
his life, in a place where he could demand nothing. A place where there
was nothing to do but wait. I knew that place very well. I had stood
there since I was born.*

---

Around then there was a gathering he wasn't invited to.

A summer gathering the young of certain families held every year. It had
no name; people just called it "the gathering." I only heard of it
later. Lytron never told me himself.

"…You didn't go this year?" I asked, after hearing of it from someone
else.

"…No invitation came," he said. It wasn't the face of a man pretending
not to care. It was the face of a man trying very hard not to.

"…Did they leave you out on purpose?"

"…I don't know," he said. "Whether someone left me out on purpose, or
whether the list is just automatically sorted by rank and I fell below
it. There's no one to ask."

*The words *there's no one to ask* stayed with me a long time. The Lytron
of before would have made somewhere to ask. His name itself was standing
enough to ask. Now he had become someone who had to go looking for a
place to ask at all.*

He smiled a little.

"…The strange thing is," he said, "I never even liked that gathering.
Bored every time I went. And yet, being uninvited, I feel strangely hurt
by it."

"…Losing something you never even liked."

"…Yes," he said. "I didn't expect I'd feel that either."

*I learned something from that. Some things are worth nothing to you
while you hold them, and only become a space the size of an absence once
they're gone. Not because of the thing that was there. Because of the
fact that the space is now empty.*

---

A few days later, I watched him ask for something directly, for the
first time.

He'd asked the office to let him see something ahead of schedule. The
next season's ration schedule. It was a document he'd once have received
the moment he asked for it. That had been the sort of seat his was.

"…I'm sorry," the clerk said. "That's released by rank, in order.
Lytron's tier is released in three days."

"…Three days."

"…Yes."

"…Is there no exception," he asked. I felt, in that moment, that he
hadn't yet fully shaken his old manner of speaking. Asking for an
exception was itself proof that he still believed, somewhere, in a world
where exceptions existed for him.

"…There are no exceptions," the clerk said. He looked apologetic, but
nothing changed. "Those are the rules."

Lytron stood there a moment. He seemed about to say something more, and
didn't.

"…Understood," he said. And turned away.

*I watched his back. He wasn't angry. It was the walk of a man who knew
there was no longer any place left for anger. That was the first moment
I felt he was truly standing in the same line as me. The line where a
request is not simply granted. I had lived my whole life in that line. He
was only now standing at its entrance.*

"…Are you all right?" I asked, catching up to him.

"…Yes," he said. He paused, then added, "But it's strange. I've never
once, in my life, heard the word *no*. Not really. Whatever I asked for,
it was always granted. If it couldn't be, there was always another way."

"…And now?"

"…Now *no* is just the end of it," he said. "There's no other way. Just
wait three days."

I said nothing back. I only reached out and touched his sleeve for a
moment, then let go. It was the only answer I had.

*I remember those three days. Nothing extraordinary about them. He
waited three days, and on the fourth he had his paperwork. That was all.
And yet I think those three days were among the longest of his life. For
the first time, he had to watch time pass without being on his side.*

---

I shared some bread with him that evening. Baked from what I'd gleaned
at the corner.

"…What's this for?" he asked. He looked puzzled.

"…No reason," I said. "You looked hungry."

He smiled. Not bitterly this time.

"…It's strange," he said. "Me receiving this."

"…What's strange about it?"

"…I used to be the one giving," he said. "I never knew receiving would
feel so different."

I said nothing to that. I only sat beside him until he'd finished the
bread.

*That day, for the first time, I confirmed once more that there are
things you cannot know without having stood in them. He'd watched me for
more than two years. He'd felt sorry for me, and helped me. But not until
he stood in the place of receiving something himself did he truly know.*

*I was probably the same. I still don't fully know what it's like for
him to stand in the place of giving. I likely never will.*
