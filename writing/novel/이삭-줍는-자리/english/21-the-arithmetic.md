# Chapter 21. The Arithmetic

> _Previously_ — Xene met the administrator. He was not ashamed of his
> own system. "The law is being kept." That sentence was true, and so
> it could not be argued with.

This is a chapter I didn't live through.

Like Chapter 16, this one is pieced together from what Lytron told me directly, much later, and what Mena confirmed. What Lytron saw and thought that night — without his own words, I would never have known any of it.

Every time I write a chapter like this, I ask myself: do I have the right to set down something I never saw. Mena answered that question for me once. "Don't ask about the right. Ask about the honesty." I hold to that standard here as well.

---

The night of the administrator's round, Lytron stayed at the office later than usual.

He couldn't quite explain why, even to himself. He only said that something he'd seen that afternoon kept nagging at him. Me, standing before the administrator. And the question I'd asked. *Why is my standing zero.*

*I was careful writing this chapter. If I wrote that Lytron stayed at the office because of me, this story would look more dramatic than it really was. Lytron told me, later: "It wasn't only because of you. The numbers just happened to catch my eye that day." I take him at his word.*

That afternoon, accompanying the administrator, Lytron had filled out the round's paperwork. "Zone under Article 9, no irregularities." He'd written that line as always — one line, no different from any other item.

But writing it, he found himself going back over the question I'd asked the administrator. *Why is my standing zero.* And the administrator's answer. *The calculation is exact.* Lytron said he'd felt no unease at all, hearing that answer. Not at the time.

*I think this detail matters. Lytron felt nothing strange about it that afternoon. What snagged at him only surfaced that evening. I first learned, through Lytron, that a person's feelings can move on that kind of delay.*

---

That night, Lytron spent a long while looking over the harvest settings screen.

It wasn't within the scope of his job. A zone management assistant had no authority to touch loss-rate settings. But viewing them was allowed. Seeing a number and changing a number were different permissions.

Lytron opened the loss-rate item. Several figures filled the screen. Current loss rate, 0.02 percent. Target loss rate, 0.02 percent. Tolerance range, zone-by-zone breakdowns, a graph correlating machine uptime with loss rate. Numbers, and numbers, and numbers.

He said he sat looking at that screen for a long while. He'd seen it many times before. It was exactly this screen that produced the number he reported to his father every morning. But that night, he said, the numbers looked different than they had before. "It didn't look like numbers anymore. It looked like a fence, holding something in." Much later, that was how he put it to me.

Beside it was a record of past figures. This number hadn't always been 0.02 percent.

---

A decade or so earlier, the loss rate had been far higher.

There had been years in the low 3 percent range. Then, over the course of several years, adjustments had brought it down to today's 0.02 percent. Optimizing machine paths, adjusting harvest speed, changing labor placement. Begun in his grandfather's generation, I was told, and completed in his father's.

*Hearing that number for the first time, a chill ran through me. Three percent. Up to then I'd only known the number 0.02. But there had once been a time when a hundred times that much had fallen at the corner. That hundredfold hadn't simply vanished. Someone had erased it, deliberately, over the span of two generations.*

To one side of the screen was a short summary of the adjustment process. Below the year-by-year graph, a note: "Two-generation zone optimization; registered as a best-practice case within the Harvest." Lytron said he read that sentence over several times. It was the story he'd grown up hearing. His grandfather had first fixed the machine paths; his father had finished the job. And that story had been reduced, on the screen, to a short phrase: "best-practice case." Looking at that phrase, he said, he felt for the first time as if he were seeing, laid out beside a number, the true nature of the thing his family had long taken pride in.

Lytron noticed, at the corner of the screen, a button for adjusting the setting value. He'd never pressed it before. There had never been any occasion to, in his line of work. But that night, he found himself curious what it might do.

---

He hesitated a moment, then pressed the setting value, just to see.

The number didn't change immediately. A confirmation window appeared. "Change the loss-rate setting? Projected harvest yield will be adjusted accordingly." Below it, the projected decrease in yield was shown.

Reverting from 0.02 percent to 3 percent would drop the yield sharply. I won't write the exact figure here. I learned it later, but even now it unsettles me every time I write it down.

Below the confirmation window was another item: "Projected impact on linked indices." Lytron pressed that as well. Numbers unfolded. Zone performance score, family-linked score, and beside them a small arrow. Pointing down. He said he looked at that arrow for a long while. The screen, helpfully, had already calculated exactly how far it would fall. Lytron told me he never once looked that number up again, after that night. Once was enough. He never needed to look again.

*I want to explain why I'm not writing that number down. Not because the number itself doesn't matter — the opposite. If I wrote it, a reader would use it as a calculator. Too much, too little, worth bearing, not worth bearing. I don't want this chapter read that way. What Lytron saw that night wasn't a number. It was a fork in the road. That's the only thing I want to set down here.*

Lytron sat a long while looking at that confirmation window. He didn't press it.

---

*I don't want to write this scene as though Lytron had done something wrong. He did nothing. He looked at a confirmation window and closed it. That's all there was. I've rewritten this chapter more than once, trying not to paint that "doing nothing" as a bad thing.*

Before closing the screen, Lytron ran a few calculations. His family's standing score had, for generations, been tied directly to this field's harvest efficiency. The number his grandfather had built and his father had held. If that number wavered, the whole family's score would waver with it.

"…What does this have to do with our score?" he asked himself, that night. The answer came quickly. It had everything to do with it. Very directly.

His brother's words came to mind. *Keep the numbers right, and everything else falls into place.* Lytron had lived his life believing that. But that night, for the first time, he noticed it was a conditional. Keep the numbers right, and it falls into place — that sentence also meant that if you didn't, it wouldn't. He said he'd never once thought through that second half before.

*I've rewritten this passage several times. There was a temptation to write that Lytron had begun to doubt his brother's words. That would be an exaggeration. He hadn't doubted. He had simply seen, for the first time, that the sentence had another side to it. Seeing and doubting are not the same thing.*

---

That night, Lytron followed the thread of what would happen if he reverted the loss rate, one step at a time.

The yield falls. The zone's performance worsens. His father learns of it. His father asks why. Lytron has to answer. *There's someone who comes to the corner every day.* He already knew, that night, how that answer would sound to his father.

He said he ran that imagined scene through his head more than once. His father asks, "And?" Lytron can't find the next words. "Because I felt sorry for her" sounds too sentimental. "Because of Article 9" would be a lie — Article 9 was already being kept. What he really meant was simpler than either: that it bothered him, that this person sat there at the corner every single day. But that reason looked far too small to justify cutting into the family's standing score.

*I think I saw Lytron most clearly in this scene. He was someone whose calculations moved fast. And his calculations weren't wrong. His family's score really would have fallen. His father really would have asked. That answer really would have sounded strange. What Lytron feared wasn't imagination. It was fact.*

Lytron thought, for a moment, of telling a friend about it. There was someone among his fellow zone-management assistants he was close with. But he had no idea even how to start. *I'm thinking about reverting the corner's loss rate.* The moment that sentence left his mouth, it would no longer be his alone. Someone would ask why, and he would have to answer.

In the end, Lytron told no one at all.

*I understand Lytron in this part. Some thoughts change weight the moment they're spoken aloud. Held privately, they're only thoughts. Spoken, they become something that demands a decision. I don't think that kind of silence is bad in itself. I only think it needs to be recorded, honestly, what that silence ultimately protected.*

---

That night, Lytron closed the confirmation window.

He didn't press the button. He turned off the screen. He got up from his seat and went home.

*Writing this chapter, I don't want to sit in judgment of Lytron. I won't write that he should have pressed that button. I wasn't there in that seat, and I don't know, the way he did, what that seat demanded of him. I only set down what happened. He looked at the window, and he closed it.*

Lytron never opened that settings screen again after that night. Not for some time, at least.

He walked home from the office. The hour was late, and the street was quiet. Water was running behind the wall. A sound he heard all the time — but that night, he said, it sounded unusually loud. The water was only there for the yard's sake, let to run freely for no purpose at all. Lytron thought, for the first time that night, about where that water had come from. Water drawn from somewhere, passing through this yard, running straight out into the ditch, unused.

*Lytron told me this scene himself. His voice dropped a little, telling it. I didn't ask what that meant. Some realizations can't fully be explained even by the person who has them. Some nights leave behind nothing more than this: that the water sounded loud.*

---

The next morning, at breakfast, his father asked as he always did.

"…Yesterday's loss rate."

"…0.02 percent. No change."

"…Good work."

Lytron said no lie in giving that answer. There really had been no change. He hadn't changed anything. But he said that "good work" sounded strange to him that morning, in a way it never had before. He couldn't have said, exactly, what it was he'd done well.

*I've set this breakfast scene down again here to place it beside Chapter 16. Same table, same question, same answer, same praise. Not a single thing had changed on the surface. But something different had passed through the person giving that answer than had the day before. Outwardly, nothing at all had happened. Writing this chapter, I confirm again that this "nothing happening" is the most frequently repeated fact in this whole book.*

---

A few days later, Lytron passed me again on his rounds.

I was sifting the dirt as always. It was a hot day. Lytron paused for a moment, then went on. He said nothing. I had no idea, then, what he knew.

*Picturing that moment now, I find myself wondering what his expression must have been. He only ever told me this much: "I just passed by. There was nothing to say." I believe him. There truly would have been nothing to say. He was carrying a problem with no answer to offer for it.*

---

I want to close this chapter with something Lytron said much later.

"…At the time, I just thought I had no reason to take a loss." he told me, much later. "Strange, thinking back on it now. That I called it a loss. You didn't even have a day's food, and I called it a loss."

I said nothing to that.

"…I thought I was right, at the time." Lytron went on. "Protecting the family was my job. I still can't say that was wrong, even now. Only — I know now that what I closed that night wasn't only a window."

"…What did you close?" I asked.

Lytron was quiet a long while before answering.

"…I don't know." he said. "That's probably why I still keep bringing it up."

*Hearing that answer, I understood that Lytron was still looking for it. I have no intention of handing him that answer. This book isn't written to give it. I only set down, together, these two facts: that he closed a window that night, and that even years later, he still hadn't been able to put that question down.*

*I've rewritten this chapter several times. The first time, I wrote that not pressing the button had been cowardice. Reading it back, I saw it wasn't honest. I've never stood before that button myself. I was someone with nothing to lose. I won't pretend to understand how someone with something to lose hesitates before it — but I won't judge it carelessly either.*

*The night he closed that window, I knew nothing. I came home that day too with half a palm's worth and shared it with Pikra. Two evenings existed, separately, in the same city, the same night. One was an evening of calculation. One was an evening of sharing.*

*Writing this chapter, I keep coming back to that downward arrow. I've never seen an arrow like that. I had no score to fall, to begin with. So I know I have no right to judge how heavy that arrow was. I only leave here, as it was, the fact that someone looked at it with his own two eyes, and closed the window.*
