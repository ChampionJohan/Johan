# Chapter 27. The Reputation Field

> _Previously_ — Mena searched the Roll's reputation field in the
> archive. The Roll wrote what was lost; Mena wrote the choices people
> made in front of that loss. Turning through the list, Mena found an
> entry that quoted her own name. "There's one more thing I have to
> confirm. Whether this matches the sentence I actually wrote."

The next morning, I went to the archive before I'd even drawn the
water.

The door was already open. The old man was outside sweeping.

"…Is Mena inside?" I asked.

"…She's been here since before sunrise," the man said. "That room
faces east, you know. She said she'd come with the light, and here she
is, right along with it."

I went in. Mena sat in the same seat as the day before. But something
was different from yesterday. Papers pulled from her case lay spread in
neat order across the table. In sequence. By date, it looked like.

The skin under Mena's eyes was a shade darker than yesterday's. Not the
face of someone who hadn't slept at all. But not the face of someone
who'd slept well, either.

I stood a moment in the doorway and looked around the room. Morning
light lay thin across the papers from the window. Dust drifted slowly
through it. The room felt far quieter than it had the day before.

"…Were you here all night?" I asked.

"…No," Mena said. "I slept at the lodging house last night. I just
came out early, before dawn."

"…What did you find?"

Mena looked at me and shifted her seat to the side.

"…Sit down," she said. "What I'm about to tell you will take a while."

---

Mena pointed to the papers on the table.

"…These are all things I wrote in different cities," Mena said. "I've
laid them out by date. From yesterday through this morning, I've been
finding and comparing everything in this city's reputation field that
quotes me, one by one."

I tried to count the sheets and gave up. There looked to be more than
twenty.

"…Have you gone through all of them?"

"…No," Mena said. "Only a few. But there's something strange."

"…What?"

"…The wording is all a little off," Mena said. "Not completely wrong.
But always, somewhere, a phrase or two has changed."

"…How does it change?"

Mena laid two sheets side by side. One was old, one relatively new.

I leaned in and looked at them. The old one was handwritten; the new
one had the Roll's smooth printed form. Set side by side, even their
shape was different. One bore the marks of a human hand. The other
looked as if no hand had ever touched it at all.

"…This smooth one is the problem," Mena said. "If a person had copied
it by hand, the one doing the copying would have felt what they were
doing. You have to look at each letter to move it. But this one has no
trace of that."

"…So it wasn't a person who copied it?"

"…I don't know," Mena said. "It could be someone copied it and was too
practiced to feel it. Or it might truly never have passed through a
hand at all. The Roll might have summarized it on its own."

"…Does the Roll do that too?"

"…It does," Mena said. "Shortening a long testimony — the Roll is very
good at that. But shortening and changing are different things. I want
to see exactly where the two started becoming the same thing."

*Listening, I wondered for the first time: whom do you even ask about a
wrong when you don't know who committed it? I never found the answer
to that by the end of this chapter. In truth, the answer never comes
out cleanly across this whole book.*

"…None of this is confirmed yet," Mena said. "I don't want to say
anything too soon. I might be misremembering."

*I found that carefulness puzzling at the time. Mena always spoke with
certainty. That moment alone was unusually cautious. I didn't grasp,
then, that it was because this was something about Mena herself.*

"…Mena," I asked. "Has this happened before?"

Mena's hand stopped.

"…Once," she said. "A long time ago."

"…What happened then?"

Mena glanced out the window for a moment.

"…I'm not going to talk about that now," Mena said. "Let me finish
what I'm confirming first. I don't want the old story to blur what's in
front of me now."

That sounded a little contradictory to me. She'd said the old thing was
similar to what was happening now, and yet she wouldn't talk about the
old thing right now.

*I didn't understand that contradiction then. I do now. Mena didn't
want the old thing to make her prejudge what was in front of her. If
she assumed it was the same shape too soon, she might miss what was
different about it. Mena doubted herself to that degree.*

Watching that carefulness, I felt that the Roll and Mena were exact
opposites. The Roll judged fast. The faster the judgment, the more
precise it was said to be. Mena was slow. The slower she went, the
harder she seemed to be trying to be exact. I wondered, for the first
time, which of the two carried the heavier weight. I found no easy
answer that night, or afterward.

---

Mena found a few more quotations bearing her name. None from this
city — people from other cities. Unfamiliar names scrolled past on the
screen.

"…These people have nothing to do with this city," I said. "So why are
they all in here?"

"…The Roll doesn't only see a person inside this city," Mena said.
"Wherever they came from, whatever they were judged to be before, it
carries that along too. It thinks it can't fully judge a person
otherwise."

"…So that's true for me too?"

"…You came from outside the city, so there's no city record for you,"
Mena said. "But if you ever leave for another city, whatever this city
has written about you will follow you there."

That was the first time I truly felt the size of this system. I'd
thought of the Roll, until then, as something belonging to this one
city. It wasn't. The Roll was like a river running between cities. If
the water in one city clouded, the city downstream clouded with it.

I thought suddenly of the settlement outside. There was nothing like
this out there. What someone said about you didn't follow you around.
People judged each other face to face. They were wrong sometimes, but
the wrongness ended right there. It never crossed into a city and
built up.

"…It's not like this outside," I said.

"…I know," Mena said. "I've spent a lot of time outside too. There's
nothing like this out there. But there's something else instead."

"…What?"

"…When there are fewer people to remember, forgetting comes faster,"
Mena said. "How many people in the world do you suppose still remember
your husband's name?"

Something went cold in my chest at that. Mena hadn't meant it unkindly.
She was only stating a fact. But the fact hurt.

"…The city doesn't forget," Mena said. "Instead, it remembers wrong. I
still don't know which is better."

*That was the first time I felt real fear that day. I'd thought it was
frightening that nothing was left at the corner. This was a different
kind of fear. The fear that when one city goes wrong, the wrong doesn't
stay inside that one city.*

"…Are all the cities connected like this?" I asked.

"…To some degree," Mena said. "Everyone says they're independent
cities. But their Rolls aren't independent from each other, not like
that."

"…Why was it made that way?"

Mena gave a small shrug.

"…Convenience," Mena said. "It's faster for a city to receive what
another city already knows than for it to learn a person from scratch.
The Roll likes fast."

"…It wasn't meant with any ill intent."

"…No, probably not," Mena said. "That's what frightens me more. That
something made with no ill intent at all can produce a bad result."

I turned that over for a long time. I remembered the first thing I'd
felt when I came into this city. The city hadn't driven me out. It had
simply given me nothing. What Mena was describing felt similar. No one
had done anything with ill intent, and yet only the result came out
bad.

*That was the first time I dimly sensed how this world turned. It
wasn't that bad people existed and made bad things happen. No one was
bad, and yet the bad thing made itself.*

---

Mena paused a moment and looked at me.

"…Xene, let's stop here for today," she said. "There's more I need to
check. My own things."

"…Your own?"

"…Something I wrote once, long ago, in another city," Mena said. "I saw
it twisted this same way once before. I thought it belonged only to
that city."

Something caught in me at that.

"…And?"

"…Now I think I'm catching the same scent here," Mena said. "I have to
check to know. A scent alone is nothing."

*I didn't understand, then, why that last line mattered so much. A
scent alone is nothing — I thought it was something Mena was telling
herself. A few days later I would know exactly what that scent was.
And that it wouldn't end at a scent.*

I looked at Mena's face again in that moment. Beneath its calm,
something was pulled taut. Like a thread on the verge of snapping. I
had no way of knowing, then, when it would.

I got up to leave, then sat back down.

"…Mena," I said. "Is there anything I can help with?"

Mena looked at me. Her face showed a little surprise for the first
time.

"…Why do you want to help?" she asked.

I couldn't answer right away. I'd never thought about the reason.

"…I don't know," I finally said. "It just seemed strange that you'd
carry all of this alone."

Mena smiled a little. The first smile of the day.

"…Thank you," she said. "But this is something I have to do alone
right now. It's something I have to confirm with my own eyes, from my
own hand. If there's something you can help with, I'll be sure to ask."

I nodded. And I believed her.

*I didn't know, then, that the promise Mena made — that there would be
something I could help with — would come back to me, much later, in a
very different shape. It wasn't handing over pages. It was handing over
this whole record.*

---

That afternoon, I went to the corner for a little while. Habit carried
my feet there. Nothing was there, as usual.

On my way back I ran into Lytron.

"…You were at the archive today, weren't you," Lytron said. "That
woman's registered. I just confirmed it."

"…Registered?"

"…Mena," Lytron said. "As an outside recorder. It's some old
arrangement, apparently. I only found out about it today myself."

I was a little surprised at that. I only then realized that what
Lytron had said the day before had been true.

"…So it's all fine now?" I asked.

"…It was never really a problem," Lytron said. "I just hadn't checked
it before."

Something complicated crossed Lytron's face. I couldn't quite read it
at the time.

"…Do you know what Mena's looking into?" Lytron asked.

"…The reputation field."

"…Do you know what that is?"

"…A little."

Lytron looked at me for a moment.

"…I should go take a look too," Lytron said. "My name must be in there
somewhere."

"…Are you curious?"

"…No," Lytron said. "It's just, I've never once thought about how my
name is written there."

That struck me as a little odd.

"…Why not? You talk about standing every day."

"…Standing is a number," Lytron said. "I always look at numbers. But
what words sit beside that number — I've never actually read them."

"…Why not?"

Lytron thought for a moment.

"…I assumed it would be good, whatever it said," he said. "Since my
number's high."

I felt something catch at that answer. Lytron had never wondered why
his number was high, what stood behind it. A high number was simply
taken as given.

*I didn't judge that indifference badly at the time. It wasn't ill
intent that kept him from wondering. There was simply no reason for him
to wonder. That day, I saw clearly for the first time what Lytron's
world was. A world where you never have to wonder.*

"…Can I go see Mena about it?" Lytron asked.

"…Go ask her. I think she'll say yes."

Lytron nodded and started toward the archive, then stopped again.

"…Xene," he said.

"…Yes."

"…What's written beside your name?"

I found myself briefly at a loss for words.

"…'Nothing out of the ordinary,'" I said.

Lytron said nothing to that. He just looked at me a moment, then left.

*Watching him go, I wondered whether Lytron felt that sentence as
strange as I did. I didn't know, then. Later I would learn that it
stayed with him that day, longer than I ever guessed.*

---

That evening I stopped by the archive again. Mena was still in her
seat. The papers on the table had grown more numerous.

"…Find anything more?" I asked.

Mena nodded. But her expression was more complicated than it had been
that afternoon.

"…I'll tell you tomorrow," Mena said. "It isn't sorted out yet today."

"…Are you all right?"

Mena looked at me. She started to smile, and couldn't.

"…Trying to be," Mena said.

That answer sounded more honest to me than anything else I'd heard
that day.

Before I left the archive, I turned back once from the door. Mena was
leaning over her papers again. No lamp lit. Just the last of the
evening light. I watched her back for a long while before quietly
closing the door.

---

When I got home, Pikra was waiting at the door. She seemed to have been
waiting for me, later than usual.

"…With that recording woman again today?" Pikra asked.

"…Yes."

"…What are you spending so much time looking at?"

I thought a moment about how to put it.

"…Something Mena wrote a long time ago has apparently turned up
strange in another city," I said. "She's checking on it now."

Pikra's hand stopped at that.

"…Her own words changed?"

"…Yes."

Pikra said nothing for a long time. I could feel that she was
remembering something in that silence.

"…Something similar happened to me too," Pikra said at last.

I looked up at her, startled.

"…What was it?"

"…Something I said once came back to me later, out of someone else's
mouth, meaning something completely different," Pikra said. "No one did
it with a bad heart. It just happened, passed along enough times."

"…What did you do, then?"

Pikra shook her head.

"…Nothing I could do," Pikra said. "I didn't even know who to be angry
at. Not the first person who said it, not the last one who passed it
on. Everyone had only changed it a little."

I thought of what Mena had said that day. *There's no place left to be
angry.* I felt, right then, that this wasn't something only this city
did. Wherever words pass through many mouths, it could happen anywhere.
Only here, it had turned into numbers, and truly moved a person's
place.

"…So how did you get through it, then?" I asked.

Pikra set down the stalks she'd been working.

"…I just lived," Pikra said. "When there's no way to set it right, you
live instead of setting it right. That's the only way I know."

"…Weren't you bitter about it?"

"…I was," Pikra said. "I still am, sometimes. But Xene, carrying
bitterness and being eaten alive by it are different things. I worked
hard not to be eaten."

I held that for a long time. The hearth fire made shadows on Pikra's
face, and erased them, over and over. I saw, in that face, an old
bitterness, and the face of someone who had endured it.

*That night, before sleep, I remembered Mena's hands arranging the
papers in order. Those hands hadn't come apart, even when she'd been
angry. I didn't know then that it was simply how she worked. I would
watch, the very next day, exactly what those hands found.*

I lay awake a long time. Beside me, I could hear Pikra's breathing,
even and steady. I stared at the ceiling and counted through, one by
one, the things I'd heard that day. *Nothing out of the ordinary. A
scent alone is nothing. I worked hard not to be eaten.*

*I felt that those three sentences, though they'd come from three
different mouths, strangely lined up into a single thread. The Roll had
told me I was nothing out of the ordinary. Mena refused to say anything
until she was certain. Pikra had worked hard not to be devoured by her
own bitterness. That night I couldn't say, in words, what those three
had in common. I can now. All three were a fight against jumping too
fast to a conclusion.*

Outside, the wind brushed against the roof. I listened to it and slowly
closed my eyes. Tomorrow, Mena would finish her confirmation. I hoped
the result would turn out well. But some part of me already knew — if
it had been going to turn out well, Mena wouldn't have been so
careful.
