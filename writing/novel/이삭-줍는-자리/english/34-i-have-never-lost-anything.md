# Chapter 34. I Have Never Lost Anything

> _Previously_ — Lytron met Mena. She wanted nothing from him. In front
> of that fact, he was shaken for the first time in his life. "Have
> you ever thought about why," Mena had asked him.

This chapter, too, is a story I didn't live through. But this time it's
a little different.

The two chapters before this one pieced together a day Lytron had
lived. This one pieces together thoughts he carried alone over several
days. I know this is a more delicate thing to do. A day is made of
events, and easy enough to confirm. A thought has no way to be
confirmed. I have filled this whole chapter only with what Lytron told
me himself, later, across several separate conversations. I know it's
possible he remembers it differently than it truly was. Even so, I
believe this much is exact: this is the memory he handed to me.

---

The day after meeting Mena, Lytron spent an ordinary day, same as
always.

He went on patrol, checked the numbers, wrote up his report. At dinner
that evening, his father asked, as he always did, "Anything unusual
today," and he answered, "No," and heard, "Good," in return.

But that night, he told me, he couldn't sleep.

"…It was strange," he said, later. "Normally I would have just gone to
sleep. But that question kept coming back to me. Have you ever thought
about why."

At first he'd tried to brush the question off lightly, the way he had
in Chapter 16. Carry something for one night, and when the next day
brought something to do, set it down. That had always been his way.

This time he didn't set it down.

*I think this difference matters. In Chapter 16, he'd felt something
looking over my entry-approach history, and forgot it by the next day,
buried under some other report. This time he didn't forget. I thought
a long time about where that difference came from. The answer was
simple. That earlier question had been about me. This one was about
himself. A question about someone else, you can forget. A question
about yourself doesn't let go so easily.*

---

For several days, he told me, he turned that same question over from
several angles in his own mind.

*Why did I keep trying to give her something.* At first he thought it
was simple kindness. But he soon knew it wasn't only that. In trying
to give Mena something, he'd also been trying to reassure himself of
something. He'd wanted confirmation that he was a useful person in
that room.

"…I'd always been in a position to give something," he said, later.
"I thought that was just my nature. But in that room, once I had
nothing left to give, I didn't even know who I was."

He followed that thought and began counting through his own life again
from the start.

---

He was a man who was quick with numbers. He was used to thinking in
them. So he laid his whole life out as figures and went through it,
one by one.

From the moment of his birth to now, his family's score had never once
gone down. A number his grandfather had built and his father had kept.
Not a bad year, not an accident, had ever touched that number. Not
when his brother went out on his own, not when he became a zone
management assistant — the number had stayed the same or climbed.

He tried to find some point where he had lost something. No matter how
far back he searched, nothing came up.

*I want to pause here for a moment. I rewrote this passage more times
than any other in this book. My first draft read: "In the end he found
nothing. That was his problem." I struck that line. It was a judgment.
I chose instead to keep only what he actually gave me, word for word.*

---

He said he thought back, too, on the house he'd grown up in.

The house I'd already described in Chapter 16 — so many rooms he'd
never once counted them. The water channel that ran through the yard
purely for decoration. Thinking of that channel, he remembered that on
that night described in Chapter 21, he had already, once, heard that
water differently.

"…That night I wondered, for the first time, where that water even
came from," he said. "And even so, afterward, I never once turned it
off. Wondering about something and actually doing something about it
are different things, it turns out."

He took that channel as one example and looked over his whole life
again through it. All the things that existed not because they were
needed, but simply because they'd always flowed. He tried to count how
many things like that filled his life. He gave up counting. There were
too many.

---

One evening, his brother came from another zone.

Not for any holiday. It was because their father had asked him to come
check in on how Lytron had been lately — something Lytron only learned
afterward. He hadn't known that, that day.

His brother spoke to him as easily as ever. "Just keep your numbers
straight. Everything else follows."

Lytron said that on hearing those words, for the first time, he asked
back.

"…Do you think that's true for everyone?"

His brother paused a moment at the question.

"…What are you talking about?"

"…Xene," he said. "She has no number to keep. She started at zero.
Does that hold true for her too?"

His brother couldn't answer. Instead he said,

"…You've been saying strange things lately."

Lytron said he didn't ask anything further after that. But later he
told me: "I knew, right then. My brother had never once been asked that
question. Neither had I. Both of us had lived our whole lives thinking
those words held true for everyone."

---

A few days after that, he went to see the administrator.

There was no particular business, he said. He just wanted to ask
something.

"…You've said the Roll has never broken the law," he said. "Does that
mean it's never taken anything from anyone, either?"

The administrator looked at him for a moment. The same plain
expression as always.

"…No," the administrator said. "The Roll takes nothing from anyone."

"…Then why is there nothing for some people?"

For the first time, the administrator paused before answering.

"…Never having taken something, and having given something — those are
two different statements," the administrator said. "The Roll only
calculates. Calculation is neither taking nor giving."

Lytron said he stood there a long while after hearing that.

*Hearing this exchange later, an old scene came back to me. Something
Glyke had said in Chapter 1. If someone takes something from you, at
least you can say you were robbed. Just being given nothing at all —
there's nowhere even to say that. I'd heard those words outside, back
then. Lytron heard almost the same words, years later, in the middle of
a city, from a different mouth entirely. Setting these two scenes side
by side, I confirm again how simple this city's secret was from the
very start. Not taking and giving are different things. But to the one
on the receiving end, they feel exactly the same.*

---

"…Do you remember the day it rained?" he asked me once. "The day you
filled a sack for the first time."

"…Yes," I said. It was the day I'd written down in Chapter 24.

"…I remember that day too. But I lost nothing that day," he said. "The
harvest was only delayed. Our family always keeps reserves for that.
One day's delay doesn't take anything away from us."

"…What does that mean?"

"…The same rain fell," he said. "For you it was the first sack you'd
ever earned. For me it was nothing at all. I'd never once thought
about that difference before."

---

From that night on, he started looking for something else. Not what
he'd lost, but a moment where he'd taken a risk.

He thought back to that night in Chapter 21. The loss-rate setting
screen. The night he'd opened the confirmation window and closed it
again. At the time, he'd felt himself standing in front of danger. But
thinking it over again, that hadn't been danger at all. As long as he
didn't press that button, he was in a position to lose nothing. Not
pressing it had always been the safe side. He had stood on that safe
side, and called it, to himself, a struggle.

"…I thought, at the time, that I was weighing something," he told me,
later. "But really, I wasn't weighing anything. I was just choosing
between two safe paths. Press it, and the family score drops. Don't,
and nothing happens. There was never a dangerous path for me to begin
with."

---

He spent several more days holding onto that thought.

Then one day, he thought back to a phrase he'd carried for a long
time. *I haven't done anything wrong.* The thing he'd said to himself
in Chapter 16.

He looked at that phrase again. It was true. He truly had done nothing
wrong. But that day, for the first time, he set another phrase beside
it.

*I have never lost anything.*

He said he read those two sentences over, back and forth, for a long
while. At first they seemed like two separate facts. One a proud fact,
one simply a lucky one. But at some point, he saw that they were,
actually, one and the same.

---

"…That day I understood, for the first time," he told me, later. He
spoke very slowly, choosing each word. "It wasn't that I hadn't done
wrong. It was that I never had to. Every time I made a choice, the
right one and the one that benefited me were always the same one. I
thought that was my character. It turns out it was my position."

I said nothing for a long while, hearing that. The sound of the
machines came faintly from the field. The sound felt louder to me that
day than it usually did.

"…What do you mean?" I finally managed.

"…Someone like you," he said, "loses something every time you choose
what's right. Every day. I never lost anything, choosing what was
right. So the fact that I've done nothing wrong isn't something I paid
a price for, the way you did. I was simply never tested."

---

Hearing that, I remembered, too, my husband's old words. *What you
spill isn't wasted. It's left behind.* I felt his words and Lytron's
were pointing, from different directions, at the same thing. My
husband, having little, had known what it meant to leave something
behind on purpose. Lytron, having so much, was only just then learning
that he had never once, truly, left anything behind at all.

*I felt the temptation to bind these two into a single sentence. But
that would have been too easy a conclusion. I chose instead to lay
them only side by side. Not stitched together.*

---

*I write this moment more carefully than any other in this book.*

*I know that Lytron didn't say these words to punish himself. I found
no room to hate him, hearing them. He wasn't saying he'd been a bad
person. He was only seeing, for the first time, that his never having
been a bad person and his never having paid a price both came from the
same place.*

*I thought longest, of any chapter in this book, about how to write
this one. I didn't want this chapter to turn Lytron into someone bad.
Nor did I want it to let him off entirely. In the end I chose to carry
over exactly what he gave me. Both things were already inside it. He
had done nothing wrong. And he had just seen, for the first time, what
that absence of wrongdoing had been made of.*

---

He did nothing right away, after that.

I once thought that strange. Having seen something so large, shouldn't
something have changed at once? He answered that question this way.

"…Seeing something large doesn't mean you can do something about it
right away," he said. "For those few days, I just carried the fact
around with me. It followed me everywhere. Looking at the field,
looking at the numbers, going home."

"…Was it hard?"

"…It was hard," he said. "But strangely, I didn't want to go back to
before."

Around that time, he said, he started staying alone in his office each
evening, opening that same screen from Chapter 21 again. The loss-rate
setting. 0.02 percent. The confirm button beside it.

"…You opened it again?" I asked.

"…Yes. A few times," he said. "But I looked at it differently than
before."

"…How differently?"

"…Before, I thought that button decided whether I'd lose something,"
he said. "Now I know. That button is my first chance to actually lose
something. I still haven't pressed it. But now I know why I haven't,
and it isn't the same reason as before."

*I held onto that answer for a long time. Some kinds of knowing don't
move a person right away. They only settle inside, quietly, adding
weight. I wanted this book to set down the fact that Lytron carried
that weight alone, for several days, before anything else happened.*

I want to end this chapter here — not as a chapter with an answer, but
as a chapter where the shape of a question changed completely. The
Lytron of Chapter 21 had asked, "If I press this, do I lose something."
The Lytron of Chapter 34 held a different question. *Why have I never
once lost anything.* I think the distance between those two questions
is the longest road he walks in this book. And he never took a single
step to cross it.
