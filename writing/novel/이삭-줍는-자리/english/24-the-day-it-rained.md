# Chapter 24. The Day It Rained

> _Previously_ — Lytron asked whether she couldn't go somewhere else,
> instead of here. Xene answered that she wasn't there because she had
> nowhere else to go — she was there because Pikra was. Lytron couldn't
> understand the answer, but he didn't call it strange either.

The rains came late that year.

They usually ran late anyway. I watched this city's sky for weeks that season. Day after day without a single cloud. The wheat only ripened better for it. Good sun means good wheat, Damia told me.

"…Rain would actually be a disaster." Damia said. "If it hits grain that's already fully headed, the weight bends the stalks over. Then the machines can't harvest it."

I let the remark pass without much thought. Being afraid of rain sounded strange to me. Outside, rain had always been welcome. Here, even rain was a variable to be calculated.

---

The clouds rolled in late that afternoon.

At first no one thought much of it. A cloud passing through on a summer afternoon was common enough. But this one didn't pass. One whole side of the sky darkened fast. The wind suddenly shifted direction. The smell of dust gave way, all at once, to the smell of wet earth.

I knew that smell. Outside, it came before a late frost too. The smell of something on its way.

Somewhere across the field, something like a horn sounded. I didn't know what it meant. Damia heard it and shot to her feet.

"…Machine-stop signal." Damia said. "It means rain's coming."

---

It wasn't long before the rain began falling in heavy drops.

The first few were large. They left round marks in the dry dirt. Then, all at once, it poured. I had never seen rain arrive that suddenly. Outside, rain always gave some warning first. Here it came with none at all.

The whole field turned loud in an instant. People running. The harvesters stopping. I had never seen those enormous machines come to a halt so abruptly. Things that had always moved at the same speed, with the same sound, simply went still, all at once.

*I remember that moment by its sound, even now. The machine noise cut off, and rain filled the space where it had been. That was the first time I heard this city fall so quiet. A field that had always been full of something running was, for a few seconds, nothing but the sound of rain.*

---

People were shouting, running everywhere.

"…Clear the front rows first! Whatever's already cut!"

Someone yelled that. I didn't know who. Someone in charge, it seemed. The workers surged in one direction at the call. There were sheaves already cut but not yet loaded onto the carts. If the rain got them, they'd spoil, I was told.

I stood in the middle of that chaos, not knowing what to do. I wasn't someone called on for work like this. The unregistered weren't mobilized for urgent tasks. I just stood there in the rain.

Far off, I spotted Lytron. In a raincoat, a terminal in a waterproof sleeve in his hand, running among the workers. Giving directions, checking things, running again. He didn't seem to have any room to notice me. I didn't call out to him either.

*I heard later how busy Lytron had been that day. It was a crisis that struck directly at the whole field's yield. It must have been the most frantic hour of his day. I caught a glimpse of his face, briefly, in the middle of that chaos. None of his usual composure left in it. That was the first time I learned that he had a face like that too.*

---

I walked toward the corner, letting the rain fall on me.

No particular reason. It was simply the place I always went. There was nowhere else to go. Pikra was home in the cottage that day. Once the rain stopped I'd have to head back there anyway, and it seemed natural enough to pass by the corner on the way.

As I neared the corner, I saw something strange.

The ground was yellow.

---

Ordinarily, the corner's ground was the color of dirt, apart from a few handfuls of trampled stalks. Not that day. Grain lay scattered across the rain-soaked earth. Whole stalks of wheat lay flattened too. For a moment I thought I was seeing it wrong.

I knelt down and touched it with my hand. It was real.

*I've gone back over that moment hundreds of times since. The weight against my fingertips. Up until then, whatever I'd touched at that corner had always been one or two grains, at most half a palm's worth. That day, wherever my hand landed, there was something there.*

I didn't understand why, at first. A moment later, hearing the sound of people running toward the front of the field, I guessed.

---

That day's final harvest stretch had happened to be the section nearest the corner.

The machine was halfway through that stretch when the rain signal stopped it, I learned afterward from Lytron. Half the cut wheat lay right where it had fallen. On an ordinary day, the machine would have finished crossing and taken every one of those grains with it. That final, precise pass was exactly what made the 0.02 percent loss rate what it was.

That day, the pass never happened.

Everyone was too busy scrambling to save the sheaves already cut and waiting to be carted off. The rows near the corner, still standing uncut, flattened by the rain, had been pushed entirely off that day's list of priorities. That stretch had never counted toward the Roll's harvest tally to begin with. There had been no reason to protect it.

*I confirmed this more than once. I asked Lytron, and later the administrator too. Both gave me the same answer. No one had left that section on purpose. No one had thought of me. It had rained, the machines had stopped, people had rushed to save what was urgent, and the corner had never been on that urgent list. That was all there was to it.*

---

I sat there a long while, letting the rain fall on me.

At first I gathered it grain by grain. Then I realized there was no need. I set my sack down entirely and began sweeping it up with both hands. Whole stalks and all — I could thresh them at home later.

I could see the sack filling. I had never, in my whole life, seen a sack fill that fast. Or — I had, once. Back at the outer settlement, gleaning a field others had already harvested, with our own hands. But that had been a different kind of filling. That field had been marked, then, as ours. This one wasn't.

*I didn't cry, filling that sack, strangely enough. I'd thought I'd been waiting a long time for a moment like this. But when it finally came, no tears came with it. Instead my hands moved faster. I had no idea how long the rain would last. All I thought was that I had to gather as much as I could before it stopped.*

For a long while I was at it like that, when another hand appeared beside mine.

It was him. I didn't notice when he'd arrived. Soaked through with rain, wordlessly gathering fallen stalks beside me. I didn't know what zone he'd been assigned to that day. He might have been called in to help carry the urgent sheaves. But at that spot, it was just the two of us.

"…Isn't this a place they need you more?" I asked. I had to raise my voice over the rain.

"…This is needed too." he said, briefly. He didn't explain what he meant.

After that, we worked in silence. Rain ran down our faces. Our fingers went numb with cold. But I don't think my hands had ever been this busy and felt this good, before that day.

*I've turned that brief "this is needed too" over in my mind a long time. I couldn't ask, that day, what it meant. I'm still not certain, even now. I only know this much: that he spent his time that day on work no one had assigned to him. Whether that went beyond the chance the rain had made, or whether he was simply someone conscientious who couldn't walk past grain lying in plain sight — I still don't have that answer either.*

---

In the middle of the rain, I looked up, once.

Far off, Lytron was looking this way. Only for a moment. He was still running back and forth, and that glance lasted no more than a few seconds. I didn't know what was in that gaze. There was no chance, that day, to ask.

*I asked him about that look much later. Lytron said this: "That day I'd actually forgotten you were there at all. Then I happened to glance over. You were filling a sack. I didn't think anything, in that moment. I just thought — good, I'm glad. That's all there was to it." I believe that answer, precisely because it wasn't any bigger than that. For him, that's really all it would have been.*

---

The rain stopped toward evening.

The machines didn't run again that day. They'd start up again the next morning, I was told. People carried what wet sheaves they could salvage back to the storehouses. I stood off to the side, holding my sack.

It was more than half full. I kept feeling its weight the whole walk home. I carried it in my arms rather than over my shoulder. I didn't want to let go of it.

On the way to the cottage, I ran into Damia. Her eyes went wide at the sight of my sack.

"…You gathered all that today?"

"…Yes."

"…The rain did you a favor." Damia said. She meant nothing much by it.

"…Yes." I said. "It did."

*Saying that, I knew it was exactly right. It was the rain, and nothing else.*

---

Pikra was standing outside the door when I arrived. She seemed to have come out to catch the air, after the rain.

Pikra looked at my sack. She couldn't say anything at first.

"…It rained." I said. There was no other explanation I could find.

Pikra reached a hand toward the sack, stopped, reached again. Her fingers trembled. I didn't stop, then, to think whether it was her age, or something else.

"…Let's go inside and talk." Pikra said.

That night, for the first time, we emptied the whole sack out inside the cottage and sifted through it together. The two of us sat there by a single lamp for a long while. Sorting grain from stalk, picking out the flattened straw, separating out what could really be eaten.

---

By the time we finished, it was past midnight. In front of us sat more grain than I had ever seen in one place.

Pikra looked at it for a long while.

"…How long has it been, since I saw this much." Pikra said. I couldn't tell if she was speaking to me or to herself.

I didn't answer. Instead I went back, one by one, through what I'd seen that day. The machines stopping. The direction the people ran. No one so much as looking toward the corner. All of it had happened because it rained.

"…Will there be more of this tomorrow?" Pikra asked.

"…I don't know." I said. "I don't think it'll rain tomorrow."

Pikra nodded. She asked nothing further.

---

*I write this night as carefully as anything in this book. I was glad, that day. Truly glad. We ate our fill for once, and there was enough left to divide over the coming days. But alongside that gladness, from the very first, I set down something else too.*

*This was not mercy.*

*No one had left that wheat behind for my sake. The administrator hadn't broken the law that day, either. No one had changed the loss-rate rule. Lytron hadn't done anything for me that day, not really. The Roll had been exact that day too. Only the sky had stood outside the Roll's arithmetic. The rain never asked about standing. The rain had never, to begin with, calculated any such thing.*

*I said none of this to Pikra that night. She probably knew it too. We simply gave ourselves over to dividing the grain. Some truths, even known, have to be set aside — for one night, at least.*

---

The next day, it truly didn't rain.

The sky cleared again. The machines ran again. I went out to the corner at the usual hour. The ground was dirt-colored again. It was hard to believe that so much had been there, in that same spot, the day before. A few handfuls of trampled stalks. Same as it always was.

I sat down and began sifting the dirt, fingers laid flat as always. The same motion as the day before. Only, the day before, that motion hadn't been needed.

*That morning I confirmed, all over again, that this city had given me nothing. What I'd received the day before hadn't come from the city. It had come from the sky. And this city would go on giving me nothing, tomorrow, the day after, until the sky chose to do that again. I set this fact down without resentment. There was no one to resent. The administrator had been right. The law had been kept. Yesterday and today, both.*

A few days later, I ran into the administrator near the office. Unable to hold back my curiosity, I asked.

"…That day it rained — some wheat was left at the corner. Does that count toward the loss rate?"

The administrator thought a moment.

"…No." the administrator said. "That's pre-harvest scattering. It happened in a section that hadn't yet been logged as harvested, so it doesn't figure into the loss-rate calculation. It's recorded separately, under weather exceptions."

"…So that day just gets treated as if it never happened?"

"…The record stays." the administrator said. "But the loss-rate figure holds. 0.02 percent. That day, and today."

I didn't ask anything further after that. There was nothing left to ask.

*I had to set that conversation down here. For a while, I'd hoped that the day it rained had shaken the Roll's number, even a little. It hadn't. That day remained, in this city's record, nothing more than a weather exception. The number never moved at all. Only hearing that answer did I fully understand how much of what I'd received that day had come from outside this city.*

---

That sack was the biggest topic in our house for days afterward.

Pikra stretched it out, using it sparingly. Not using it all in one meal, dividing it instead across nearly ten days. For those ten days I was less hungry. Once they'd passed, it went back to the way it had always been.

*I remember those ten days as something special. But the real reason they were special is that they were an exception. I never met rain like that again — not, at least, within the span of time this book covers. I think that fact is one of the most important things to know about this city. The good things came from outside this city, from places it couldn't calculate. What was calculated inside this city was always 0.02 percent.*

---

I close this chapter here.

The story turns a little different from here on. I'm soon to meet a woman. The one who writes things down. She'll tell me a great many things I never knew, about this city, and about myself. I'd thought, until then, that I had been carrying this record alone. I learn, at that point, that this wasn't so.

*I'll write that meeting in the next part. For now I set down only this: the sack, on the day it rained. It was not mercy. I knew that, and I was grateful anyway. The two could exist together. I learned that, that day.*
