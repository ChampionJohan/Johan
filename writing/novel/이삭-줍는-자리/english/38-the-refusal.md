# Chapter 38. The Refusal

> _Previously_ — A relative was found who could register Xene — far
> better positioned than Lytron to do it without cost. The husband's
> cousin, Medon. The morning we went to meet him, Pikra took Xene's hand
> and said, "You're already mine."

Medon's office was clean.

Clean wasn't quite the word for it. There was no dust. I had never seen a
room without dust before. Outside, a room was always something that came
with dust attached.

He rose from his seat to greet us. Tall, well-dressed. Older than Lytron,
younger-looking than the administrator.

"…Mrs. Pikra." His voice was gentle. "It's been a long time."

"…Medon."

That was all, from the two of them. No embrace, not even a hand taken.
The distance between them struck me as strange. In the Roll they were
close kin; in that room, they looked like two strangers meeting for the
first time.

*I didn't fully understand that distance then. I understand it now. I saw
with my own eyes, in that room, how different the Roll's closeness is
from a person's.*

There were three chairs in the room. They'd known we were coming and set
them out ahead of time. I took that for kindness. Thinking back now, it
wasn't kindness — it was procedure. Set out three chairs for guests. That
was all it was, a rule.

A painting hung on one wall. A field of ripe wheat leaning in the wind. I
looked at it and thought, briefly, of our settlement's field. It never
lay down that way, like a picture. It was always a little thin somewhere,
patchy.

*There was no corner in that painted field. I don't know whether the
painter had simply not drawn it, or thought there was no need to. I
couldn't take my eyes off that painting the whole time we were in the
room. I learned later that it's the most common painting in this city. A
field with no corner. No one finds that strange.*

---

He offered us seats. We sat.

"…I've heard the situation," he said. "The administrator explained it in
detail. You're Xene, correct?"

"…Yes."

He looked at me for a moment. Not an unkind look. Courteous, if anything.
That made it stranger.

"…I understand the circumstances," he said. "It's an unfortunate
situation."

*The moment I heard that word, something felt wrong. Unfortunate is a
word for something already over. I still believed, at that point, that
this thing hadn't ended yet.*

"…And how have you been, Mrs. Pikra?" he asked. He didn't go straight to
the matter at hand.

"…Getting by," Pikra said.

"…I heard about your husband. And your sons." His voice held something
genuine. That confused me more. That a person could be genuinely sorry and
do nothing at all — until then, I hadn't known such a person existed.

"…And your mother? Is she well," Pikra asked.

"…She passed away two years ago."

The room went quiet for a moment. I felt something old passing between
the two of them. Twenty years, thirty years I knew nothing of, sitting in
that room along with us.

---

He opened a screen and turned it toward us.

There was a number. His standing. I still couldn't tell, looking at it
again, anything more than that it was large. Beside it, a smaller number
— the amount required for transfer.

"…At this level," he said, pointing to the screen, "it's true that it
would barely register for me. I checked."

My heart jumped at that.

"…Then—"

"…But I'm not going to do it," he said.

The room went quiet.

---

"…Why?" I asked. I felt I'd asked something I shouldn't have, and yet the
word had come out first.

He looked at me. He didn't grow angry, didn't look uncomfortable. He
answered in the same ordinary tone as before.

"…My score would be cut," he said.

I didn't understand the words. Hadn't he just said, himself, that it
wouldn't even show?

"…You just said it wouldn't show—"

"…It wouldn't show," he said. "Not to anyone else. But being cut is being
cut. I have never, not once, had my score go down. Not since I was born."

The words didn't sound like a boast, to my ear. His face wasn't the face
of a man boasting. It was the face of a man stating a fact.

"…Why should I be the first case?" he asked. It seemed half addressed to
me, half addressed to himself.

*I had no answer then. I have none now. There was, in truth, no reason it
had to be him. Nothing in the Roll required it of him. It was purely a
door the system had left open, and opening it had never been anyone's
obligation.*

"…I don't understand," I said again. "If the amount is that small, why
is this such a difficult conversation?"

Medon smiled, for the first time. Not a scornful smile. Just a bitter
one.

"…It's difficult because it's small," he said. "If this were a request
for my entire fortune, I'd have already refused and been done with it.
This is small enough that no one could blame me for it. Which means I
have no choice but to blame myself."

I felt I might understand that, and didn't want to.

"…Are you saying a small amount weighs more than a large one?" I asked.

"…No," he said. "The weight is the same. It's only that in front of a
small amount, there's nowhere to hide. In front of a large amount, you
can say it couldn't be helped. In front of a small one, that excuse
doesn't hold."

*I have never forgotten that answer. He knew exactly how thin the ice was
that his refusal stood on. And knowing it, he stood on it anyway.*

---

Pikra had said nothing this whole time. She looked at the screen, at
Medon, back at the screen.

"…Your father," Pikra said, finally, "wouldn't have done this."

Something in Medon's face shifted, for the first time.

"…Mrs. Pikra," he said. "My father was a man who took losses. That's why
our family ended up the way it did."

"…So that's why your mother split off."

"…Yes," he said. He wasn't ashamed of it. "I believe my mother was
right. I don't live that way."

I felt something solid in that answer. A solidity that couldn't be
argued with.

*Pikra said nothing more. I understood, at that moment, completely, why
she had been so careful these past several days. She had known Medon
would say something like this. She had known his mother. And she had
known the son took after her.*

"…Your mother," Pikra said again, her voice gentler now, "was protecting
you. I don't resent her for it. But you're not the one who needs
protecting anymore. Your score is already large enough. Larger than
large."

Medon lowered his eyes for a moment.

"…Do you know when 'large enough' gets decided?" he asked. "I don't. My
father probably thought his was large enough once, too. Then he started
taking losses, and it went down and never stopped."

"…That's a different matter."

"…You would think that, Mrs. Pikra," he said. "I don't. I was taught
that protecting what you have is a habit it's never too late to start."

Hearing that, I understood he really wasn't a bad man. He was only
living the way he'd been taught to live. It was only that what he'd been
taught and what I'd watched, for two years, pointed in completely
opposite directions.

Medon closed the screen. He looked at each of us in turn.

"…I'll tell you one thing," he said. "I could have blocked this
procedure entirely. If I'd asked the administrator. When the person with
priority claim refuses, there are ways to delay how quickly it moves to
the next in line. I didn't do that."

"…What difference does that make?" I asked. I don't think I expected the
words to bring any comfort.

"…Maybe none," he said. "It only means I won't stand in the way. I can't
do this myself. But someone else might."

I took those words, at the time, for one more excuse. I hear them a
little differently now. He really hadn't locked that door — though he
could have. It seems like nothing. It wasn't nothing. There are people in
this world who could block a thing and don't, and people who never had
the power to block it in the first place. I learned that day not to
weigh those two the same.

As we rose to leave, Medon looked once more at the paperwork the
administrator had given us. The standing-transfer procedure form. He
studied it for a long moment.

"…The procedure itself is simple," he said. It sounded almost like he
was speaking to himself. "One signature on the application, and it's
done. The Roll calculates the rest. There isn't even a separate
verification step."

"…Why does that sound strange, the way you say it?" I asked.

"…Because it is strange," he said. "That something this large ends in a
procedure this small. I don't know what the person who built this was
thinking."

I didn't understand those words then. I understand them now. He was, in
that moment, already weighing exactly what he was refusing. How far
apart the size of the procedure was from the size of the outcome — he
had seen that before we had.

*One signature. I remembered those words for a long time. I had no idea,
then, that they would come back to stand in front of me a few days
later.*

---

"…Don't misunderstand me," Medon said. His voice softened again. "I don't
dislike either of you. I'm sorry for this. Genuinely."

"…And yet you won't help," I said.

"…Helping and giving up what's mine are different things," he said. "I
can help in other ways. I could add to your rations, if you need it. But
my standing — that's mine. I've kept it my whole life."

*What was hardest was that nothing he said was wrong. He didn't tell a
single lie. He showed no hypocrisy. He was genuinely sorry, and genuinely
wanted to help in other ways. Only one thing he wouldn't do — let his own
number fall.*

*And the Roll found no fault in him. It couldn't. He had broken no law.
He had simply stood in front of an open door and not walked through it.*

The administrator, in a moment like this, would have stayed even. Medon
wasn't even. He kept offering explanations, all the way until we left the
room. That he'd send more rations if we needed it, that we should send
someone if we ran short of fuel in winter, things like that.

I tried to take those words as kindness. If I hadn't tried, I think I
would have been angry instead.

*Looking back now, the rations and the fuel were real. He sent someone
every winter after that, without fail. I don't want to dismiss that. He
did exactly as much as he'd decided he could do. He simply never stepped
one pace past that line.*

Before we walked out the door, Pikra called to him once more.

"…Medon."

"…Yes."

"…I don't resent you," Pikra said. "But remember this. You did, today,
what you believed was right. I know that. But right and —"

Pikra stopped. She never finished it.

"…Good?" Medon finished for her. He knew the word too.

"…Yes," Pikra said. "That."

Medon said nothing. His silence was his answer.

*I remember that silence exactly, even now. He didn't argue. Not because
he had no argument, I think, but because he didn't want to make one.
Some people, told they were wrong, neither agree nor disagree — they
simply listen. I think that, too, is a kind of honesty.*

---

We left the room.

The sun outside was strong. I stood for a while, blinded.

"…Are you all right?" Pikra asked.

"…Yes." I lied.

We walked without speaking. Past the gate district, toward the field.
Somewhere along that road, I stopped and asked.

"…You said standing transfer's been around a long time."

"…That's right," Pikra said.

"…And no one's ever used it," I said. "How many Medons must there be in
this city?"

Pikra didn't answer.

*That was the day I first saw the real shape of standing transfer. It
wasn't a locked door. It was a door wide open. All it required was one
thing — the will to give up what was yours. And that will was not
something the system could manufacture.*

Instead of going to the field, we passed through the market for a while.
Pikra wasn't buying anything. I think she just needed somewhere to walk.

The market was as it always was. People buying wheat, buying cloth,
children running. No one looked at us. Whatever had just happened to us,
whatever words had passed between us in some office of this city, left
no trace in that market.

*That was strangely comforting, somehow. That the world doesn't stop
just because I have. And at the same time it was the most frightening
thing of all. That the world doesn't stop just because I have.*

Pikra spoke suddenly, walking.

"…I always knew he'd grow into that." She wasn't pointing toward the
market lane, but I knew she meant Medon.

"…How?"

"…He was quick with numbers, even as a child," Pikra said. "If someone
gave him something, he took it. If he borrowed something, he paid it
back. Exactly that much. No more, no less. I thought that was cleverness."

"…And now?"

Pikra stopped for a moment.

"…It is cleverness," Pikra said. "But I've learned it's a different
thing from being a good person. I thought the two were the same. When I
was young."

*I turned that over many times. Being quick with numbers, and being good.
Keeping the law, and being righteous. I kept seeing those pairs pull
apart from each other in this city. The Roll was one of them.*

A stall gave off a familiar smell. Roasted barley. I stopped in front of
it for a moment.

"…Hungry?" Pikra asked.

"…No."

"…Then why'd you stop?"

I had no answer. The smell was simply familiar, and I'd stopped for it.
Standing in front of something familiar felt like safety. That day, it
was the only safety I had.

---

Lytron came out to the corner of the field that evening, once he heard
the news.

I was standing there alone. Not really there to glean. Just there to
have somewhere to sit.

"…How did it go?" he asked. His face already guessed the answer.

"…He won't do it."

Lytron nodded. He wasn't surprised.

"…Aren't you angry?" I asked. "You heard him in that room. It really
would have cost him nothing."

"…I'm not angry," he said. After a while, he added, "What he said wasn't
wrong, either. It's his. That's his."

Something caught at me in that. I felt I could almost tell why he was so
quiet, and yet not quite.

*I didn't know it then. What he was already thinking, in that moment. I
despaired in front of Medon's door. Lytron was looking at something
else. That another door was still open. His own.*

---

That night, I told "him" what had happened. It wasn't the hour to still
be at the field, and yet he was still there.

"…I was refused," I said.

"…I know," he said. He didn't say how. He sometimes already knew things
no one had told him.

I sat down beside him. The field was darkening. Evening insects were
singing.

"…I really thought, right up until today, that something would come of
this," I said. "Not because I deserved it, but just because I'd come
this far — I thought something would."

"…That's not a strange thing to think," he said.

"…But it didn't."

"…No," he said. He didn't try to console me. That, somehow, was the
comfort.

"…He wasn't even a bad person," I said. "That's what makes it hardest.
If he'd been bad, I could at least hate him."

He nodded.

"…A refusal you can't hate is the one that lasts longest," he said.

I almost asked what he meant, and didn't. He always spoke that way, and
I always understood, only later.

"…So what do I do now?" I asked. I didn't know myself who the question
was addressed to.

He didn't answer. He only gathered a few stalks from his hand and set
them down beside me. A little less than I needed, as he always did.

*I thought, that day, it was simply his habit. I think differently now.
Maybe he wasn't refusing to give me an answer that day. Maybe he already
knew the answer would come from somewhere else.*

I got home late that night. Pikra was already lying down. I thought she
was asleep, but her eyes opened when I came in.

"…You're late."

"…Yes."

"…Are you all right."

I lay down beside her instead of answering. After a long while, I said,

"…I'm going out tomorrow too. To the field."

Pikra made a sound of laughter in the dark. A silent one.

"…You're really something." There was something more behind those words
that she never said aloud.

*I didn't know it then. That within a few days, Lytron would already have
made up his mind. I only thought about going to the field again the next
day, and the day after that. It was the only way I knew.*
