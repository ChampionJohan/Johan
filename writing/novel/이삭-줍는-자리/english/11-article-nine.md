# Chapter 11. Article Nine

> _Previously_ — From an old man she meets in the city, Xene hears how
> people without standing actually live. No one drives them out. No
> one simply gives them anything. Even the patrol was kind. The old
> man mentions "an article in the law," then puts it off for another
> time.

I did nothing much, those three days. Pikra and I set the room in
order. I baked bread from the flour we'd been given. I learned where
to draw water. Becoming a city person, it turned out, meant a
succession of small, ordinary tasks like these.

Pikra grew quieter over those few days. That worried me, but I didn't
ask. I was already learning, by then, that asking also takes a kind of
strength.

*Those three days looked like nothing happened, on the outside. But
the whole time, inside, I was turning the old man's words over and
over. An article in the law. I pretended to forget it. I never did. At
night, closing my eyes, it was the first thing that came to mind, and
in the morning, opening them, it was still there.*

Three days later I went back to that alley.

I told myself, at the time, that I hadn't gone on purpose. That I'd
just been walking and my feet happened to go that way. Looking back
now, that was a lie. I went on purpose, wanting to know what that
article was.

The old man was in the same spot. Same wall, same posture. As if not
three days but a single hour had passed.

"…Back again," the old man said. No surprise in it.

"…Yes." I sat down beside him. "…Will you tell me about that
article?"

The old man looked at me for a moment. Something gauging in his eyes.

"…In a hurry, aren't you," the old man said. And laughed.

---

"…It's called the corner clause," the old man said. "Ever heard of
it?"

"…No."

"…When they harvest, they're supposed to leave the corners of the
field," the old man said. "By law. A very old law. They say it's been
there since this city was founded, though I don't know that for
certain."

"…What does *leave the corner* mean?"

"…That the designated corners of a field — the four corners — are left
unreaped," the old man said. "And they're not supposed to gather the
fallen grain, either. It isn't the harvesters' share."

That struck me as strange. At the settlement, not gathering what fell
was simply how things were. Here, they'd made it a law. That felt
foreign.

"…So whose share is it, then?"

The old man looked at me and said, slowly,

"…Ours," the old man said. "The share of those with no standing."

---

Something inside my chest lifted a little, in that moment. The first
feeling like that since the zero I'd been handed at the gate.

"…Is that true? So if I go there, there's actually something?"

"…There's supposed to be," the old man said. His voice sank a
little. "By law."

I let that phrase — *by law* — catch in me, but not enough to weigh it
down, not then.

"…Why is there a law like this?"

The old man thought for a moment.

"…I don't know the real reason either," the old man said. "I only
know it's a rule from way back. Not to reap it all clean, at harvest.
Something about the ones who have leaving a little for the ones who
don't. Something like that, I think."

"…Was it ever abolished?"

"…Not as far as I know," the old man said. "This city's remade and
changed a hundred other laws in that time. That one stayed. Maybe it's
just too old for anyone to think of touching."

"…Strange, isn't it. Why wouldn't an old law like that just
disappear."

The old man shrugged.

"…Maybe because no one loses anything by leaving it be," the old man
said. "Now that the Roll handles the reaping, that clause hardly
changes anything, whether it's there or not. So no one bothers taking
it out."

I let that pass, lightly, at the time. I didn't yet see the distance
between *no reason to remove it* and the real reason being that it
left nothing behind anyway.

*Now I know. The old man already knew the answer, then. He simply
didn't say it to me straight out. I think he wanted me to go see for
myself. He knew there's a difference between being told a thing and
seeing it with your own eyes.*

*I remember that moment for a long time. It was the first thing I
could call hope, since coming into this city. A very small hope. And
still, that smallness was large, to me, that day. Up to then I'd only
thought in terms of receiving — receiving, or not receiving. But
Article Nine told me not of receiving, but of a place to go and glean.
The difference didn't feel large, then. Now I know it is. Gleaning
carries a share that's mine — the walking there, and the bending
down.*

---

"…Where do I go, then?" I asked.

The old man gestured roughly in a direction.

"…All around the city is field," the old man said. "Somewhere in
there are the marked-off sections. Stakes driven in, they said. I
haven't been myself, not in a long while."

"…Is it at every field, this spot?"

"…Probably. There's more than one field, so probably more than one
spot," the old man said. "Start with the near ones. I heard there are
a few within half an hour's walk of the wall."

I gauged that distance. Half an hour meant a place I could go every
day.

"…Can I go every day?"

"…There's no law against going every day," the old man said. "Whether
there's anything there, every day — that I don't know."

I let that too — *what's there, I don't know* — pass, lightly. I was
already picturing tomorrow morning.

"…Why don't you go?"

The old man paused a moment at that question.

"…No point," the old man said. His voice went low.

I didn't quite catch what he meant, then.

"…No point, how?"

"…I've been a few times. Not much there," the old man said. "The law's
the law, but these days the machines do the reaping. When it was done
by hand, there was always something dropped. These days…" He let the
words trail off.

"…It was different, before?"

The old man looked into the distance.

"…Before — when I first came here," the old man said. "You'd go to
the corner and there really would be something. Stalks, grain. A day's
worth, off that. Because a person cutting with a sickle naturally
spilled some."

"…And now?"

"…Now the machines reap," the old man said. "A machine doesn't spill.
No reason to. I've watched it with my own eyes. What's left in the
corner shrinking, year by year. First a sack. Then half a sack. Then a
handful. These days…"

The old man stopped there.

"…These days what?"

"…Go see for yourself. Better than my telling you," the old man said.

*Writing this exchange down now, I understand why the old man didn't
finish it, that day. Some facts, heard in words, become only
information. Seen with your own eyes, they become something else. The
old man didn't want to hand me information. He wanted me to know it
myself.*

---

That comment troubled me, but the hope that had just risen in me was
larger.

"…I'll go anyway," I said.

The old man looked at me. Not a face trying to stop me. Not a face
egging me on, either.

"…Go," the old man said. "Go see for yourself. I just don't have the
strength for it anymore. That doesn't mean you shouldn't go."

"…If it's a place set by law, it's no trouble for someone with no
standing to glean there, is it?"

"…None at all," the old man said. "That's ground set aside as ours
from the start. Even the Roll doesn't touch that far. It's the law."

I felt easier, hearing that. At least going to that place, itself,
couldn't be stopped by anyone.

*I didn't really hear the old man's* no point *that day. I only heard
what I wanted to hear. There's a law. There's a place. That place is
ours. Those three sentences were enough for me. I let every condition
attached to them pass, that day.*

---

"…What's it called, that law? The proper name," I asked.

The old man thought for a moment, searching his memory.

"…I've heard it called Article Nine," the old man said. "I don't know
if that's exactly right. I've only heard the people working the
fields call it that."

Article Nine. I said the number over to myself.

"…I'll remember that number," I said.

The old man smiled a little at that.

"…You've got the face of someone who could live on that alone, for
months," the old man said.

*I didn't understand what that meant, then. Now I do. The old man
already knew, that day, how long I'd cling to that single hope.*

---

On my way home, I passed through the market. In front of a bakery I
saw again the person carrying a basket I'd seen before. I worked up
the nerve to approach.

"…Excuse me, I have something to ask."

The person looked at me and nodded.

"…Have you heard of the corner clause?"

"…Oh, that." No surprise showed on their face. "It's in the law, yes.
The corners of a field, unreaped."

I felt relieved all over again, that it was real.

"…So it's all right for someone with no standing to glean there?"

"…By law, yes," the person said. "That's how it's always been, I've
heard."

"…Is it still kept, these days?"

The person looked at me a moment and made an odd face.

"…It's kept, sure," the person said. Their tone was strange. "The
corners get left, for certain. I've seen the markers myself, passing
by."

"…That's a relief, then."

"…Yes, well," the person said. Without saying more, they went on
their way.

*I took that* well *for nothing but a vague answer, that day. Looking
back now, that person hadn't told me everything either, just like the
old man. It's kept — but whether there's anything inside it is a
different question. Everyone in this city seemed to know that
difference. I was the only one who didn't.*

---

I hurried home that night. I went back and forth for a long while
over whether to tell Pikra.

In the end I didn't. I still don't quite know why. Maybe this hope was
too small and fragile, and speaking it aloud felt like it might break
it.

Pikra glanced at me as she prepared dinner.

"…You look different today," Pikra said.

"…Do I?"

"…Yes. Something happen?"

I hesitated a moment, then shook my head.

"…I just walked around a bit."

Pikra didn't press further. Instead, she put an extra ladle of soup
in my bowl.

"…Whatever it was," Pikra said. "If it's good, it's good, and if not,
it's not. I can tell, mostly, from your face."

I smiled a little at that. "…Then why not ask?"

"…If I ask, you'll have to make up an answer," Pikra said. "You'll
tell me, when it's time."

*I didn't fully understand that kindness, then. Now I know. Pikra had
already learned not to rush me. Probably raising her sons, or maybe
just on herself, she'd learned that lesson first.*

*Looking back, that was a wrong call, on my part. I went on carrying
things alone like this, often, after that. Good news and bad both.
Pikra didn't know, that day, what I'd found. What she would have said,
if she had, I still wonder sometimes.*

Before sleep that night, I kept repeating the words to myself. The
corner clause. Article Nine of the law. The share set aside for us.

I pictured that place in the dark. I didn't know exactly what it
looked like. I only imagined a corner of a field, untouched, quiet
wheat standing there. The wind would blow, and it would sway too. Just
imagining that sway made me feel, for the first time in days, that my
body had grown lighter.

*Looking back now, that imagining was naive. I don't regret the
naivety. There are days a person can only bear by imagining naively.
That was one of those days for me.*

I thought again of what I'd heard at the gate. *We don't expel
anyone.* At the time, that had sounded only bleak. That night, it
sounded different. Not expelling might also mean not stopping me from
standing in that corner. If the Roll gave me nothing, I wanted to
believe, at least, that no one could take away that small place the
law had set aside.

*I remember that night precisely, even now. It was the first night in
this city I fell asleep waiting for something, rather than afraid.
Fell asleep on expectation instead of fear, for the first time. The
next morning, I made up my mind to go to that field before anything
else.*

*I set down here that this was the first of a resolve that would
repeat, many times, through this book. I would go on making this same
resolve, again and again, after that. Some days the resolve was right.
Some days it wasn't. That night, I didn't know which it would be. I
only looked forward to tomorrow.*
