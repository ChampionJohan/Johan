# Chapter 32. Where There Is Always a Little More

> _Previously_ — Mena taught Xene the five rules of writing: write
> only what you've seen, write that you don't know when you don't,
> write action and not the heart, don't write everything, write on
> paper. That night, Xene wrote down a day with her own hand for the
> first time.

From that day on, I wrote a few lines every evening.

At first I didn't know what to write, so I wrote anything at all.
Today's weather. Three sentences Pikra had said that day. How much I'd
gleaned at the corner. I tried to record that last one exactly — not
"gleaned a lot," but "a palmful of three," that kind of thing. The way
Mena had taught me.

*I didn't know, then, what this habit would grow into. I only wrote so
as not to forget. But writing exactly, it turned out, was something
that eventually made me see things I'd been passing over without
knowing it.*

I kept that paper behind a wooden frame on one side of the cabin. I
took it out from the same spot every night. The first few days, the
sentences ran long. Soon they shortened. Just as Mena had said, once I
kept only what was worth keeping, that happened on its own.

I never dated the page. Instead, I put the single surest thing about
that day first. It rained, or it didn't. Pikra coughed, or she didn't.
Something was gleaned at the corner, or nothing was. I had no idea,
then, that this order would become, later on, the thread by which I
found my way back into a day. At the time it was only habit.

*I keep that old bundle of pages beside me now, writing this book.
Some pages are stained; on some the ink has run, barely legible. But
without those pages, I couldn't have written this thirty-second
chapter. Memory alone would never have given me a sentence like "over
two days I confirmed it." Memory blurs things into "it happened
several times."*

---

Damia laughed the first time she saw the paper.

"…What are you writing so hard at?" Damia asked. We were working side
by side at the machine.

"…What happened that day," I said.

"…A diary?"

"…Similar, but different," I said. "I don't write my feelings. Just
what happened."

Damia tilted her head at that.

"…What's fun about that, if there's no feeling in it?"

I couldn't answer right away. I'd thought the same thing myself, at
first. But writing it for a few days, I began to see things that only
came into view once the feeling was left out.

"…I'm not writing it for fun," I finally said.

Damia didn't press further. She just gave a shrug and went back to her
own work. I wasn't hurt by that. I already knew, by then, that some
people would understand this and some wouldn't, and that neither side
was wrong for it.

---

Within the week, I noticed something strange.

It was the numbers. Setting side by side the days I'd written "a
palmful of three" against the days I'd written "a palmful of five," I
saw that the days with five always came from the same spot. His spot.

At first I thought it was coincidence. The field was wide, and the
machines' paths shifted a little from day to day. It wasn't strange
for one side to leave a little more some days, the other side other
days.

But writing it down for several more days, it was too consistent to be
coincidence. I flipped back through the pages. A week's worth of
entries.

*Monday: his spot, a palmful of five.*
*Tuesday: the other side, a palmful of two.*
*Wednesday: his spot, a palmful of four.*
*Thursday: the day he didn't come, and his spot, too, a palmful of
two.*

My hand stopped at that last line. On the day he wasn't there, nothing
extra was left at his spot either.

*I thought back, at this point, to something further back — Chapter
19, "The One Who Gleans Beside Me." I had already half-noticed this
once, back then. I'd taken it for coincidence and let it go. Why hadn't
I asked further, then? Thinking of it now, the answer is simple. Back
then I didn't yet know how to write exactly. Noticing something by
instinct and confirming it with numbers are different things. Instinct
can be let go of. A number can't.*

---

I decided to test it.

The next day, I went early and sat at the spot he always took. He came
a little later than usual, and, seeming unconcerned, went and sat
somewhere else — a few steps from where I'd been, not his usual spot.

That evening, I counted the palmfuls. The place I'd sat, his old spot,
was the same as always. But the new spot he'd taken that day — the
place where he'd actually been — had more than usual.

*I was certain, in that moment. It wasn't the place that was good. It
was the place he was in that became good.*

I didn't want to be certain from just one day. I tried the same thing
the next day. This time he took his seat first, and I went and sat far
off to the side, somewhere he wouldn't be likely to sit. The result was
the same. Where I'd been was no different from usual. Where he'd been
had a little more than usual.

That night I wrote it in my paper.

**"Wherever he sits, that spot always leaves a little more than the
others, even when the spot itself changes. Confirmed over two days."**

I read that sentence twice. I hadn't mixed in a single guess, and yet
what it said was entirely clear.

On the second day I tested it, Lytron happened to pass by on patrol.
He stopped and scanned the field — the machines' path, the line at the
scales, the placement of the workers. The same routine as always.

"…Anything unusual today?" he asked.

"…No," I said.

I nearly laughed, right then. Just a few steps away, I was running my
own experiment, and none of it registered with him at all. He saw only
the numbers he always saw, the routes he always saw. What I was
watching that day wasn't numbers. It was a person. The same field, and
he and I were looking at entirely different things.

*I found that fact frightening for the first time, right then. There
was so much to see in that field. And yet everyone saw only what they'd
been trained to see. Lytron had been trained to see numbers. I was
only just then learning to see a person exactly.*

---

By the end of that week, I told Mena what I'd found.

"…I found something strange," I said. "There's always a little more
at his spot. I confirmed it over two days."

Mena stopped and looked at me.

"…Did you ask him?" Mena asked.

"…No. Not yet."

"…If you ask," Mena said, "an answer might not come. Know that going
in."

"…Why?"

Mena thought a moment.

"…Some answers aren't a matter of can't. They're a matter of won't,"
Mena said. "I've worked out that difference over a long time. Someone
who can't answer, their face shakes. Someone who won't, doesn't shake.
They just go still."

I didn't fully understand that, then. A few days later, at the ditch,
I would see exactly what she meant.

---

That night before sleep, I thought of something from long ago.

My husband, teaching me to swing the sickle, the first autumn. I was
scolded then for reaping too cleanly. "What you spill isn't wasted,"
he'd said. "It's left behind."

I'd taken that as a joke, at the time. But since finding that a little
more always turned up where he sat, that memory kept overlapping,
strangely, with this. What my husband had spilled, and what this man
left behind. I couldn't be sure, at the time, whether they were the
same thing.

*I never wrote that thought down on paper that night. It was a guess.
But now, setting it down in this book, I've chosen to keep the guess
rather than erase it. This is a thought I actually had, that night.
Mena's rule was to separate fact from guess — not to pretend a guess
never existed.*

---

I hesitated for several more days.

Mena had taught me not to write everything. She'd never taught me not
to ask. I knew those were different things. Writing, and asking.

*Even now, I'm not entirely sure why I hesitated so long. Maybe I
wanted to hear the answer, and at the same time didn't want to. Some
questions, once answered, you can't go back from. I think I already
knew that, on some level.*

Once, I sat down beside him meaning to ask, and in the end said
nothing at all, just sifting the dirt. That night I wrote in my paper,
"Still didn't ask, today." A short line. But it took longer to write
than any other line I'd written.

In the end, one evening, I followed him down to the ditch, where he'd
gone to wash his hands, and asked.

"…Why does a little more always turn up at your spot?"

I asked it plainly, without dressing it up. Days I'd spent choosing the
question, and what came out, in the end, was the shortest sentence of
all.

---

He stopped washing his hands and looked at me.

It wasn't an angry face. I thought I knew that face well. But that
day it was none of the faces I knew. He didn't smile. That struck me
as strange. Usually, when a question was hard to answer, he smiled.
That day he didn't.

He kept shaking the water from his hands for a long while. He didn't
seem to be preparing an answer. He was just shaking off water. Only
the sound of the ditch went on for a time.

"…I don't know," he said at last.

"…What does 'I don't know' mean?"

"…It means I'm not sure either," he said.

That didn't sound like a lie to me. But it didn't feel like the whole
of it, either.

"…So did I just imagine it, then?" I asked.

He looked at me for a moment. Then turned his eyes back to the ditch.

"…I didn't say you imagined it," he said.

I thought about pressing further. But his shoulders had gone a little
stiffer than usual. Not from anger. I couldn't put a name to what it
was, then.

"…You don't have to answer if you don't want to," I said.

"…It isn't that I don't want to," he said. "There's just nothing to
say."

---

I understood there was nothing more I could draw out of that answer.

He wasn't angry, and he didn't leave. He just finished washing his
hands and, as always, picked up his basket and walked back toward the
field. Watching him go, I felt a strange kind of relief. Watching
someone avoid an answer was somehow easier to bear than hearing a lie.

*That evening I wrote down the question and the answer exactly as they
were. What his face had looked like, how many words he'd said. I
wanted to write "he was hiding something." I wrote it down, and crossed
it out, several times. It was a guess. In the end, I only left this:*

**"He said, 'I don't know.' He also said, 'I didn't say you imagined
it.' He didn't get angry. He didn't smile."**

Writing that sentence, I felt, for the first time in my body, why
Mena's fifth rule mattered. Written down on paper, I could take that
face back out and look at it again, later. Memory faded. Paper didn't.

---

That night, Pikra saw me writing and asked.

"…Him again?"

"…Yes," I said. "There's always a little more where he sits. I asked
him. He wouldn't answer."

Pikra set down the stalks she was working.

"…Didn't he get angry?"

"…No. He didn't smile either."

Something in that made Pikra's face turn thoughtful.

"…That's strange," Pikra said. "I've seen him smile plenty of times."

"…That's why it seemed strange to me too."

"…Someone who smiles their way past things is covering something up
with the smile," Pikra said. "But not smiling means—"

Pikra stopped there.

"…What does it mean?"

"…Either there's nothing to cover, or there's nothing they can cover
it with," Pikra said. "One or the other. I don't know which."

I held onto that a long time.

---

*I still didn't know, that night, why a little more always turned up
at his spot. I can't say I fully know it even now. But since that day,
I've come to tell a little more clearly when he was answering and when
he was avoiding. It was a small kind of learning. But that small
learning became, later, the eye that let me recognize something much
larger.*

*By that point I had already been watching two different kinds of
silence for a while. One was the silence Lytron showed in Chapter 16,
talking about his family — not silence from having nothing to say, but
from having no need to say it. The other was the silence he showed at
the ditch that day — silence with something to say, and a choice not
to say it. I hadn't thought to set those two side by side, back then.
Only in writing this book do I come to know how different a weight
those two silences carried.*

The next morning, I went out to the corner as usual. He was there in
his usual spot too. As if yesterday had been nothing at all, he gave a
single nod, and I nodded back. We sat side by side, as always, sifting
the dirt.

I found that calm strangely welcome. Some people, once you ask them
something awkward, avoid you afterward. He didn't. He hadn't given me
an answer, but he hadn't taken away my place beside him either.

*That evening I wrote this down too. "He came to the same spot the day
after the question, as always." I think this one line, in its way,
says more than that day's "I don't know" did. Avoiding an answer and
avoiding a person are different things. He only ever did the first.*
