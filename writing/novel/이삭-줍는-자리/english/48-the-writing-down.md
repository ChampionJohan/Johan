# Chapter 48. The Writing Down

> _Previously_ — "He" left. I asked him, at the last, whether he'd
> dropped the grain on purpose. He smiled and turned the question aside.
> "What's spilled is something the gleaner discovers." I never saw him
> again after that.

Mena left the next day.

I saw her off at the gate. The same spot where I had said goodbye to
"him" a few days before. The coincidence struck me as strange.

"…Same spot," I said.

"…There's only one gate," Mena said. Plainly. "Everyone comes in through
here, and everyone goes out through here."

The words sounded less simple to me than she meant them.

I looked up at the gate. I tried to count how many times I had passed
through it. Once, coming in. Once, going to my first standing hearing.
Once, on my way to meet Medon. And now I was standing here a second
time, seeing two people off.

*That gate never felt unfamiliar to me, somehow. When I first stood
before it, I thought this city was a place meant only to push me away. I
know now. That gate was both a door that pushed away and a door that
took in. The same door had always done both, at once.*

---

Mena didn't have much to carry. A few articles of clothing, a bundle of
papers, some writing tools. Her whole life fit inside that much.

"…You're taking all those records with you?" I asked.

"…Of course," Mena said. "This is my work. Write it, carry it, write
some more."

"…It looks heavy."

"…It is heavy," Mena said, and smiled. "But I can't leave it behind. If
I left it, I'd just be someone who passed through. What's written down
is what turns passing through into staying."

I held that in my heart. I didn't yet know, then, that Mena's own name
meant *to remain.* When I learned it later, I would come back to this
moment.

---

We sat a while on the bench.

"…There's something I wanted to ask before you left," I said.

"…What is it."

"…What you found in the reputation field," I said. "Records that came
in from other cities. Did you confirm all of it?"

Mena nodded.

"…I confirmed it," she said. "There was more than I'd expected. Lines
I'd written were being quoted in several cities, each time slightly
differently. The direction was always the same. *No explanation* became
*no repentance.* Silence became guilt."

I had heard this before. Hearing it again, it was still heavy.

---

"…Can you fix it?" I asked.

Mena shook her head.

"…No," she said. "No city is breaking the law. Every one of them
believes it's quoting its own records exactly. I never found a place to
correct it."

"…Then what will you do?"

"…Keep writing," Mena said. "There's no other way. I can't erase a
false quotation. But I can leave one more true record."

*I think I understood that, fully, for the first time that day. Mena
wasn't someone who fought. She was only someone who went on writing. I
keep learning that those are two different things, writing this book.*

---

"…Can I ask one more thing?" I said.

"…Ask."

"…These cities," I said, carefully. "Do you think they're all
connected? Seeing how records move between them this way?"

Mena didn't answer for a long time. I felt, the longer that silence
stretched, that something large was approaching.

"…Yes," she said, finally. "I've come to think so."

---

"…And before?" I asked.

"…Before, I wasn't sure," Mena said. "I traveled through many cities for
a long time. Each one had its own AI, its own rules. A ledger, scales,
a roll. Different names, work that differed a little from place to
place. I thought they were simply different cities, each facing a
similar problem in its own way."

"…And now?"

"…Now it feels like more than that," Mena said. "A line I wrote in one
city shows up, twisted, in exactly the same way, in another city's
precedent. It's too precise to be coincidence. I still don't know
exactly what it is. But—"

She paused for a moment.

---

"…But what?" I pressed.

Mena took a single sheet of paper from her bag. The last thing she'd
written, she said.

"…I wrote this this morning," she said. "I wanted to show you first."

I took the sheet from her.

---

A single line was written on it.

**"I have come to think these cities are all one city."**

I read the sentence several times over.

*I set this sentence down here, exactly as it was. I didn't fully
understand, that day, why it mattered so much. I still don't fully
understand it, even now. I know only this. Mena never once wrote this
thought down, in all the years and cities she'd traveled through. And
here, in this city, on a bench near our house, she wrote it for the
first time.*

---

"…What does this mean?" I asked. My voice shook.

"…I don't know exactly, myself," Mena said. "I only wrote down what I
feel. I can't prove it. But I couldn't go on without setting this
feeling down, any longer."

"…Aren't you afraid, writing it?"

Mena looked at me.

"…I'm afraid," she said, honestly. "But not writing it was more
frightening. Because if it's forgotten, it never happened."

Something raced in my chest at that. It was the same reason I had once
found for myself, without her ever teaching it to me.

---

"…What will you do with this page?" I asked.

"…Carry it," Mena said. "To the next city. And if I find something more
there, I'll write it beside this."

"…What about me?" I asked. "What should I do with this record?"

Mena looked at me for a long time.

"…You keep writing," she said. "What you lived, what you saw. That's
why I came here. To teach you that. Did you know?"

I started to say I hadn't, and realized, saying it, that I had.

---

"…Can I do this well?" I asked.

"…Doing it well isn't what matters," Mena said. "Being honest is what
matters. I didn't teach you how to write well. I taught you how to write
honestly."

"…Are those different?"

"…They're different," Mena said. "A well-written record moves the
reader. An honest record makes the reader believe it. I think the
second one lasts longer."

---

Mena stood.

"…I have to go now," she said.

We stood facing each other for a moment. I'd prepared words for this
moment, and forgotten every one of them.

"…Thank you," I finally managed.

"…And you," Mena said. "You're the fastest learner of anyone I've
taught."

"…What did I learn fastest?"

Mena smiled.

"…How to ask," she said. "A recorder isn't someone who knows the
answers. A recorder is someone who keeps asking. You were that, from
the start."

---

Before Mena left, the administrator came briefly to see her off, too.

I was a little surprised by that. I'd never seen the administrator see
anyone off outside the office before.

"…You came," Mena said.

"…A recorder isn't someone I meet often," the administrator said. "I
wanted to ask you something before you left."

"…Ask."

"…How did you write me?" the administrator asked. "In this record."

Mena looked at him for a moment.

"…Exactly as you are," she said. "A man who keeps the law precisely.
No more, no less."

The administrator nodded. His expression barely changed.

"…That's enough," he said. "I don't want to be written as more than
that. Not as a good man, and not as a bad one. I only did my work."

*I've kept that brief exchange a long time. The administrator was the
most frightening person in this entire book, and, at the same time, one
of its most honest. He never worried how he'd look. He only wanted to be
exact about what he'd actually done. Even now, I can't easily bring
myself to hate that honesty. He was never, not once, a bad man in this
book. And I can't write that he was a good one, either. I've decided to
remember him simply as an exact man.*

He bowed his head briefly to us and went back to the office. His
retreating figure, and "his" from before, and soon Mena's — I was about
to watch all of them, that day.

---

She walked toward the gate. I watched her go for a long time. The same
road he'd walked a few days before. A different person, the same road,
the same dust.

*I stood at that gate that day and thought of the people who had come
into this city and the people leaving it, side by side. Xene, who had
entered as Xene and stayed as Xene. Mena, who had come as Mena and left
as Mena. He, who came without giving a name and left without giving one.
I thought about how each of the three of us had come to this city, and
passed through it, in our own different way.*

*I thought, too, that of the three of us, I was the only one who stayed
in this city. Mena went on to the next city. He went to some other
field. I stayed. I don't think that's because I was somehow less than
the other two. I was only, simply, the one whose place this was. Not
everyone has to stay in the same place.*

---

That night, I began writing this record in earnest, for the first time.

I had jotted a few lines before, the way Mena had taught me. That night
I began again, from the start. From the sentence about the outside — how
I didn't know that word until I turned sixteen.

*My hand shook, writing that first sentence. I didn't know, until then,
whether I had any right to write down what I'd lived through. After
Mena left, I had to find the answer to that question myself.*

---

Pikra watched me writing and asked.

"…What are you doing."

"…Writing," I said.

"…Writing what."

"…Our story," I said. "From the outside, on."

Pikra came and sat beside me.

"…Why are you writing it," Pikra asked.

I could answer that right away. I'd already known the answer for a long
time.

"…Because if it's forgotten, it never happened," I said.

Pikra said nothing to that. She only took my hand once, and let it go.
As she had, long ago.

---

I went on writing this record, after that.

I wrote of Lytron, slowly recovering some of his standing. He never
fully returned to what he'd had before. He didn't mind. "I learned I
didn't need that tier," he told me once.

I wrote of Pikra, still called Pikra. She kept that name to the day she
died. I never tried to change it again.

I wrote of the corner, still there, still holding what it held. Three
percent. Not a large number. And every year, exactly, someone gathered
something there.

---

Years have passed.

I am writing this final chapter a good while after that year. Pikra
passed away a few years ago. Pikra, to the very last. I had that name
carved onto her gravestone. I never considered another.

Lytron still lives in this city. His family standing never fully
recovered. He says, often, that he doesn't mind. I took that for comfort
at first, and came, in time, to understand it was simply true. He works
in the office now. Explaining the standing-transfer procedure to people
who want it. No one assigned him to that post. He asked for it himself.

"…It's strange," he told me once. "I became the person best able to
explain it. I was the last person to understand it."

I think that's the closest thing to an answer he ever found, in this
city. Not a complete answer. But he no longer carries that question
alone.

---

I set down honestly, closing this book, that some things I never found
answers to.

When Lytron asked, *So why wasn't that enough,* I had no answer. I still
don't.

Whether "he" spilled the grain on purpose, I never did learn. He
deflected, smiling, to the end. I still don't think that smile is the
exact answer. I only know that the smile has stayed with me longer than
any answer would have.

The last line Mena left — that these cities are all one city — I still
don't know exactly what it means. I only set the sentence down here.
Someone, someday, reading this record, might find the answer I
couldn't.

I take that line out sometimes, at night, even now. I still have the
copy Mena gave me. If these cities really are all one city, I wonder. A
city where the ledger was, a city where the scales were, and this city,
where the Roll is. If people in every one of those places once stood
before their own system, under its own name, do those systems, in the
end, point to one thing? Or was it the nameless one who walked quietly
behind all those systems who was, himself, the one thing? I never found
the answer to this question either. I won't find it inside this book.

---

I changed too. My standing score is now far larger than what Lytron
once gave me. Years of working in this city, teaching people, forming
ties with those I met at the corner — the number grew naturally, out of
that. I look at it sometimes, and think of the number that first stood
there. The number that barely cleared the threshold. I think of that
first number far more often than I think of what I have now.

Medon still lives in this city too. We run into each other, now and
then, at the market. He always greets me with courtesy. I do the same.
Neither of us has ever brought up what happened that day, in that room.
I don't think we need to, anymore. He did, that day, what he believed
was right. I walked through a different door. Both things remain true,
at once.

---

Not once, in this book, did the Roll break the law.

I have confirmed that fact many times over. When my standing was set to
zero, at the gate. When Medon refused. When Lytron paid the price. The
Roll was always exact. I learned, in this city, for the first time, how
frightening exactness can be.

But I know one more thing now. Exactness was not everything. Inside an
exact Roll, one person gave up what was theirs, and another did not.
The Roll received both, equally, in the same silence. The difference was
never the Roll's making. It was a person's.

---

I mean to close this record here. Closing it doesn't mean everything has
been resolved. I only mean, in saying this, that I have written this
far.

I began this record with the sentence that I had been a stranger. I can
add a line to that sentence now. A stranger, and a guest. Standing in an
unfamiliar place, and also a place I had, all along, been meant to be
received into. I will carry both of those, all my life, together. I
learned from Pikra that one does not erase the other.

I think now, writing this last line, of something my husband once said
to me, long ago.

"…What you spill isn't wasted. It's left behind."

I understand that now, exactly. The value Lytron gave up. The grain "he"
may have spilled. The record Mena carried, writing it down across so
many cities. All of it was something spilled. And what is spilled
doesn't disappear. It stays somewhere.

I hope this record becomes something like that, too. A record written
with a spilling heart. So that someone, someday, standing before their
own field where nothing falls, might be able to glean it.

I will go on writing, even after this record is finished. As Mena did. I
don't yet know what the next story will be. I only know this now.
Whatever comes, I will write it down. Because if it's forgotten, it
never happened. And some things are too large to become things that
never happened.

I close this record with that sentence. There is no answer. Only this,
written honestly, as far as it goes.

*(The End.)*
