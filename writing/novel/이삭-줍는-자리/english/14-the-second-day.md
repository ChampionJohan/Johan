# Chapter 14. The Second Day

> _Previously_ — Xene learned why nothing was left at the corner of the
> field. The Roll had optimized the harvest until the loss rate was
> 0.02 percent. And that it was no one's fault.

The next day I went out again.

I could write a reason here. It would sound convincing on the page. But what I actually thought that morning wasn't convincing at all. I simply opened my eyes, sat up, and put on my shoes. The thinking came trailing slowly along behind.

*Writing this book, I keep finding myself checking that order. People assume the reason comes first and the action follows. I lived through the reverse of that, too many times over. My body got up first, and the reason caught up with it later. On some days the reason never came at all.*

Past the door there was a road. I had it memorized within a day. Past the well, through an alley hemmed by a low wall, past three grain silos, and the field began. The silos always had their doors shut. I tried not to imagine how much was piled up inside them. That didn't work well either.

On the way to the corner I always stopped in the same spot to retie my shoelaces. Some days I stopped there and retied them even when there was no need. I learned then, for the first time, that habit can arrive before reason does.

There was a low stone at that spot. I would sit and catch my breath there. Its surface was worn smooth — which meant plenty of others had sat on it before me. I often wondered who they'd been.

---

Pikra saw me leaving. She said nothing.

The third day was the same. I woke before dawn, drank a mouthful of water, and went. Pikra pretended to be asleep. I knew it was pretense. She must have known that I knew. We never spoke a single word about it.

*I still can't explain that silence. Anyone watching would have thought we were indifferent to each other. We weren't. Pikra didn't know why I went out, and neither did I. When two people who don't know say it aloud, they end up lying to each other. So we didn't say it.*

Instead Pikra did something else. Before I left, she always had a bowl of water waiting. She never said anything about it. I'd simply wake to find the bowl full. I never once caught her getting up to fill it. She must have risen before me.

*I'm writing about that water bowl in this book for the first time. I thought it was nothing at the time. I don't think that now. When Pikra couldn't find words to stop me, she left water instead. That was the whole of what she could do, and I took it.*

---

By the fourth day, I knew there were others who went out around the same hour.

An old man was sitting on the path to the corner, ahead of me. A sack lay across his knees, empty.

We didn't greet each other. We just happened to walk the same direction, and at the corner, we each took our own place. He sat in one corner as if it were the seat he always took.

*I learned his name later, but I won't write it here. What matters in this chapter isn't who he was, but the fact that he was there. I wasn't strange for being the only one. There were others who came, every day, already knowing there was nothing there.*

From then on I started counting how many came to the corner. On the busiest days, five or six. They scattered themselves as though each had a fixed seat. Few spoke to one another. Everyone kept their eyes on their own patch of dirt.

I thought that was strange. People in the same situation might be expected to help each other, but no one did. I understood later. It wasn't that there was nothing worth helping with. It was that there was so completely nothing, that no one could face the others.

*I'd been told that poverty binds people together. I only half believe that now. Poverty binds people when there's still something, however little, to share. When there's truly nothing left to share, poverty scatters them. I learned that at the corner.*

---

On the fifth day, I timed it for the first time.

Half an hour to walk from home to the corner. Another half hour standing there sifting the dirt. Another half hour to walk back. An hour and a half spent, and for that hour and a half, what I gained was usually nothing at all.

I did the arithmetic and asked myself what use it was.

No answer came.

Outside, I had never timed anything that way. Work was something you did until it was finished. You went out when the sun rose and came in when it set. Counting the hours you'd spent didn't matter. Here, for the first time, I turned my time into a number. An hour and a half. From the moment I made that number, I began to price that time. And I came to see that the price was traded, always, for nothing.

*It took time even to stop doing that arithmetic. Once a person starts measuring, they want to keep measuring. I kept timing myself for several more days after that. Then one day I stopped looking at my wrist. My body seemed to understand, before my mind did, that not timing it made the loss hurt less. There were more and more things, around then, that my body knew before my head did.*

*Looking back now, I think the question itself was wrong. I'd already decided it had to be useful before I asked, and that's why no answer came. I was doing something else entirely then, something other than use. I still don't fully know, even having finished this book, what that was. But I need to set down two things together — that I found no answer to that question that day, and that I went out again the next day regardless.*

---

On the sixth day, a little rain fell.

Not much. Just enough to wet my clothes. I went out anyway. The corner looked no different from any other day. Trampled stalks, dirt, an occasional broken grain. Only the color was a little darker for the wet.

*Standing in that rain, a thought crossed my mind. If the rain got heavier, the machines wouldn't be able to come in. Wouldn't something be left, then? I didn't hold that thought long. The rain stopped soon after. I forgot it.*

*I'm setting this thought down in advance. A day comes, later, when it actually happens. On that day I remember this sixth day again.*

I came home soaked, and Pikra held out a dry cloth. I took it and dried my hair first.

"…You'll catch cold." Pikra said.

"…I'm fine."

"…Stop saying that." Pikra said. Her voice wasn't angry. "You never look fine when you say it."

I laughed. Pikra laughed a little too.

*I remember that brief laugh. Laughter had grown scarce between us by then. That was the first one in days.*

---

On the seventh day, I saw a stranger far off in the field for the first time.

He was standing beside a harvester, looking something over. What he held wasn't a spade or a sickle — something flat. His clothes were different too. Not sweat-soaked like the other hired hands, but clean.

I didn't watch him long. I decided he had nothing to do with me. He didn't seem to notice me either.

*I don't remember his face from that day. It was too far. What I remember is the way he stood. The stance of someone checking something. Not the stance of someone fixing something. I couldn't tell the difference then. Later I could.*

He kept turning up in that same spot after that. Some mornings, some evenings. I guessed it must be his job — tending the harvesters, or watching over them. I didn't think further than that.

*I'll write no more of him than this in this chapter. Anything more would be a lie. That was all I knew about him then. I didn't know his name. I didn't know his face. Only in the next chapter do I hear his name, and learn it belongs to him.*

---

On the eighth day, I thought for the first time about giving up.

My feet ached badly that morning. My shoes were worn through at the heel. Walking was hard. I stood at the door a long while, hesitating.

Pikra didn't pretend to sleep that day.

"…Rest today." Pikra said, her voice thick with sleep.

"…I'm fine."

"…I can see your feet."

I said nothing.

"…Resting won't change anything anyway." Pikra said. It was impossible to tell whether that meant *stay* or *go.*

In the end I went out. I wrapped my feet in rags instead of shoes.

*I still can't explain exactly why I went that day. It wasn't that my feet had stopped hurting. It wasn't that I was expecting anything either. I simply felt that if I didn't go, everything I'd gone out for until now would become, from that moment, as if it had never happened. I didn't want that.*

I walked slowly that day, twice as long as usual. I stopped several times to rub my feet. And at some point it struck me, all over again, that I was going through all this to reach a place with nothing in it.

I laughed then. A strange laugh. Not sad, not happy. I just found what I was doing so absurd that laughter came out of me. Aching feet, dragging myself toward a place with nothing — for a moment I looked like a stranger to myself.

*I agonized a long time over whether to write this laugh into the book. I worried it would look comic. But I decided to write it in. Because that laughter was really there, in that spot, that day. This book doesn't pretend nothing but sorrow happened.*

---

On the ninth day, I spoke with the old man for the first time.

I wasn't the one who started it. He looked at his sack and said, seemingly to no one in particular:

"…Why do you come every day, miss?"

I wasn't ready with an answer.

"…I don't know." I said. "What about you, sir?"

He laughed a little — silently.

"…I don't know either." he said. "Been at it years now. Still don't know."

"…So why do you keep coming?"

"…Good question." He gathered a fistful of dirt and let it fall again. "If somebody asks, I just say it's habit. A habit probably had a reason once. Where the reason went, I couldn't tell you. Just the habit's left."

"…How many years?"

"…Counted long enough, then quit." He set the sack back on his knee. "What's the point of counting."

That frightened me a little — that he'd come every day for years and still didn't know why. But it comforted me a little too. I wasn't the only one without an answer.

*I met him often after that. We never became friends. Just two people who came to the same place. But I learned later that this could be steadier than friendship, some days. Standing beside someone who doesn't ask you why is, sometimes, its own kind of strength.*

From that day I started watching his sack more closely. It was always empty. Occasionally there was a thin layer at the bottom, but I never once saw it take on the shape of a full sack. I wondered when it had last been full. I didn't ask. We'd only exchanged a single conversation in nine days. That wasn't a question you asked, at that stage.

*I still think of that man's sack, even now. What shape an empty sack takes after years of being carried empty — I learned that by watching him. A sack, like a person, comes to fold a certain way if it goes unused long enough.*

---

On the evening of the tenth day, I stopped counting for the first time.

I mean I stopped counting the days. Until then I'd walked along ticking off the number on my fingers. From that day I didn't.

*I think that's the true end of this chapter. As long as I was counting the days, I was proving something to myself — how many days I'd held out, how many more I could hold out. When I stopped counting, I stopped proving. I simply became someone whose body rose at the same hour every day. What was left where the proving used to be wasn't much of anything. Just the one fact that morning would come again, and I would get up again.*

*I think this is the easiest chapter in the whole book to write, and at the same time the hardest. Easiest because nothing happened. Hardest because I know that, even so, it's the most important chapter. Beginning in the next one, things start to happen. Someone recognizes me. But the reason that person could recognize me is that I had been there, at the same hour, in the same spot, every single day.*

Ten days on, the corner was still exactly as it had been. The dirt was the same color, the stubble the same height, and what my hand found there was still next to nothing. I could feel that fact growing duller with every day that passed. The first few days, every time I came home empty-handed, something had tightened just below my ribs. By the tenth day, that tightness had settled into something more like the ordinary weight of a day. Not a special grief anymore — just a burden I carried around all the time.

*I don't think that was getting better. Growing dull and getting better are not the same thing. I was only learning how to carry that weight while I walked. And that walk, without my knowing it, had already caught someone's eye. Someone had been watching me, day after day, in the same spot — and had begun, day by day, to find me strange. I didn't know that yet.*

*I went, every day. That's the whole of this chapter.*
