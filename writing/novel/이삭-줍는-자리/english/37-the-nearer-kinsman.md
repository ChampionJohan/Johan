# Chapter 37. The Nearer Kinsman

> _Previously_ — Lytron's tampering with the loss rate was discovered. It
> wasn't illegal. It was simply a loss. His family's standing fell, and his
> father asked him why he'd done it. Lytron had no answer.

The Roll measures closeness in two ways.

One is the closeness I know. Seeing a face every day. Eating from the same
bowl. Knowing, when you hear someone crying at night, when not to go to
them. That kind of closeness is built out of time. It takes long to make,
and long to lose.

The other is the closeness the Roll knows. Blood, marriage, and the number
the two leave behind. This closeness holds even if you've never seen the
face. It holds even if you've never met. Two people who have never once
stood in the same room can be recorded, inside the Roll, as the closest of
kin.

*Until then I had forgotten the second kind of closeness existed.
Forgotten isn't quite right — I had never known it at all. There was no
such closeness in our settlement. Out there, there was only the one kind.
Seeing someone every day.*

*I mean to write, in this chapter, how different the two kinds of
closeness are from each other. And what that difference finally made
possible.*

*Mena told me once, later, that a record only ever keeps the second kind
of closeness. The Roll, the paperwork, even this book. The first kind is
never written down. I remember that now, writing this chapter.*

---

That summer was unusually long.

Since Lytron's family standing had fallen, the way people treated him in
the field had shifted, little by little. Nothing large. A greeting arriving
half a beat slower. People not quite stopping their talk when he passed.
Things like that.

I noticed. I didn't tell him. There was nothing to be gained by saying it.

We still went out to the corner every day. The loss rate, set back to 3
percent, hadn't been touched. No one had reset it to 0.02 percent again.
Resetting it was work, and no one cared enough to do that work. Lytron's
family standing had already fallen; turning the setting back now wouldn't
have undone that.

So something kept falling at the corner of the field. Not much. But more
than before.

*I still remember that summer as a strange season. Something bad had
happened, and because of it, there was a little more to eat every evening.
It took me a long time to find a way to make sense of that strangeness.*

---

Pikra brought up the name on an ordinary evening.

We were washing the barley from that week's ration. Water was scarce, so
the three of us shared one basin, taking turns. Pikra spoke without
stopping her hands.

"…Medon might still be here."

"…Who?" I asked.

"…Your husband's cousin," Pikra said. "The only one of that family who
never left."

I stopped my hands.

"…My husband had a cousin?"

"…He did," Pikra said. "You must have seen him a few times as a child.
Though you wouldn't remember."

*I didn't remember. I had never even heard his name spoken. In two years
as that family's daughter-in-law, that name had never once come up. Later
I learned it wasn't an accident. The family had chosen not to speak of
him.*

"…Why didn't he leave?" I asked. "The whole family went outside."

Pikra gave herself, for a moment, entirely to washing. Only the rasp of
barley in the basin.

"…He was never blood to this house," she said, finally. "He's my
husband's brother's son. That brother died young, and his widow stayed on
here with just her boy. We'd already split from that side long before. It
happened before he'd turned ten."

"…So he isn't close family."

Pikra looked at me. Water dripped from her hands.

"…He's close to the Roll," Pikra said. "By blood."

"…What's the difference?"

"…That it's a different closeness than the one I know," Pikra said.
"Would he know me? Probably not. He wouldn't know my face. But the Roll
has us written down as close kin."

*I let the words pass by that night. It wasn't unusual for Pikra to bring
up a name from the past. I took it for something said over an evening
chore. I still thought so the next morning.*

---

Lytron came out to the field the next day.

He didn't smile much lately. His face had changed a little, since his
family standing fell. Not for the worse. Just a face that seemed always to
be calculating something.

"…Xene."

"…Yes."

"…I need to ask you something," he said. "On your mother-in-law's side —
does anyone still have family left in this city?"

I didn't know why he was asking.

"…There is, apparently. I only heard it yesterday. Someone named Medon."

Something registered on Lytron's face. It was an expression I would learn
to recognize only later — not the face of calculating something, but of
confirming it.

"…Medon," he repeated. "I know that name."

"…How?"

"…He's in the Harvest management Roll," he said. "His family standing is
high. Very high. Among the highest in this district."

"…What does he do?"

"…Supply allocation," Lytron said. "That family's never wavered,
generation to generation. They never went outside, and they've never had
much occasion to lose anything."

Something in that caught at me, though I couldn't have said what.

*I can name it now. Lytron already knew, even then. That a family that
had never wavered meant a family that had never lost anything. And that
this wasn't only a good thing.*

---

The administrator sent for us that same week.

Three of us sat before the Roll terminal — Pikra, Lytron, and I. The
administrator paged through the screen, explaining something. His voice
was even, as always. The same pitch whether the news was bad or good.

"…There's a procedure called standing transfer," the administrator said.
"You may already know of it."

I didn't. Lytron looked as though he did.

"…It lets a person lower their own score to add to someone else's," the
administrator went on. "It exists in the system. It's rarely used."

"…How rarely?" Lytron asked.

The administrator paged through once more. His fingers moved with
practiced ease.

"…According to the record, it has never once been used," the
administrator said, still even. "The provision exists, but there is no
case of it having been carried out. Not once, since it was created."

That didn't strike me as strange. I didn't yet know how strange it was —
that a door could exist and no one had ever tried it.

*I know now. It wasn't that the provision existed and nobody used it. It
was that nobody used it, and so the provision simply existed. A door no
one has ever tried isn't open, even when it's open. I didn't understand
the difference then.*

The administrator pointed to a corner of the screen.

"…In a case like this, there's a procedural order of priority," the
administrator said. "The first opportunity goes to a blood relation. It's
not fixed by law — it's custom. And this person" — he pointed to a name
on the screen — "is the only one registered as blood kin to you, Miss
Xene."

It was Medon.

I looked at the number beside his name. I couldn't read numbers well, but
I knew it was a large one. Far larger than Lytron's. Several times larger,
it looked like.

"…At this size," Lytron said quietly, "it wouldn't even show. For him."

The administrator gave a small lift of the shoulders.

"…It wouldn't."

"…Then why hasn't anyone done it already?" I asked. "If it wouldn't even
show."

The administrator looked at me. For the first time, something crossed
that even face. I couldn't name it then.

"…Not showing is still not nothing," the administrator said. "Not in the
direction of going down."

*I didn't fully understand that at the time. I understand it now. Not
showing means others don't see it — not that the person himself doesn't
feel it. And a person knows the things others can't see.*

I raised my hand.

"…What changes, if I'm entered?" I asked.

The administrator looked at me. He didn't seem surprised by the question.

"…Rations come regularly," he said. "You can apply for housing. You can
hold a proper job. And" — a brief pause — "the Roll records you. Right
now, the fact that you live here isn't recorded anywhere. Once you're
entered, it is."

That caught at me strangely.

"…So right now, my living here isn't recorded anywhere?"

"…A person with zero standing isn't a subject of record," the
administrator said. "The Roll doesn't note a birth. It doesn't note a
death. It isn't that you're treated as not existing. There's simply no
field to write you into."

*Hearing that, I understood for the first time what registration actually
meant. It wasn't a matter of rations or a place to live. It was whether
the fact of my being here was preserved anywhere at all. I had been in
this city more than two years by then, and inside the Roll, I was a
person who existed nowhere.*

---

The administrator said he would begin the procedure. Medon would be
contacted.

For the first time, I felt something like hope. Relief, more precisely.
If someone could do this without any loss at all, Lytron would no longer
need to lower his own family standing further. I thought that was a good
thing.

I looked over. Lytron's face wasn't relief.

"…What's wrong?" I asked.

"…Nothing," he said. "It's good news."

*I believed him, when he said it. Thinking back on it now, what was on
his face then wasn't relief but something else. I wouldn't learn what it
was for several months.*

---

Pikra stayed quiet even after hearing the news.

"…They've contacted Medon," I said, on the way home. "The administrator's
started the procedure."

Pikra stopped walking.

"…All right," Pikra said. That was all.

"…What's wrong?"

Pikra looked at me for a long time. The evening light touched only half
her face.

"…Nothing," Pikra said. "Let's just wait and see."

"…Is Medon a bad person?"

"…He isn't a bad person," Pikra said. She started walking again. "He
never was."

The answer struck me as strange. Saying it twice felt like leaving
something else unsaid.

"…Then why did the families split?" I asked.

Pikra slowed her steps.

"…His mother — Medon's mother — left our family," Pikra said. "After her
husband died. She decided there was nothing to gain by staying. But she
didn't go outside. She stayed in the same city. She only split from us."

"…Why?"

"…Because our family's standing was already bad by then," Pikra said.
"One of my husband's brothers had already died. She didn't want her son
tied to that number. She was a clever woman."

Something went cold in me at that.

"…So Medon's family ran from the bad blood."

"…You could say that," Pikra said. There was no resentment in her voice.
Just the tone of someone stating a fact. "She was protecting her son. I
might have done the same."

*That stayed with me a long time. Pikra didn't resent the choice. I found
that more unsettling, somehow — that there are choices in this world one
cannot resent. And that the outcome of such a choice could reach all the
way to me, decades later.*

*A person, protecting themselves, often can't see who falls because of
it. Or perhaps they can, and simply don't look. I still can't tell the two
apart.*

*I didn't understand then why Pikra was so cautious. I know now. Pikra
knew Medon. His face, his voice, the way he chose his words. I didn't
know him. Pikra did.*

*That difference decided the days that followed.*

A few days later, an answer came. Medon would see us in person. He asked
us to come to his office near the gate.

I couldn't sleep that night. Not the good kind of sleeplessness. It had
been a long time since I'd lain awake for a good reason.

Pikra didn't so much as turn over beside me. I knew she was only
pretending to sleep.

*I didn't know then that it would be the last night I lay awake for a
good reason.*

---

That night, unable to sleep, I thought about my husband.

He had never once mentioned a cousin. I'd taken that for how little he
spoke in general. He was the sort of person who said a thing once and let
it lie for a long while before saying it again, so there was no
particular reason a cousin should have been an exception.

*I think differently now. Some silences aren't simply an absence of
speech. Someone made them, deliberately. He may not have chosen not to
speak of his cousin. He may have been unable to — if the family had
already erased that story, long before.*

I'd asked him once whether he had relatives in the city. He'd been quiet
for a long while, and then said only this.

"…Even if I have one, it's as good as not having one."

I let it go at the time. It catches at me now. To say *even if I have
one, it's as good as not having one* is to say you know he exists. That
isn't something you say about a man who isn't there at all.

---

I went out to the field the next day, at midday, and he was in his usual
spot.

I didn't glean much that day. My mind was elsewhere. He worked quietly
beside me, and then looked at me once.

"…Something wrong?" he asked.

I was surprised. It was rare for him to ask first.

"…A relative on my mother-in-law's side has turned up," I said. "He might
register me."

He nodded. No sign of surprise.

"…That's good news."

"…Then why can't I let myself feel easy about it?"

He paused, hands still.

"…That's how it is, waiting for something big," he said. "Waiting for
something small is easy — if it doesn't come, that's the end of it.
Waiting for something big, if it doesn't come, that's never the end of
it."

I turned that over for a long time.

*I didn't understand then why he spoke that way. I didn't know what he
had ever waited for. I think now that he must have been waiting a very
long time, for something.*

---

The day Medon was contacted, Lytron didn't come to the field.

He didn't come the next day either. On the third day, when he reappeared,
I didn't ask where he'd been. His face was the face of someone who had
already come to know something.

"…I went to Medon's office," he said first.

"…What did he say?"

"…He'll meet with you," he said. "You and your mother-in-law."

Something in his tone struck me as wrong. It wasn't the tone of someone
delivering good news.

"…And?"

"…Nothing," Lytron said, shaking his head. "See for yourself."

*I should have asked more, then. I didn't. I think I wanted only to
believe it would go well. A person, sensing that the answer they want is
coming, asks fewer questions. I learned that for the first time that
day.*

---

I heard, later, how Lytron's father had taken the news.

His father had been glad. Very glad, apparently. A way had appeared to
stop his son's family standing from falling any further — that was reason
enough. He'd sent someone directly to the administrator, asking that
Medon's side of the procedure be hurried along.

"…My father said something," Lytron told me a few days later. There was
something held back in his voice. "That it was good news. That I could
finally stay out of this."

"…Why does that bother you?"

"…It's nothing," he said, shaking his head. "The words just sounded
strange to me."

I couldn't name what was strange about it, then. His face wasn't relief.
It was closer to a face holding something in.

*I can name it now. His father had seen this, from beginning to end,
purely as a matter of loss. His son had taken a loss; if a way existed to
end it without further loss, that was that. That I was inside that way,
that whether I was registered at all hung on it, had no place in his
father's arithmetic. Whichever way the answer came out, I was an item
that mattered very little to him.*

*Lytron was the one who had begun this from outside that arithmetic. I
didn't know it then. I should have. I learned, very slowly, in that city,
how to tell the people inside the arithmetic from the people outside it.*

---

The morning we were to meet Medon, Pikra took my hand.

It wasn't something she usually did. Her hand was rough, the knuckles
thick.

"…Xene."

"…Yes."

"…Whatever happens," Pikra said, "you're already mine. Whatever the Roll
says."

I couldn't answer. My throat closed.

*I took the words for comfort, that day. Thinking back, it wasn't comfort
— it was a warning. Pikra already knew what Medon would say.*

We walked toward the gate. It was the same road I had walked when I first
entered this city, two years before. Then, I'd been on my way to be
assessed for standing. This time, I was on my way to be registered.

It was strange that the two roads were the same road.

Pikra walked without speaking. I walked beside her, watching the office
buildings near the gate grow larger as we approached. Medon's office was
among the largest of them.

*I remember exactly the moment I stood before that building. Before the
door opened. Before any answer had been given. Up until that moment, I
too had standing enough to hope. I know how strange it is to use that
word this way. But that is exactly how it felt, in that moment.*
