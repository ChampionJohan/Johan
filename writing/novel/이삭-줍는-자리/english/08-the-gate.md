# Chapter 8. The Gate

> _Previously_ — The two lodge overnight in the village outside the
> gate. Xene wonders how her name will be entered on the Roll, then
> falls asleep. Listening to the insects cry, she spends her last
> night sleeping without calculating anything.

I woke to find Pikra already packed.

"…Sleep well?" I asked.

"…Not really," Pikra said. The shadows under her eyes were darker than
the day before.

We shared bread and left the inn. The gate was less than half an
hour's walk. As soon as we left the village, the crowd thickened.
Everyone was walking the same direction.

I looked at their faces. All of them wore no expression. Some looked
bored, some looked set hard. I wondered if we would look like one of
those faces too, from the outside.

The gate wasn't what I'd imagined.

I'd pictured a door — a great gate with someone standing before it,
letting some through and turning others away. But there was no door,
not separately. Or rather, there was one, but it stood always open.
What kept people out wasn't the gate. It was something inside the
gate.

A line.

---

The line split in two. One short, one long. A sign hung over the short
one: RENEWAL. Over the long one, a different sign: NEW REGISTRATION.

Looking at those two words, something struck me as wrong. That the
short line was the renewal side. Confirming someone already there took
no time; bringing someone new in took a long while.

"…Which one?" I asked Pikra.

"…That one, for me." Pikra pointed to the short line. "That one, for
you." And the long line.

That was the moment I first truly understood that from today, we
would stand in different lines. Until yesterday, we'd walked the same
road.

*I can still draw those two lines with my eyes closed. The short one
and the long one. I would meet those same two lines again, many times,
later in my life. The names changed every time. The shape never did.*

---

We each stood in our own line, close enough to see each other. I spent
the time watching the people ahead of me.

Inside the line stood a wide roof. No walls, only pillars, so you
could see clear through. A device was mounted on every pillar. I'd
never seen anything like it. A smooth, polished panel — letters
appeared on it without anyone touching it.

Beside each device sat another box, about waist height. A low sound
came from inside it. A humming sound — not from just one, but from the
whole line, so the space under the roof was filled with it.

*Now I know. That was the Roll. Part of it, at least. No one ever told
me where the Roll as a whole was kept. But that day, I could tell at
least this much — that sound was the sound the Roll made. The
calculating never stopped. For every person standing in that line, at
once.*

A large board hung on the inner wall under the roof. Numbers kept
changing on it. PROCESSED TODAY. The number beneath climbed slowly. I
learned later that the number was how many people had passed through
that gate that day.

I watched that board a long while. Each time the number climbed by
one, it struck me as strange — that one number was one person. I would
soon be added to that number too.

At the front of the line, a man stood before a device. The person
watching the device asked him something. He answered briefly. The
screen changed a few times. He nodded and walked on. It took barely
five minutes.

I was surprised at how brief it was. I'd imagined something far more
complicated.

*Now I know. Renewal was meant to be that fast. There's no reason it
should take long to confirm a name already there. What takes long is
making a name that doesn't exist yet. And my line was the line doing
that work.*

Several people sat in a row at the devices. All dressed alike. There
were as many screens as people, and every screen kept shifting numbers
and letters without pause. Watching those screens, I saw with my own
eyes, for the first time, what the city called the Roll.

It was quieter than I'd expected. I'd imagined something loud — bells
ringing, people shouting. It wasn't. Fingers tapping screens, low
voices, that was all. That quiet made me more tense, if anything.

---

Pikra's line moved first. I watched from my own line.

The person seated at the device was young. A few years older than me,
maybe. When Pikra stepped up, the person looked at the screen and
asked,

"…Your name."

Pikra paused a moment. I knew what that pause meant.

"…Glyke," Pikra said. Her voice was low.

Hearing that name come out of her mouth, something in me felt strange.
For three days I'd been practicing not calling her by that name — and
here, the very owner of the name had to speak it herself, today.

*That was the first time I understood something. Even wanting to
change a name, there are names you cannot change before the Roll. The
Roll doesn't know a new name. It only knows the old one. Pikra had to
call, with her own mouth, at that gate, the very name she wanted to be
rid of.*

The person tapped the screen a few times. "…There's a prior
registration. Some thirty-some years back. Exited by marriage,
relocated to an outer settlement."

"…Yes."

"…And the reason for returning?"

Pikra paused again, then said,

"…My husband died, and both my sons. I'm alone now."

The person showed no reaction to that at all. No change of
expression. He entered something into the screen.

"…Confirmed," the person said. "I'll process the re-registration."

The screen changed a few more times. A number came up. I couldn't see
it from where I stood. The angle was wrong.

"…This should keep your housing," the person said. "Though it's lower
than before. Fewer dependents."

Pikra nodded. "…I know."

*I thought about that moment for a long time afterward. Fewer
dependents. The person said it without any weight at all. That was the
first time I heard a husband and two sons called dependents.*

The person handed Pikra a small card. About the size of a palm.
Numbers and letters were etched on one side.

"…What's this?" Pikra asked.

"…A confirmation card," the person said. "Show it when you claim
housing or rations. If you lose it, you'll need to have it reissued."

Pikra took the card in her hand. I didn't fully understand what she
was holding, but I understood at least this — it was the object that
proved Pikra existed, as a person, in this city.

*I saw that card for the first time that day. And for a long time
after, I wanted something like it, for myself. I wanted to be someone
with something to show as proof. How long that wish would last, I
didn't know then.*

---

Pikra stepped aside and waited for me. I kept moving forward in my own
line the whole time.

Most of the people ahead of me took long. New registration meant a lot
of questions. Listening to their answers, I tried to guess what I
would be asked myself.

"…Where are you from?"
"…Your parents' names?"
"…Marital status?"

One person couldn't answer any of it. Said they didn't know their
parents. The person at the device checked something a few more times,
then sent that person to a third line. I only noticed then that this
third line had no sign at all.

*I would meet that third line again, later. I'll only note its name
here for now. It had no sign. It was more frightening for having
none.*

Another couple stood ahead of me. Young, holding an infant. As the
husband answered, the wife kept rocking the baby to sleep. The person
at the device pointed at the child and asked,

"…No birth registration on this child."

"…Born outside," the husband said. "There was nowhere to register."

"…Would you like to register today, together?"

The couple looked at each other. Neither spoke for a moment. Then the
wife nodded.

"…Yes."

The person entered something into the screen. I didn't see what number
appeared. I only saw the wife hold the baby a little tighter.

*I never saw that couple again. I don't know what number the child was
given that day. But I remember, still, the hands with which the wife
held her child a little tighter. Even at the moment a person's own
child becomes a number, they hold the child with their body first.*

---

My turn finally came.

Seen close up, the device was simpler than I'd expected. One panel,
beside it a small box with a few holes in it, and thin lines running
from the box down under the floor. I wondered where those lines led.
Probably not just to this line — probably connected to every other
line, all across the city.

The person at the device smiled at me. A courtesy smile. Not an
unkind smile, but that only made it stranger to me. Outside, no one
smiled at a stranger like that.

"…Your name."

"…Xene."

"…Family name?"

I was caught off guard by that question. "…We don't have family
names. We just use one name."

The person marked something on the screen. I didn't know what it
meant.

"…Where are you from?"

"…Outside. A settlement. It doesn't have a name."

"…The settlement has no name?"

"…No."

The person tapped the screen, then stopped. "…It's not on the
registered settlement list. I'll substitute coordinates."

That struck me as strange. That the place I was born and raised would
be written down as coordinates instead of a name.

"…Your parents?"

I gave their names. The person tapped, then shook their head. "…No
registration record. I'm guessing they were born and raised outside
too?"

"…Yes."

"…Marital status?"

"…I was married. My husband died."

"…Your husband's name?"

I couldn't answer right away. I said earlier that I never once write
that name in this record. That day at the gate, I hesitated too. But
it was for a different reason than this record's. I was afraid to say
the name. Afraid that saying it would turn it into a handful of
numbers.

*Now I know. The name did turn into numbers, in the end. Whether I
said it or not made no difference. The Roll is a place that turns
names into numbers. My hesitation changed nothing.*

I said the name at last. The person entered it into the screen.

"…No marriage record," the person said, looking at the screen. "A
marriage outside isn't subject to registration. Neither is a death."

Something inside me felt like it was collapsing. A person I'd lived
with two years had, inside the Roll, simply never happened.

"…What about the fact I was married?"

"…It doesn't stay on record," the person said. No apology in their
voice. A voice simply stating a fact. "What happens outside doesn't
count, by our standard."

"…What about him dying?"

"…That too," the person said. And then, just for a moment, looked at
me again. For the first time, not a purely clerical look. "I'm sorry.
I'm not saying it didn't happen. I'm only saying it doesn't count, by
our standard."

That struck me, strangely, as honest. The person hadn't said my
husband never died. They'd only said that death held no place inside
this screen.

*I didn't understand that distinction, that day. Now I do. The Roll
doesn't lie. The Roll simply doesn't build a place to count certain
things. The two are very different acts, but the result feels
identical.*

Pikra had been listening to that exchange, standing behind me. I
didn't turn around, but I could feel it. Her breathing had quickened,
a little, from its usual pace.

---

The person pressed one last screen. A small mark in the middle of it
began to spin. A circle, turning.

"…Calculating," the person said. "One moment."

I watched that spinning mark. It looked like nothing much, but those
few seconds felt longer than any I'd ever known.

Pikra took my hand beside me. Only then did I realize my hand had been
shaking.

Outside the roof, the sound of carts, of people passing, went on as
before. None of those sounds cared what state I was in. The city
wasn't going to wait for my calculation to finish. In another line,
someone was already giving their name for the next turn.

I felt that, strangely, as sorrow. The most important seconds of my
life, and to someone else, just a scrap of passing noise.

*I would meet that sorrow again and again, after that. This city never
stopped for my circumstances. It never stopped for anyone's
circumstances. I thought, at first, that this was unfair. Later I
learned that the city took pride in exactly that. No special
treatment for anyone. That fairness never once felt fair to me.*

*I remember those few seconds precisely, even now. My whole life, as
I'd known it, was inside them. The settlement. The summer I learned
the sickle. My husband. The three days Glyke became Pikra. All of it
was, in that moment, turning into a single number on a screen.*

The spinning stopped.
