# Chapter 18. The Hired Hands

> _Previously_ — Xene gathered less than a day's food at the corner and
> shared it with Pikra. Neither spoke of it. On Pikra's word, Xene
> decided to try speaking to the people who worked the field.

The next morning, I went out earlier than usual.

The reason was simple. I wanted to arrive when the workers gathered. At the corner there was never anyone at all. I had to go closer to where the machines stood, into the field itself, to find people.

*I don't think it's like me to have carried out that resolve within a single day. I was, by nature, someone who put things off. But there was no reason to put it off, just then. Putting it off didn't seem likely to make tomorrow any better than today.*

The whole walk there, I rehearsed what to say. I ran a few greetings through my head. All of them sounded awkward. Outside, greetings hadn't been necessary — you simply opened whoever's door and went in. The city wasn't like that. Here, speaking to a stranger felt like asking permission first.

*I understood later where that feeling came from. Outside, no one needed standing to speak to another person. In the city, somewhere along the way, it had come to feel like something you needed. No one had ever told me I needed permission to check. I was already feeling it, regardless.*

---

The hired hands gathered before dawn.

I learned that for the first time that day. They gathered in front of a small storehouse at the edge of the field and received their assignments for the day — who would take which zone, who would follow which machine. It was decided fresh each morning.

Someone else handed out the assignments. Not part of the Roll — I learned later he worked under the administrator. He called names one by one and handed out slips of paper. I didn't recognize a single name.

*Watching that morning scene, a strange thought struck me. These people had names. I didn't. More precisely: I had a name too, but it wasn't in the Roll. If you have a name but it's never called, is that having one, or not. I still haven't answered that question.*

---

I stood some distance from the storehouse and watched.

I couldn't quite bring myself to go closer. I'd decided the night before to greet someone, anyone — but standing there, my legs wouldn't carry me forward.

One group took their assignments and scattered. I followed one direction — people heading toward the corner. Since we were going the same way, I naturally ended up walking beside them.

The first person I spoke to didn't even look at me.

"…Excuse me, hello." I said to the man walking beside me.

He glanced at me and quickened his pace. No answer. I stopped and watched his back.

*I remember that moment for a long time. It wasn't the being ignored that stung. I was already used to that. What stayed with me was that he'd quickened his pace. I wasn't a dangerous person. But in his eyes, maybe I'd looked like something better avoided. Being unregistered meant that just standing still could make you something someone needed to steer around.*

I almost gave up. But I started walking again.

A young woman was the first to actually see me.

"…What is it?" she asked. Not unkindly. Just curious.

"…Oh, just — walking the same way." I said. I heard, as soon as I said it, how foolish that sounded.

She laughed. Not a mocking laugh.

"…Unregistered, right?" she said. "We know about you people, coming here every day. We know."

I didn't know what to say, so I just nodded.

*That was the first day I learned that I was called "you people." I wasn't anonymous. I'd thought I went unnoticed, but I'd already been sorted into a category. The one who comes every day. Those people. I was inside that category.*

---

Her name was Damia. A few years older than me.

Damia had low standing, but she was registered — some points inherited from her parents' generation, enough to let her take wage work in the city. She said her household wasn't well off. But she had a day's food, without fail.

"…I envy that." I said, without thinking.

Damia looked at me a moment.

"…Nothing to envy." Damia said. "We're day-to-day too. Only difference is, we work today, we get paid today, for sure. You don't."

I had no answer to that.

*That was the first time I heard the phrase "day-to-day." I learned later it was common among the hired hands — people living off what they earned that same day. I heard it and thought I was day-to-day too. Except I was day-to-day without even the day part certain.*

---

Walking a while longer beside Damia, the man who'd been handing out assignments came over.

"…That's an unregistered, isn't it?" he asked Damia. He didn't ask me directly.

"…Yes, she is." Damia answered.

"…Can't come further into the field. Know that?"

"…We're heading to the corner." I answered for myself. "Just walking together, that's all."

He looked at me a moment. Not looking me up and down — just checking.

"…The corner's fine." he said. "That's what it's for. Just don't come into the work zones. It's in the law too."

"…All right."

He turned away, satisfied.

*I think that brief exchange is the most important thing in this chapter. He didn't chase me off. He didn't hate me. He simply confirmed a distinction laid out in the law. The corner is fine. The work zones aren't. I saw, for the first time that day, that distinction being kept exact. No one pushed me back any further than that. Exactly as much as the law allowed — no more, no less.*

---

There were a great many people in the field that day.

Three driving the machines directly. Six following behind, checking their condition. Some carried tools from the storehouse; others drove boundary stakes. That was the first day I saw their faces one by one. Before, I'd only seen them, from a distance, as a mass.

Toward noon the sun grew fierce. The machines never stopped. Only the people took turns going in and out of the shade. Watching that rhythm, I thought of the outer settlement. There had been a rhythm to work there too. Only there, when people rested, the field rested with them. Here, when people rested, the field didn't. The machine kept going in their place.

*I saw that difference clearly, for the first time, that day. Outside, people and field lived at the same pace. In the city, the field moved faster than the people. People were allowed to rest; the field wasn't allowed to. I didn't know, then, whose sake that pace was for.*

Damia was assigned to follow one of the machines. I followed her out toward that zone and stood off to the side while she worked.

When break came, people gathered in the shade by the storehouse. Drank water, traded a few words, scattered again. I stood at the edge of the group.

"…Come sit." Damia called to me.

I went over carefully. No one said anything. I sat down beside Damia.

---

There was a man in that shade.

I saw him then for the first time. I couldn't guess his age — neither young nor old. His clothes were no different from anyone else's. Nothing distinctive about his face. He just sat quietly, drinking water.

While the others traded jokes back and forth, he said little. He laughed when there was something to laugh at, but he never spoke first.

"…Has he worked here long too?" I asked Damia, low.

"…Don't know." Damia shrugged. "Doesn't say much. But he's good at the work. Handles the machines, drives the stakes — whatever you ask, he does it."

"…What's his name?"

Damia thought a moment.

"…No idea, actually. Everyone just calls him 'him.'"

*Hearing that answer, I didn't think it strange. I didn't yet know it was strange. The field was full of people whose names I never learned. I was one of them myself. One more nameless person didn't seem worth remarking on.*

---

He looked at me once.

Our eyes met. Flustered, I looked away. But he showed no particular expression — just gave one small nod. Something like a greeting.

I nodded back, awkwardly.

That was all. No conversation followed. He finished his water, and when the break ended, he got up quietly and went back to his zone.

*I've gone back over this scene many times since. Nothing at all remarkable about it. One nod. That was it. I didn't hold onto any special memory of him that day. Just — there's a quiet one, I thought. Nothing more than that.*

Toward the end of the break, someone carrying tools dropped a spade handle. It had split. He was turning it over in his hands with a troubled look when the quiet man walked over, without a word, and took it from him.

He turned it a few times, then bound the crack with cord. It didn't take long. He handed it back and returned to his own spot without another word.

"…Thanks." the man who'd received it said, to his back.

He didn't turn around. Just raised one hand.

*I didn't think much of this scene either, that day. There was always one person like that in a field — quick-handed, fixing things before being asked. I put it down to just how he was, nothing more than that.*

---

That afternoon, I went back to the corner.

The time with the hired hands lasted only through the break. The rest of the time, they worked, and I sifted dirt alone as always. Nothing had changed. I filled about half a palm again that day.

But something felt a little different. Sifting the dirt, I wasn't lonely. Voices carried faintly from a distance — the machines, people calling out to one another. Before, those sounds had had nothing to do with me. That day they did.

*I don't want to make too much of this small change. Nothing was solved. I was still unregistered, still someone who came home with half a palm and no more. Only, that day, for the first time, sifting the dirt didn't feel like something I was doing alone. That was all.*

---

That evening I told Pikra about the day. Not right when I walked in — after supper, after the bowls were cleared away. Pikra never asked about things like that before we'd eaten. A conversation had on an empty stomach, she said, never gets properly heard by either side.

"…Did you talk to anyone?" Pikra asked.

"…Yes. Someone named Damia."

"…And?"

"…Not bad." I said. "She says she's day-to-day too. Same as us."

Pikra nodded.

"…That's true." Pikra said. "Everyone's day-to-day, in one way or another. Only for some, today's certain. For others, even today's not certain."

I sat a long while without answering.

*That night I only talked about Damia. I said nothing about the quiet man. Not that I meant to hide him — I just had nothing much to say. How do you describe a single nod to someone? I didn't think about him again, not once, that night.*

*Now I know. That was my first meeting with him. And that the meeting began as quietly as anything in this book begins. I noticed nothing that day. There was nothing to notice.*

---

The next day, I went back to the storehouse.

Damia was there again. We exchanged a few words. She treated me, by then, as someone she knew a little. That felt good.

The quiet man was there too. That day too, I just nodded and went on. He went to his zone; I went to the corner.

The days that followed went much the same. Stop by the storehouse in the morning, trade a few words with Damia, nod to the quiet man, go on to the corner and sift the dirt. In the evening, tell Pikra briefly what had happened that day. It was mostly about Damia. There was rarely much to say about the quiet man.

In those days I also learned a few of the other hired hands' names. The one who drove the stakes was called Nicol; the one who carried tools, Petros. I memorized those names. It had been a long time since I'd memorized a new name. I'd hardly learned a new one since leaving the outer settlement.

*Writing these names down again now, I feel something strange. Nicol and Petros never appear again in this record. They were only people who stood near me for a few days. I set their names down here so as not to forget them. A person doesn't stop having existed just because this record has no name for them.*

There were a few others in the field whose names I never heard. The quiet man was one. I never thought it strange. Seeing a face every day without ever learning its name — that was common enough in the city.

*Around then I started walking, every day, the same path — a stop at the storehouse, then on to the corner. On that path, I saw him every day. Nodded every day. That was all. I thought he was just a quiet person. I thought nothing more than that.*
