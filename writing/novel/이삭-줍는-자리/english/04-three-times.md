# Chapter 4. Three Times

> _Previously_ — Glyke, Xene, and Daphne set out together on the road
> to the city. Daphne still hasn't decided where she'll go. As evening
> comes and the fork in the road draws near, Glyke stops walking and
> speaks in a voice they don't recognize: "From here, each of you goes
> your own way."

At first I didn't understand what she meant. Go your own way. I
already had somewhere to go — following my mother, into the city. That
already-decidedness felt so obvious that it hadn't once occurred to me
that it might need deciding again.

Daphne wore the same face I did. Neither of us said anything.

Glyke leaned back against the tree.

"…Go back," Glyke said. "Both of you. Each to your own family."

*I remember this sentence exactly. I only learned, years later, how
strangely close what followed was to a story that appears, whole, in
the first chapter of the book of Ruth. I didn't know such a book
existed, then. I'm only setting down what happened to me.*

The sun had nearly gone down. The tree's shadow had stretched long
enough to cover the three of us. When the wind blew, the leaves made a
sound. I remember that sound still. Without it, I don't think I'd have
felt how quiet that evening was.

Glyke didn't wear the face of someone trying to persuade us. She wore
the face of someone who had already decided everything, and only
needed to put it into words now. Seeing that face, I knew this hadn't
come to her suddenly. She'd probably been preparing it the whole way
here.

That evening, Glyke said it three times. I wasn't counting as I
listened. But looking back, it strikes me strangely that it was
exactly three times. The last chapter set down the story of losing
three. This chapter sets down three sayings. The number three kept
following this household.

*I thought it was coincidence. I still think so. But when a
coincidence repeats, a person tries to read something into it. So did
I.*

---

The first saying.

"…Mother." Daphne spoke first. "Why should we?"

"…You were my sons' wives, not my daughters," Glyke said. "My sons are
dead. Whatever bound you to me is gone."

"…Even so."

"…Listen," Glyke said. "I can't bear you another son now, and you're
not at an age to wait for one even if I could. There's nothing left
for you here, staying by me."

I listened to that. It was arithmetic. Precise arithmetic. Glyke was
speaking in numbers, not in grief.

*I know now. That wasn't because there was no grief. Wrapping grief in
arithmetic was, in that moment, the only way Glyke could manage to
speak at all. Spoken as arithmetic, you can say it without crying.*

Daphne began to cry. Silently, just the tears.

"…I married your son partly because I liked him," Daphne managed.
"But partly because I liked you too, Mother."

Glyke's face wavered for a moment at that. Then it set again.

"…I'm grateful for that," Glyke said. "But gratitude and your life are
two different questions."

I said nothing. I just stood beside Daphne, listening. I think, by
then, I'd already decided what I would do. It only took a little more
time to turn it into words.

---

The second saying.

Glyke looked at the two of us again. This time her voice shook a
little.

"…Go back," Glyke said again. "To your mothers. I know how hard it is
for a woman to live alone. You don't need to walk that road too."

Daphne said through her sobbing, "…What about you? Who looks after
you?"

"…I'm going to the city," Glyke said. "It doesn't matter to me if
there's no one there. I'm just going to my own place. You're not like
that. You can still find a new place."

I caught something in that, then. Glyke was drawing a line between
herself and us. Herself, a person already finished. Us, people who
could still begin. That distinction struck me, strangely, as sad.

*I would meet this same distinction again later, in the Roll. The
Roll divides people too — those still usable, and those already
finished. The only difference is, Glyke drew that line only against
herself. The Roll draws it against others.*

Daphne wiped her tears and said, "…So you're going to the city alone?
It's days away."

"…I'm not going alone," Glyke said. Then she glanced at me. Something
complicated was in that look — wanting me, and being sorry for wanting
me, both at once.

Seeing that look, I understood for the first time. Glyke was trying to
send me back, and at the same time hoping I wouldn't go. I learned
that day that a person can want two things at once, fully, in the same
moment.

Daphne finally broke down and sobbed. I stood beside her, stroking her
back. My hand was trembling.

"…Mother, are we really parting like this?" Daphne asked.

Glyke couldn't answer. After a long while, she said,

"…It isn't parting," Glyke said. "Each of us is going back to where we
live. *Parting* is too sad a word."

I knew that wasn't comfort. It was something Glyke was saying to
herself.

---

The third saying.

This time Glyke said nothing for a long while, just looking at the two
of us. Then, slowly, she said,

"…Listen to me," Glyke said. "Even if I took a new husband tonight,
you couldn't wait around for his sons. You're young. I have no right
to hold your lives back."

*This was the strangest thing she said, to my ear. At the time I took
it for some old-fashioned figure of speech. Now I know. This was the
language of an old law — the law by which a dead man's brother takes
his widow, to carry on the line. Something like that custom must still
survive somewhere outside, in this world too. Glyke was speaking in
that law's own language, to say that the law was no longer possible
for her.*

Daphne heard that and, through her tears, half laughed.

"…Oh, Mother," Daphne said. "I never thought you'd go that far."

"…I never thought I'd say it either," Glyke said. "But once I started
looking for reasons to hold you back, I ended up here. That's what
happens when you go hunting for reasons that aren't there."

I felt, for the first time, that I was seeing Glyke's true heart.
She knew she had no reason to hold us — and still she was
manufacturing reasons to let us go. I sensed, dimly, in that moment,
that the reasons to hold on and the reasons to let go come from the
same place.

"…I'm old," Glyke said. "There's nothing more I can do for you. This
is the last of it. Go."

Daphne collapsed into Glyke's arms. She cried like that for a long
while. Then she pulled away.

Daphne kissed Glyke's face — her forehead, then both cheeks.

"…I'm sorry," Daphne said. "I can't do it."

"…It's all right," Glyke said. "It's nothing for you to be sorry
for."

Daphne looked at me. She kissed me too, briefly.

"…Take care, Xene."

And Daphne walked off from the fork, toward her own family. She looked
back several times. She waved. I watched until she was entirely out of
sight.

Glyke watched that same retreating figure alongside me. When Daphne
was completely gone, Glyke let out a short breath. I couldn't tell if
it was relief or grief. Probably both.

"…That girl will do well," Glyke said, almost to herself. "Her family
is good people. She'll marry well again too."

I said nothing. Listening to Glyke speak with such certainty about
Daphne's future, I felt there was something else behind that
certainty. It was a comfort to herself. A voice telling herself that
what she'd just done had been right.

*I never saw Daphne again after that. I don't know how her life went.
But I've never once thought the choice she made that day was wrong. It
was her choice, and it was the right one.*

---

Now it was just Glyke and me.

The sun had nearly set. One road at the fork was the one Daphne had
vanished down. The other led to the city. We still stood at that fork.
Neither of us had taken a step, either way.

Glyke looked at me. Her eyes were red.

"…Xene, you heard that too," Glyke said. "Your sister-in-law's going
back. You go back too."

I said nothing.

"…Why aren't you going?" Glyke said. Her voice rose a little.

I still said nothing.

"…Xene." Glyke stepped toward me. "Didn't you hear me? Do you know
what happens if you follow me?"

"…Yes."

"…And you still won't?"

I couldn't answer that question. Not exactly that I had no words for
it — I simply wasn't ready yet to speak it.

"…You're just being stubborn right now," Glyke said. "It's because
you're attached. Attachment fades with time. Go back to your family
and within half a year a good match will turn up. That's how it goes,
here."

"…That's not what this is."

"…Then what is it?" Glyke asked. "Say something, so I know."

I still couldn't answer. What I felt in that moment wasn't that I had
no answer to the question — it was that saying the whole answer out
loud, right there, would make it too small.

Glyke suddenly grew angry. I had almost never seen her angry, until
then.

"…If you follow me, your standing goes to zero," Glyke said. "Zero,
Xene. Do you understand? You don't become a city person. You become
nobody at all. I don't mind that for myself. I've already lost
everything there was to lose. But you — you haven't lost anything
yet!"

I listened to that quietly.

"…I didn't take you into this family to make you into that," Glyke
said. Her voice shook. "Go, Xene. Please, go."

"…Mother," I said.

"…Don't listen to me, just go," Glyke said. It was nearly a plea now.
"What are you going to do, being nobody at all, at your age? I'm old,
it's fine. When an old person is nobody, well, that's just how it
goes. But you're sixteen. If a sixteen-year-old lives as nobody, that
isn't living at all."

Listening to her, I felt that Glyke wasn't talking about me anymore.
She was talking about herself. In those words — *when an old person
is nobody, that's just how it goes* — was already the trace of
someone who had accepted that this was what she herself would become.

*I thought, for the first time, then: this woman is pushing me away
and settling her own future at the same time. Setting me, who would
become a zero, and herself, whose standing would fall to the floor,
side by side, hoping that at least one of us could avoid it.*

*I count this the scene I've returned to most often in this whole
book. Glyke wasn't angry to push me away. I know now. That was love,
come out as anger. There are moments when a person is angriest in
front of the one they cherish most. That was one.*

I looked at Glyke's face. She was angry, but underneath the anger was
something else. Fear. The face of someone afraid I might ruin my life
because of her.

The road around us had gone quiet. Daphne was already far off. Even
the birds had stopped. I felt, in that moment, as if Glyke and I were
truly the only two people left in the world.

"…Answer me," Glyke said again. Her voice was smaller now than before.
Closer to a plea than to anger. "Why won't you say anything."

I still couldn't answer. Not because there was no answer. I didn't
know how to say it out loud.

*I remember that silence exactly, even now. I don't know how many
seconds it was. I only know it felt long. In those seconds, I was
confirming something already settled inside me. It wasn't a time for
deciding. It was a time for finding what had already been decided.*

Glyke took my hand. It was cold.

"…Xene," Glyke said. Her voice was very small now. "I really don't
want to make you into this."

I took her hand in return. This was the hand that had taught me to
grip a sickle, that autumn I learned. Two years ago that hand had led
me. Now it was trying to push me away. The same hand, pointed the
opposite direction.

Looking at that hand, I knew what I had to say didn't need to be long.
Say it at length, and it becomes a reason, and Glyke would knock down
every reason, one by one. I wanted to say something that couldn't be
argued against.

"…Listen," I said.

Glyke looked at me. Her face was waiting.
