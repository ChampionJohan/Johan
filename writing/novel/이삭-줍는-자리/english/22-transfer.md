# Chapter 22. Transfer

> _Previously_ — Lytron learned that the loss-rate setting could be
> reverted. He also learned that reverting it would lower his family's
> score. He closed the window.

A few days later, I had reason to go to the administration office again.

It was for Pikra's registration renewal. Once a year, the procedure required a confirmation. Pikra wasn't feeling well that day, so it was I who went to collect the paperwork.

---

*Around this time, I'd begun to sense that Pikra's health was slipping a little. She sat down to rest more often, and sometimes had to catch her breath in the middle of supper. I couldn't quite tell whether it was age, or the amount we were splitting between us every evening. Probably both. The whole walk to the office that day, that was what occupied me. It worried me more than the paperwork did.*

Pikra hadn't objected when I offered to go in her place. Once, she'd have insisted on going herself — said she couldn't ask that of a daughter-in-law. That day she only said, "…Be careful, go on," and nothing more. The shortness of it worried me more than any protest would have.

The office stood near the gate, in the same building where I'd first been reviewed for registration. I wasn't eager to walk back into that building. The memory wasn't a good one.

I stood in front of it a moment, hesitating. If I opened that door, I felt, the feeling I'd had standing before that counter would come rushing back. Standing zero. I took a breath and went in.

*I would end up in that building many more times after that. Every time, I paused a moment at the door. The habit never went away, even years later. Some places never grow familiar, however often you visit them.*

---

I waited a long while in line at the window. Everyone ahead of me carried similar paperwork. Renewals, corrections, confirmations. All their faces wore the same tired look.

The man in front of me said he'd come about a relocation procedure. Moving to a different zone meant re-registering your residence too, he grumbled to the person beside him. Ahead of him stood a woman holding a baby, there about the child's birth registration. She was arguing at length with the clerk — the paperwork had come back wrong.

I killed time listening to that argument. This city seemed to have an endless number of things that required paperwork. Once at birth, once at marriage, once at each move, once at every renewal. Listening to that list, I found myself thinking, again, that outside, none of that paperwork had ever been necessary.

The line behind me kept growing. An old man stood leaning on a cane, shifting his weight from leg to leg, looking for somewhere to sit. Eventually the clerk came out and offered him a chair. The man thanked her, over and over. I thought the thanks a little excessive, at first — then understood it. Inside that building, even a small kindness was rare.

*I still remember that old man's face. I never learned his name, never saw him again after that day. But the way he thanked her, so gratefully, for a single chair — it seemed to say something about how stingy this city was, ordinarily, with its people.*

The woman with the baby was eventually directed to a different window. Watching her walk away, I thought: even a child born into this city has to be met with paperwork before anything else. It struck me as strange. Outside, when a child was born, you simply gave it a name, and that was the end of it. Here, apart from the name, you had to receive a number too.

At the next window, a young man was arguing in a low voice with a clerk. He seemed to be asking why his history score had dropped a little in this renewal. The clerk flipped through the screen a few times and explained that he'd changed residence twice in the past year. The man said it hadn't been a move — just a temporary relocation for work. The clerk answered that the reason wasn't considered; only the number of relocations counted. The man argued a while longer, then finally said he understood and left with his papers. Watching him go, I thought: in this building, even a grievance only amounts to a single sheet of paper.

When my turn came, the clerk handed over the form and began explaining it. The administrator, passing by just then, recognized me and stopped.

"…The one who comes to the corner." the administrator said.

"…Yes. I'm here for my mother-in-law's renewal papers."

The administrator nodded and gave a brief instruction to the clerk. Then, seeing I wasn't familiar with the procedure, he explained a few things to me himself.

---

"…Renewal has to be done by the person themselves; a proxy is only allowed in limited cases." the administrator said. "Marriage relations, direct family relations — cases like that."

"…Does a mother-in-law count as family?"

"…A daughter-in-law isn't counted as direct. Marriage relations are handled under a different procedure. Doesn't matter today, though — you're only collecting the form."

I went a little blank, hearing that. Two years living under the same roof, and by this city's rules, that still didn't count as direct family. I thought the distinction strange, but it didn't seem worth arguing over.

---

The administrator kept going, working through the rest as someone who knew the whole procedure by heart, several items at once.

"…There are a few more things to confirm besides this form." the administrator said. "Residence confirmation, relationship confirmation, and whether there's been any change to history within the past year. As I understand it, your mother-in-law's side has had none — is that right?"

"…Yes, none."

"…Then this one will be simple enough."

"…Score assessment moves through a few different channels." the administrator went on. "The origin item is fixed once, at registration, and adjusted afterward by history and relation. On marriage, it's combined with the spouse's score; on having children, it's inherited by a fixed ratio; on death, an attrition ratio applies. And there's also a thing called registration-item adjustment — transfer's possible too, technically, though in practice it's almost never used. There's a separate application form for it, but I understand there haven't been any filings in recent years."

The administrator said all this while already sorting the next form with his hands.

"…Once a marriage relation is dissolved, it's automatically reassessed, and the appeal period's one month, and appeals only go through the dedicated window, and processing usually takes two to four weeks once filed." the administrator kept on. "Right, write your mother-in-law's name and registration number on this form and take it along. Get the confirmation stamp at that window over there — I'm not sure how late that window stays open today, so you'd best hurry."

The administrator added a few more things in the meantime — notes printed on the back of the form, the reassessment cycle, the correction procedure, which cases were fee-exempt and which weren't. I couldn't hold onto any of it separately. One regulation overlapped another until I lost track of where one item ended and the next began. The administrator's voice moved through all of it at exactly the same pitch. Not a single inflection that told you which parts mattered and which didn't.

---

I let most of the explanation slide past me. What form to fill in mattered more, just then. The clerk handed me another sheet, and I wrote Pikra's name and registration number on it. I got a digit wrong and had to write it again.

*I remember how stifling the dry air in that office felt that day, more than usual. There were no windows in the room. Just the sound of people breathing and paper turning. All I wanted was to finish the form and get out.*

---

It took a long while to get the confirmation stamp. I was sent to two more windows. At the first, they said the registration number I'd written didn't match the required format, and I had to rewrite it. At the second, the clerk was away from the desk and I waited more than ten minutes. Only at the third window did I finally get the stamp.

By the time I got through it all, most of what the administrator had explained earlier had slipped from my mind. One thing stuck: that a daughter-in-law wasn't counted as direct family. Only that.

I kept turning that over as I walked. I didn't even know the name Ruth then. That was a name from an old story I'd only learn much later, from Mena. But in that moment, it struck me all over again how this city sorted me. Two years living here, and I was still, in its eyes, someone "joined by marriage."

*I heard, from Mena much later, about an old regulation this city drew on. There'd been a long-standing dispute, apparently, over how to count a relationship like a daughter-in-law's. In the end it had been settled on the side of marriage. Hearing that, I thought back to what I'd been told at that window. At the time I'd taken it for nothing more than paperwork procedure. I hadn't known that someone, long before, had argued over that very distinction.*

The light had slanted low by the time I left the building. I'd spent nearly half a day inside. My legs ached the whole walk home. Standing still, waiting, I learned that day, could wear a person down every bit as much as sifting dirt in the field.

On my way back I passed through the market alley. The sounds of people preparing supper drifted by. Listening to them, I tried to reckon up how much of what I'd heard that day was really mine to keep. Not much. Most of it had been regulation with nothing to do with me; the only part that touched me was that single line — *a daughter-in-law isn't direct family.* At the end of the alley I caught a familiar back — Lytron, carrying a clipboard, apparently on his way back from a round. He didn't seem to see me. I didn't call out either. I just watched his back disappear around the corner, then turned toward the cottage.

---

Back home, I handed the papers to Pikra.

"…That took a while." Pikra said. Her color looked a little better.

"…The line was long. And they said a daughter-in-law isn't direct, so I couldn't be proxy — I just brought back the form."

"…That's how it goes." Pikra said, looking the form over. "Always has been."

"…They explained a lot of other things too, but I couldn't follow most of it."

"…Don't worry about it." Pikra said. "As long as the paperwork's right, that's what matters. No need to follow everything they say."

We didn't discuss it further.

I studied Pikra's face again. Better than that morning, but still pale.

"…How are you feeling today?" I asked.

"…Fine. Just a little dizzy, that's all."

"…Tell me if it happens again tomorrow."

"…All right." Pikra said. Then, as if changing the subject, she folded the form away. "Better keep this safe. We'll need it again next year's renewal."

*That night, I watched Pikra's hands as she folded the form. Her fingertips looked thinner than before. I noticed it and said nothing. Around that time I was learning that noticing something doesn't mean you have to say it out loud.*

---

A few days later, I mentioned the long wait at the office to Damia too.

"…I had no idea the renewal process was that complicated." I said.

"…Everyone says that." Damia said. "I spent half a day at the window myself last year doing mine. Nobody actually understands all the regulations. You just fill in the form the way they tell you and get your stamp, that's all."

"…Is there anyone who actually knows all of it?"

"…Probably just the administrator." Damia laughed. "People like us don't need to."

I agreed.

"…Still, it took forever." I said. "I got sent to three different windows."

"…That's just how it is there." Damia laughed. "I spent half a day there last year too. Next time I'm going first thing in the morning."

"…I should do that too."

"…Funny thing, though." Damia said. "All that complication, and what actually matters comes down to a few lines. The rest you can go your whole life not knowing and be fine."

"…So how do you know which few lines matter?"

Damia shrugged.

"…You just learn it, living long enough." she said. "Or you never learn it at all. I've seen both."

We moved on, after that, to talk about the day's work. Damia had a machine inspection scheduled; I had to get to the corner.

---

The next day, I went to the corner later than usual. The morning had been eaten up by the paperwork.

He was already working the ditch when I arrived. He saw me and gave one nod. I nodded back.

"…I'm late." I said. There wasn't really any need to explain, but the words came out anyway.

"…That's all right." he said.

I didn't tell him about the office. There wasn't much to tell, and he didn't seem particularly curious either. We each sifted our own patch of dirt that day, same as always. Late start or not, my basket filled to about the usual amount by evening.

*It still strikes me as a little odd that I never told him what had happened that day. I'd told him a fair amount, by then. But what I'd heard that day just didn't seem like it would mean anything to him. Paperwork talk. I thought, at the time, it had just been a dull day, nothing more.*

Partway through, Damia stopped by and asked again about the office windows. I told her how many tries it had taken me to get the stamp. She clicked her tongue.

"…Longer than me, even." Damia said. "Come with me next time. I know the order of those windows."

"…I'll take you up on that, next time."

Only after I'd said it did it occur to me that I now had one more person in this city I could ask a favor of.

*Writing this chapter, I tried to set that day down exactly as it was. Nothing remarkable happened. I picked up a form, stood in line, went home. Some days just pass that way. Without leaving any mark at all.*
