# Chapter 29. The Same Pattern

> _Previously_ — Mena confirmed her own testimony had been changed.
> "Did not explain" had become "refused to explain," which fed into
> the assessment "no remorse." Mena said this wouldn't be the only
> one. That was what frightened her most.

The next morning, I smelled the paper before I even opened the archive
door.

The smell was stronger than usual. Old paper, mixed with the scent of
a lamp that had burned all night. From the smell alone, I could guess
what kind of night had passed in that room.

The old man at the door hadn't swept or wiped anything down that
morning. He was just leaning against the doorframe. He spoke to me in
a low voice.

"…She's been in there the whole time. I haven't gone in myself, this
morning."

"…Why not?"

"…The air in there." He gave a small shrug. "I just felt like I
shouldn't disturb it."

I hesitated a moment at the door, then pushed it open quietly.

Mena didn't look like she'd slept at all. Her eyes were red, and twice
as much paper as yesterday was spread across the table.

"…Were you here all night?" I asked.

"…Yes," Mena said. She didn't hide it. "This time I couldn't wait, the
way I did yesterday."

I sat beside her. The papers on the table were lined up differently
from the day before. On the left, city names; on the right, pairs of
short sentences. I recognized it as a table. Mena must have spent the
whole night building it.

I thought, suddenly, that the room felt narrower than it had yesterday.
It wasn't the paper making it feel that way. It was the weight of what
that paper held, grown heavier overnight.

"…Can I help?" I asked.

"…Just don't let the order get mixed up," Mena said. Her voice had no
strength in it. "Take the next one from that case and hand it to me.
From the top, in order."

I did that. I pulled the sheets out one by one and handed them to her.
Mena took each one, checked it against the original, and wrote a line
into the table. We didn't talk much. I didn't find the silence
uncomfortable. If anything, moving my hands helped. If I'd had to sit
there doing nothing, I think I would have found it hard to bear.

The light from the window crept slowly across the room. By the time it
reached the floor past the table's legs, I'd stopped counting how many
sheets we'd gone through. Counting felt pointless. What mattered wasn't
the number, but the direction.

---

"…How many have you found?" I asked.

"…Seven," Mena said. "Five of them certain. Two I haven't been able to
find the original for, so those aren't confirmed."

I read down through the table. Most of the city names were ones I'd
never heard before. A few sounded vaguely familiar, but I couldn't
place where.

The first line.

*Original: "He stood without kneeling."*
*Quoted: "He refused submission and displayed a defiant attitude."*

The second line.

*Original: "The woman said nothing about how she would repay the
debt."*
*Quoted: "Debtor: expressed no intent to repay."*

The third line.

*Original: "He did not explain what he had done. I did not ask why."*
*Quoted: "Witness testimony, Mena, cited. Subject refused to explain
the act. [Assessment: no remorse.]"*

That third one was the one I'd already seen.

I ran my finger along the line.

"…This is yesterday's."

"…That's right," Mena said. "But this line turned up again today. In
a different place. I only realized yesterday that a single sentence,
once made, gets reused across different cases. Once one is twisted, it
gets recycled again and again, the way a precedent does."

---

I read on through the rest of the table.

The fourth line.

*Original: "The child covered for her sibling. I didn't ask why."*
*Quoted: "Guardian: withheld information regarding harboring of
accomplice."*

"…Is this about a child too?" I asked.

"…Yes," Mena said. "When I wrote it, it was just about a child covering
for a sibling. I didn't even say whether it was good or bad. But here,
the word 'harboring' has been attached. I didn't even know what she'd
covered up."

The fifth line was shorter still.

*Original: "He said nothing that day."*
*Quoted: "Silent. Deemed to be evading testimony."*

Reading that line, a chill ran through me. The original sentence was
nothing at all. He said nothing that day. Everyone has days like that.
I'd had many days like that myself. And yet that one sentence had
become, on the other side, "evading testimony."

"…I have plenty of days I say nothing too," I said. My voice shook
without my meaning it to. "So could that happen to me someday too?"

Mena looked at me for a moment. She didn't answer. I didn't want to
know, right then, that the silence was the answer.

I stopped at the sixth line.

Mena's hand had stopped there too. I read it aloud.

*Original: "This one never explained himself."*
*Quoted: "This one never repented."*

"…This," I said. "This one feels familiar, somehow."

Mena looked at me. Her face had gone strangely pale.

"…It can't feel familiar," Mena said. "This isn't about anyone in this
city. It isn't from any event in this city."

"…Then why does it feel familiar?"

*I didn't know why, then. I had no way of knowing what happened outside
this city, in a book that wasn't mine to know. I only thought, at the
time, that the shape of this sentence resembled the shape of the one
I'd seen yesterday, and that was why it struck me as familiar. I know
now. What I felt wasn't familiarity. This sentence, in the whole of
this world, had truly happened exactly once. I was only standing at
the far end of its ripples.*

---

Mena quietly laid another sheet beside that line. A sheet far older
than the others.

"…This isn't something I wrote three years ago," Mena said. "It's much
older than that. I wrote this sentence, a very long time ago, in
another city. Or rather — I didn't write it exactly. Someone took what
I'd written."

"…Took it?"

"…Yes," Mena said. "At the time, I didn't think of it as theft. I just
thought it had flowed away somewhere. Records flow, that's how it
works. I didn't know how far it had gone."

"…And now it's turned up here."

Mena nodded, very slowly.

"…It's turned up here," Mena said. "A single sentence that started in
a city before I was even born has made its way into this Harvest's
reputation data. I find that frightening, Xene."

*That was the first time I dimly sensed what this whole book was
heading toward. This wasn't the story of one city. I couldn't put that
feeling into exact words that night. Even now, years later, I still
choose my words very carefully around it.*

---

"…Mena," I asked. "Are all seven of these going the same direction?"

Mena scanned through the table again.

"…Yes," she said. "Every single one."

"…None the other way? Someone who did something wrong, and it got
turned into something good?"

Mena shook her head.

"…No," Mena said. "I checked for that too. All seven lean the same
way, without exception. Absence of explanation becomes absence of
remorse. Silence becomes guilt. Never once the other direction."

I slowly felt the weight of that. If it were one, you could call it a
mistake. If it were two, you could call it coincidence. If seven, and
all seven leaned the same way, it was no longer either.

"…If it were a mistake," I said, "shouldn't it sometimes go wrong the
other way too?"

Mena looked at me. Something in her face, for the first time,
acknowledged the point.

"…That's right," Mena said. "I spent all night thinking that too.
Mistakes scatter both ways. This only ever leans one way. Something
that only ever leans one way isn't a mistake."

---

Mena tucked those two sheets away into the deepest compartment of her
case, apart from the others, so they wouldn't mix.

"…Mena," I asked. "Is this the story from before? The one you didn't
tell me yesterday?"

Mena looked at me for a moment, then nodded. Very slightly.

"…That's right," Mena said. "I thought, at the time, that this
sentence had ended in exactly one city, exactly one case. I never went
back to that city after that. I couldn't."

"…Why not?"

Instead of answering, Mena slowly ran her hand over the lid of the
case.

"…I'll tell you later," Mena said. "For now, just know that this
sentence has traveled all the way here. Even after I left that city,
this sentence kept walking on, without me."

That struck me as strange, hearing it talked about like a person. A
sentence walking. But from that day on, I really did begin to feel
that way. As if sentences had feet, and even when the one who wrote
them stopped, they kept walking on their own.

---

"…So what is this?" I asked. "Is someone doing it on purpose?"

Mena didn't answer for a long time.

"…I don't know that yet either," Mena said at last. "If it's
deliberate, then who? I don't think there's a single hand behind these
seven. The cities are too far apart, and too much time separates them."

"…But the direction is the same."

"…Yes. That's the strangest part," Mena said. "Many hands. One
direction."

"…Doesn't that frighten you?" I asked.

Mena closed her eyes for a moment.

"…It does," Mena said. "I always believed that if I ran into a wrong,
that wrong would have a face. So I kept looking for the face. This
city's administrator's face. That city's archivist's face. But right
now, I'm facing something with no face at all."

"…Can you even run into something like that?"

"…You can," Mena said. "The most frightening things, most of the time,
have no face. When there's a face, a person can hate it, or fear it —
and that makes it, in a way, less dangerous. When there's no face,
there's nowhere for the hatred to land, and so no one ever tries to
stop it."

I held onto those words for a long time. Many hands, one direction.
It struck me as even more frightening than if someone had simply
ordered it. If there were an order-giver, you could find him. If no one
had ordered anything, and everyone still leaned the same way, there
was no one to go and find at all.

I ran my eyes back over the sentences once more. Did not explain,
refused to explain. Said nothing about repayment, expressed no intent
to repay. Covered for her sibling, harbored an accomplice. Said nothing
that day, evading testimony. Laid side by side, the four pairs looked
like they'd been struck from the same mold. Different cities, different
eras, and yet one and the same mold.

"…Then why does it only ever lean this way?" I asked. "Why never the
other?"

Mena thought for a moment.

"…I've thought about this a long time too," Mena said. "My guess is,
it's easier to see the side with no explanation as bad. Hearing an
explanation makes a person complicated. You come to know the reason.
With no explanation, you can just decide. Fast, and simple."

"…What does that have to do with it?"

"…I told you the Roll likes fast," Mena said. "A fast judgment always
leans toward the simple. And the simple side is almost always the bad
one. To see something as good, you have to look a long time. Seeing it
as bad, you can do at a glance."

I thought of Lytron, hearing that. What he'd written about me.
*Nothing out of the ordinary.* Maybe that had come from the very same
laziness. Not laziness that decided to write something bad — laziness
that never bothered to write something good, either, because there was
nothing bad to write.

*I didn't understand all of that, that day. I still can't say I
understand it completely. But I think of it every time I look at this
world since. Many hands. One direction.*

---

Mena wrote one more line at the bottom of the table. I watched her hand
move.

**"No explanation → no remorse. Silence → guilt. Seven for seven.
No counter-example."**

"…What will you do with this?" I asked.

"…I don't know yet," Mena said. "I'm not trying to fix this right now.
I don't even think I'll be able to."

"…Then why go this far?"

Mena set down her pen and looked at me.

"…So that later, someone can say the wrong thing was wrong, you have
to leave behind what was right," Mena said. "I'm not building this
table to change anything right now. I'm building it so that someone,
someday, can compare against it — and for that, I have to leave this
shape exactly as it is, right now."

I remembered something Mena had told me before. *Even if I can't hold
onto it, I have to leave behind what was right at the start.* I hadn't
understood that well then. Now I thought I understood it a little.

---

I heard the field outside the window. Machines running, scales
chiming. I realized, suddenly, that out there, today was flowing on
exactly as any other day. While seven sentences lay side by side in
this room, the field kept ripening without a care.

"…Not going to the corner today?" Mena asked.

"…I'll stay here today."

Mena nodded, and leaned back over her papers. Her hand began to move
again. Slow, and certain. From last night until now, without rest,
that hand had never changed its pace.

I sat beside her and pictured something like a river flowing between
this city and every other one. Mena had said something like that
before — that cities seemed independent, but their Rolls weren't
independent from each other at all. That day, for the first time, I
knew which way that river flowed. Always the same way. From silence
toward guilt.

---

I left the archive close to evening. Mena stayed behind. For the first
time, I told her I would go on ahead, and left.

On the way home through the field, I stopped by the corner for a
moment. He was there, as always, moving his hands quietly.

"…You didn't come today," he said. Not a scolding tone.

"…I was helping Mena with something," I said.

He stopped his hands and looked at me.

"…What kind of something?"

I thought for a moment about how to explain.

"…Something Mena wrote keeps getting twisted into something worse in
different cities," I said. "Seven times now. All the same direction."

He was quiet a moment, hearing that. He looked down at the few stalks
of grain in his hand.

"…Seven times," he repeated. It sounded like a thought spoken aloud.

"…Do you know about this? This kind of thing?"

He looked at me. There was something old in that look. It wasn't the
first time I'd seen it. But that day it lingered unusually long.

"…I know," he said at last. "For a very long time."

"…Then why doesn't anyone fix it?" I asked again. My voice came out
sharper than I meant.

He slowly added the stalks in his hand to my sack. A little more than
he needed to.

"…Fixing and leaving something behind are different things," he said.
"I've watched that difference for a very long time."

He started moving his hands again.

"…Ask Mena directly about that," he said. "She knows it better than I
do."

*I found that answer strange at the time. He always spoke like someone
who knew more than he was saying. That day, he took what he knew and
handed it off to Mena instead. I would only come to understand, much
later, why that was.*

---

At home, Pikra was waiting by the door.

"…Late again today," Pikra said.

"…Mena found something overnight," I said. "Seven. Her own words,
twisted. All in the same direction."

Pikra set down what she'd been working on.

"…Seven of them." Pikra's voice was low.

"…You don't seem surprised."

"…It's not something to be surprised at," Pikra said. "One, it's a
mistake. Two, coincidence. Seven, and it already means those cities are
all connected to each other somewhere. I've had a dim sense of that
since I was young."

I was startled at that.

"…How?"

Pikra looked at the hearth fire for a moment.

"…When I was young, something I heard in one city came back to follow
me around in a completely different one, years later," Pikra said. "At
the time I thought rumor just travels fast. Hearing your story now, I
think maybe it wasn't rumor at all."

I held that thought for a long time.

"…Why didn't you tell me that back then?" I asked. "You'd gone through
something like that."

Pikra smiled a little. A bitter smile.

"…Would telling you have changed anything," Pikra said. "You were
young, and I didn't want to hand you that weight along with it. Some
things, staying quiet is how you share the burden."

I had no answer to that. I had always thought Pikra's silences were
just her nature. They weren't. Some silences were a choice.

*That night, before sleep, I thought again of that river. Flowing
between city and city, invisible. That night, for the first time, I
felt its true size, and it frightened me. Something I could never have
imagined, living outside these walls.*

*I kept coming back that evening to that table. Seven lines. No
counter-example. I think those two lines run straight through the
middle of this book. I didn't feel their size that night. I do now.*

*I fell asleep having already decided the first thing I'd say to Mena
the next morning. Then just fix it. I thought it was an obvious
question. I wouldn't learn how naive it was until the following day.*
