# Chapter 28. That Isn't What I Wrote

> _Previously_ — Mena found a record in this city's reputation field
> that quoted her name. The sentence was smooth. It looked flawless.
> Mena said she needed to confirm whether it matched what she had
> actually written, and decided to wait until daylight.

The next morning, I went straight to the archive without even filling
the water jar.

The walk was short, but I slowed my steps more than once along the
way. I didn't know exactly why. My heart simply didn't want to go
forward. I couldn't explain it to myself then. I know now. I already
knew it wasn't going to be good news. And knowing that, I still walked
as if arriving late might make the news arrive a little later too.

The archive door stood open. The old man wasn't sweeping this time; he
just stood in the doorway. He saw me and, without a word, tipped his
head toward the inside.

Mena sat in the same seat as the day before. More paper had piled up
on the table. It looked like it had come from some compartment deep in
the case I'd never seen before. Every sheet was old. Some had crumbling
edges; some were splitting along their folds.

"…Did you find it?" I asked.

Mena nodded. The answer didn't come right away.

I could tell this silence was different from yesterday's. Yesterday's
silence had been careful. Today's was the silence of someone who
already knew something. I stood a moment in the doorway and waited. It
seemed right to wait for Mena to speak first.

---

"…Sit down," Mena said at last.

I sat beside her. The papers on the table were far more orderly than
yesterday. Somehow that made me more uneasy, not less. I learned that
day, for the first time, that things arranged too neatly can be more
frightening than things scattered in haste. Watching Mena, I learned
that when a person's mind is unsteady, they reach instead for
something they can set right with their hands. That neatness wasn't
calm. It was a gesture reaching for calm.

Mena slid two sheets to the middle of the table.

One was handwritten. The ink had faded, the letters tilted slightly.
Not the writing of someone rushing — the writing of a hand worn into
its shape by long use. I thought the handwriting looked familiar. It
matched the other pages Mena had shown me exactly.

"…This is what I wrote," Mena said. "A long time ago. I won't say the
name. It doesn't matter now. Or which city."

I read the line.

**"He did not explain what he had done. I did not ask why. That was
not my work to do."**

A short sentence. I didn't know what event it was about. It didn't
seem necessary to know. The sentence said everything on its own. What
someone had done, and what Mena hadn't done.

"…What's wrong with this?" I asked. "It's just stating the facts."

"…That's right," Mena said. "There's nothing wrong with this. Looking
at it again now, I wouldn't change a word. The problem is this one."

Mena slid the other sheet in front of me.

---

This one wasn't handwritten. It was printed — the Roll's distinctive,
smooth typeface. I read the sentence beneath it.

**"Witness testimony, Mena, cited. Subject refused to explain the
act. [Assessment: no remorse.]"**

I read that sentence twice and still couldn't quite place what was
wrong with it. It looked similar. It looked like the same story, told
differently.

"…Isn't this saying about the same thing?" I asked.

Mena looked at me. For the first time, I saw something give way in her
face. Not a collapse. Only, briefly, beneath the calm, something
shook.

"…It isn't the same," Mena said. Her voice was lower than usual. "I
didn't write that he refused to explain. I wrote that he didn't
explain. I didn't know why he hadn't spoken. I never even asked. That
wasn't my work to do."

I still hadn't quite caught the difference.

"…Is refusing and not explaining really different?" I asked.

"…Very different," Mena said. "Not explaining tells you nothing at
all. There might have been a reason, or there might not. He might not
have been able to, or he might have chosen not to. I didn't know, so I
didn't write it. Leaving it not-known was my work."

"…And this one?"

"…This one decided, without knowing," Mena said. "It says he 'refused'
to explain. Refusal means you could, and chose not to. I didn't even
know whether he could have. But here, that's already been decided."

---

I read the last line again as I listened.

**[Assessment: no remorse.]**

"…What's this? This part in the brackets."

"…A classification," Mena said. "The Roll adds it. Below my
testimony. I never once wrote the word remorse in that sentence. I
never even looked at whether he felt remorse or not. That's a matter
of the heart. I don't write the heart. I only write what was done."

"…And the Roll wrote the heart in for you?"

"…In effect, yes," Mena said. "I write 'he did not explain,' and
somewhere — someone, or something — turns that into 'refused to
explain,' and attaches the assessment 'no remorse' beside it. I only
wrote the first of those three steps. The other two aren't mine."

I read the three steps aloud to myself. *Did not explain. Refused to
explain. No remorse.* With each step, the man became a little worse. And
yet at no point between them was there any new fact about what he had
actually done. There had only ever been one fact from the start. That
he had said nothing at all. That single fact had grown three times
heavier by the time it passed through those three steps.

"…Who does this?" I asked. "Is it a person doing it, or does the Roll
just do it on its own?"

"…I don't know either," Mena said. "That's the part that maddens me
most. If a person did it, I could go find that person and ask. If the
Roll did it on its own, I have nowhere to go. Right now I don't even
know which it is."

*That was the first time I began to guess, dimly, at what whoever wrote
Book 3 must have gone through. I won't set down here exactly what that
person discovered — Mena herself never told me the whole of it. I'll
only note what she said that morning: "It wasn't the same shape. But
the direction was the same."*

---

"…What direction?" I asked.

Mena looked out the window for a moment. Morning light lay long across
the papers.

"…The direction that turns an absence of explanation into an absence
of remorse," Mena said. "The direction that turns silence into guilt."

I turned that over for a long time.

"…You said this happened before, once," I said. "What shape did it
take, then?"

Mena looked at me a moment, then shook her head.

"…I won't talk about it today," Mena said. "What I want to say right
now is that this happened in a completely different city, in a
completely different way, and the result is identical. I wanted to
think it was coincidence."

"…And now?"

Instead of answering, Mena set down the paper in her hand.

---

I saw Mena's hand shaking.

It wasn't a large tremor. Just a small slip as she set the paper down.
I had never once seen Mena's hand shake before that. Not pouring
water, not surveying an unfamiliar field, not standing before the
administrator. That hand had always moved the same way. I think I'd
been quietly relying on that, without realizing it — on the hand
always moving at the same pace. Seeing it shake, I felt something I'd
been standing on shake with it.

"…Are you all right?" I asked.

Mena didn't answer. Instead, she laid the two sheets side by side
again. Handwritten and printed. What she had written, and what she had
not. The gesture of laying them side by side was the same gesture as
yesterday. But yesterday it had been the gesture of confirming
something. Today it looked like the gesture of someone enduring
something.

"…This sentence," Mena said at last. "This isn't what I wrote."

Her voice wasn't loud. But something was in it. I couldn't put a name
to it then.

"…That isn't what I wrote." Mena said it again. This time she wasn't
speaking to me. She seemed to be speaking to the paper.

*I remember those words exactly, even now. That isn't what I wrote. So
few syllables. And yet I dimly felt, in that moment, that it was a
sentence shaking the whole of what Mena had done with her life. Mena
was someone who wrote things down. For someone who writes, to find
words she never wrote still standing under her own name — that was a
kind of theft no one else could quite feel.*

---

"…What happened to that person?" I asked. "The one written about here."

"…I don't know," Mena said. "I don't even know who he is. There's no
name. Only a case number."

"…Can't you find him?"

Mena shook her head.

"…I looked by the case number. He's not from this city. He must have
passed through here once, or this city took in the record from
somewhere else."

"…So you don't even know where he is now?"

"…No," Mena said. "I have no idea what standing he received after
this, what he was made to go through, where. There's exactly one thing
I know. That my sentence changed, somewhere along the way."

I felt, for the first time, the full size of that helplessness. Mena
couldn't resent anyone. Couldn't go find anyone. A wrong had clearly
happened. But whose hands it had happened in, no one knew. A wrong with
no face to it.

I had always thought, until that day, that a wrong always had someone's
face attached to it. That was how it had been outside the settlement.
If someone blocked a water channel, that person had a face. If someone
stole grain, that person had a face. If there was a face, you could go
and confront it. You could get angry. That day, for the first time, I
learned there was such a thing as a faceless wrong. And that it lasted
far longer than a wrong with a face.

*After that, I began to see everything in this city differently. The
nothing at the corner. The "nothing out of the ordinary" beside my
name. All of it was a faceless wrong. A wrong you couldn't grab hold of
anyone and ask why about. I was, that day, only just beginning to
understand that this was the thing this city did best.*

"…If you can't find him," I asked, "does this just stay this way
forever?"

Mena gave no answer. I understood the silence was the answer.

---

Something else occurred to me, and it frightened me.

"…Mena," I said. "Could my story get twisted like this in some other
city someday?"

Mena looked at me. She hadn't expected the question.

"…Why do you think that?" Mena asked.

"…I might leave this city someday too," I said. "Then whatever gets
written about me would flow somewhere else. If someone changed it
there, I wouldn't have any way of knowing."

Mena was quiet a moment.

"…It's possible," she said at last. "I don't lie to you. It's
possible, Xene."

"…Then why do you keep writing? It might all get changed anyway."

For the first time, Mena smiled a little at that. A sad smile.

"…If I stop writing because it might get changed," Mena said, "then
there's no original left to be changed in the first place. I'm the one
who leaves the original. I can't stop what anyone does with it later.
But if there were no original at all, I couldn't have laid these two
sentences side by side today and compared them."

Listening, I began to understand a little why Mena spent so long
arranging her papers in order. That order was the only thread that
might let someone, later — maybe Mena herself — compare something
against something else.

*That was the first time I dimly felt that recording wasn't a matter
of protecting something. It was a matter of leaving it behind. You
might not be able to protect it. But you could leave it. I would hold
onto the difference between those two for a long time to come.*

---

Mena carefully returned the papers to her case.

"…Xene," she said. "I spent the whole day yesterday confirming this. I
wanted to be certain. I kept hoping I'd made a mistake."

"…It wasn't a mistake."

"…No," Mena said. "It wasn't a mistake."

Mena closed the lid of the case. The sound was quiet. But it felt to me
like it would stay in the room a long time.

"…What will you do now?" I asked.

Mena looked at me for a moment. What remained of the tremor in her
face was slowly settling. She was returning to her usual calm. But not
completely. I chose not to press further, out of some respect for that
incompleteness.

"…I have to keep looking," Mena said. "This won't be the only one.
That's what frightens me most."

*I didn't fully understand what that meant then. The next day I would
know exactly the size of it. What Mena found that day wasn't just one
thing at all.*

---

Mena said she wanted to be alone for a while. I knew there was no ill
will in that. I thought of my water jar and went outside.

The sun was strong. I started toward the well, then found my feet
turning toward the field instead. Habit. I walked all the way to the
corner without even the jar.

Lytron was there. On patrol, it looked like — a handheld terminal in
his hand, watching the machines. He raised a hand to wave at me, then
stopped.

"…Is something wrong?" he asked. He'd asked as soon as he saw my face.

"…No," I said. It was a lie. I hadn't wanted to lie that day, but I
didn't know how to explain, so I said it anyway.

"…You're coming from the archive," he said. Not a question. A
statement.

"…How did you know?"

"…There's talk going around," he said. "That Mena found something
serious. I heard the archive keeper reported something to a superior
this afternoon."

I was startled at that. I hadn't even thought yet that this might leak
outside.

"…What did he report?" I asked.

"…I don't know that much," he said. "I only heard the rumor."

*That was the first time I felt that what Mena had found no longer
belonged only to Mena. What had happened behind the archive door was
already walking around out here, wearing a different shape entirely. I
saw, that very day, with my own eyes, how quickly something like that
could deform.*

"…I don't know the details either," I said. "It's Mena's business."

Lytron nodded. He didn't press further. But after a moment's
hesitation, he spoke again.

"…I should go see Mena myself soon," he said. "You said my name's in
there somewhere too."

"…Yes. You should."

I let that pass lightly at the time. I had no idea that the day he
actually went to see her would become another starting point for
everything that followed.

I crouched at the corner for a moment. I sifted a few handfuls of dirt
with my fingertips. Nothing there, same as always. Strangely, that
nothing was a comfort to me that day. At least this dirt was the same
today as it had been yesterday.

---

That evening I went home empty-handed, without even the water jar.
Pikra noticed first.

"…Where's the water?" Pikra asked.

Only then did I remember I hadn't taken the jar out that morning.

"…I forgot," I said.

Pikra looked at me a moment by the hearth. Her eyes read something in
my face.

"…That recording woman again?" Pikra asked.

"…Yes."

I told her, briefly, what had happened that day. That Mena had
confirmed her own sentence had been changed. How it had been changed.
Pikra listened without stopping her hands. Only once I'd finished did
her hands slow.

"…She must have had a hard day, that woman," Pikra said.

"…Yes. Her hand was shaking. I'd never seen that before."

Pikra nodded. She was quiet a moment, then spoke.

"…I know that feeling," Pikra said. "Something not yours going around
in your own mouth. How hollow it feels."

"…Has that happened to you too?"

Pikra's hands stopped. The hearth fire cast a brief shadow across her
face.

"…It has," Pikra said. "But I never tried to find a way to fix it. I
didn't have the strength for that. She's different. She has the
strength to search. That might be harder for her, not easier."

"…Why?"

"…Without strength, you give up quickly," Pikra said. "With strength,
you have to try everything you can before you give up. You don't know
yet how long that takes, or how many times you break along the way."

I thought of Mena's shaking hand again, listening to that. Pikra's
words felt true. Mena wasn't someone who could give up yet. I couldn't
decide, that night, whether that was fortunate or something to grieve
over.

*I thought about that sentence late into the night. That isn't what I
wrote. I didn't know, then, that those five words would carry the
weight of this entire book. I only fell asleep hoping Mena would return
to that plain, calm face again.*

*I saw the two sentences in my dreams that night too. Did not explain.
Refused to explain. Two sentences lying side by side, and then, quietly,
one settling over the other. When I woke that morning, I still
couldn't quite shake the dream.*

*Writing this chapter, I find myself circling back, again and again,
to those five words. That isn't what I wrote. I'll hear that same
sentence, later on, from a mouth other than Mena's. That is much later.
For now, I'll only set down Mena's trembling hand that night, and the
way that hand worked to steady itself again.*
