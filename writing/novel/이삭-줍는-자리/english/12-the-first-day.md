# Chapter 12. The First Day

> _Previously_ — From the old man, Xene hears of the corner clause,
> Article Nine of the law — that the corners of a field are to be left
> for those without standing. For the first time since entering the
> city, she feels something like hope, and falls asleep looking
> forward to the day ahead.

The next morning, I woke before the sun had fully risen.

Pikra was still asleep. I got up quietly, drank a mouthful of water,
and went out the door. I didn't say where I was going. Nothing was
certain yet, and I thought there was no good in speaking it.

*I tried, more than once, to write this morning differently — more
reverently, or more full of feeling. I crossed it all out. That
morning I simply woke, ordinarily, and put on my shoes, ordinarily.
There was no special premonition.*

---

Almost no one was out yet when I left. Traces of water still lay on
the street. I couldn't tell if someone had sprinkled it in the night,
or if it was only dew.

The air was cool. I liked that coolness, even knowing the heat would
come back by noon. I liked walking through that early chill. The dust
hadn't risen yet either. The pale haze I'd seen on the road lay still
that morning.

It had been a long time since I'd set out to do something. Since
coming into the city, I'd always been either dragged somewhere or
pushed out of somewhere. This time I was walking toward a destination
I'd chosen myself.

I walked about half an hour from the wall. The direction the old man
had pointed.

The road was different from the market street I'd seen the day before.
Fewer people. Grain warehouses stood in a row instead, their doors all
shut. I passed those doors trying not to imagine how much lay stacked
inside. I didn't manage it well.

Past the warehouses, the fields began. I was struck again by their
size. Seeing it from the hill and walking straight into it were
entirely different feelings.

The wheat was nearly all ripe. A deep gold color. Seeing that color, I
knew the harvest would begin soon.

*I remember that color still, even now. That field, that morning, was
truly beautiful. I'll call this city frightening, many times, in this
book. But that fright and that beauty don't cancel each other out.
Both were true. I carried both truths with me, walking, that day.*

---

I walked along the edge of the field. Not knowing which corner was
which, I circled it for a long while.

Once I spotted something that looked like a stake and ran toward it.
It was only a marker for the water channel. I swallowed the
disappointment and kept walking.

People were already moving inside the field. The harvest hadn't
properly begun yet, but some were checking something. I tried not to
meet their eyes. I didn't know why, myself. Probably I wasn't sure I
was even allowed to be there.

After a long while walking, I saw a wooden stake. Visible even from a
distance. It looked different from the other stakes.

I hurried my steps toward it.

Coming close, I saw small letters carved into the stake. I read them
slowly. Some article, some clause — I couldn't take in the exact
wording, then. But I knew, at least, that this was the clause the old
man had spoken of.

*I had pictured this moment for a long time. Every night, for three
days. Now that the moment had actually come, I found, strangely, that
I could only stand there, saying nothing. The stake wasn't old. It
looked freshly new. That newness comforted me, at the time. It looked,
to me, like proof that this law was still being carefully kept, even
now.*

---

I stood before the stake and looked inside.

There was ground.

No wheat.

At first I thought I'd seen it wrong. I blinked a few times and looked
again. Still nothing. Only dirt, and the marks of things pressed into
it.

I crouched down. My hand dug through the dirt. Something hard came
under my fingers. A stalk. A wheat stalk, trampled and broken. Some
had only the stem left, the head torn away. Some still had the head
attached, but crushed flat, the grain nowhere to be seen.

I found a few more like that. Gathered in my palm. It didn't come to
more than a few handfuls.

There were footprints in the ground too. Not human footprints. Broad,
flat tracks. I recognized them — the marks of a machine that had come
to the boundary and turned back. The line stopped exactly at the
stake. Not a hand's width over.

*I stared at that track for a long while. That precision was what hurt
worse. If the machine had drifted a little further in by accident and
then turned back, I could at least have understood the accident. But
that line was straight, as if drawn with a ruler. It wasn't an
accident. It was arithmetic.*

I walked the rest of the inside of the corner too, in case there was
more in the opposite side. There wasn't. Every corner was the same.
Trampled stalks, crushed heads, and dirt.

In one spot, the earth had been dug through, more than anywhere else.
It looked like the trace of someone who'd been there before me. A
handprint remained. I laid my own hand over it. It was about the same
size.

*I never learned whose hand that was. I still don't. I only know they
crouched, as I did, and dug through this same dirt, as I did. And
probably went home empty-handed, as I would. I never met that person.
But that one handprint made me feel, somehow, as if I'd shared
something with them. They too, probably, had heard of this place from
someone like the old man. And come, hoping, as I had, and crouched
before this same dirt, as I did.*

---

I stayed crouched there a long while.

I looked around further. Wondering if I'd come to the wrong place, if
the real corner was somewhere else. I checked the stake again. It was
the right place. The mark was exact. The boundary was clear.

The law was being kept. That place had, in fact, been left. There
were no marks of any machine ever having been brought inside it.
Only, there was nothing worth leaving, in there.

*That was the first time, I think, that I saw this city's true face.
Until then, I'd believed that if the law wasn't broken, everything was
all right. That day I learned that a law can go unbroken and still
leave a place entirely empty.*

---

I sat there a long while, then wrapped the few handfuls I'd gathered
in a small cloth. Better than nothing. I told myself I should still
carry it home.

As I gathered it, I did a strange arithmetic. How many spoonfuls of
gruel these few handfuls would make. It didn't look like it would come
to even one day's worth. I found myself a stranger, doing that
arithmetic. A few days before, I hadn't known how to make calculations
like this at all. This city was teaching me.

*Now I know. This was the first skill a person without standing
learns in this city. The arithmetic of making what little you have
last through days you have nothing. I learned that arithmetic for the
first time that day, and for a very long time after, I couldn't put
it down.*

Getting up, my legs had gone numb. I didn't know how long I'd been
sitting. The sun had climbed high.

Voices carried from far off. Someone working, on the other side of the
field. Machine sounds mixed in. I felt, freshly, how close that empty
place stood to those sounds. Over there, abundance. Here, emptiness.
The line between them was a single stake.

*I've never forgotten that distance. A few steps, that was all. A few
steps, and abundance and emptiness were divided. And crossing those
few steps was forbidden by law. Cross it, and you became a thief. I
didn't want to become a thief.*

I thought, suddenly, of the day my husband taught me the sickle. Don't
cut too clean, he'd said, spill a little. What you spill isn't
wasted, he'd told me. It's left behind.

I'd said, before, that I took that for a joke, that day. I never
thought I'd stand here, before this empty corner, and think of it
again.

*He knew that spilling and leaving something behind were the same
act. This city had turned the two exactly backward. It took pride in
never spilling, never leaving anything. That was the first time I felt,
in my body, how different the world he'd lived in was from this
city's. The law used the same words. Leave the corner of the field.
But the meaning of that leaving had changed entirely.*

A single grain came up out of the dirt. Half broken. I set it on my
palm and looked at it a long while. This was the whole of what the law
had left for me. I searched for someone to blame it on. I couldn't
find anyone. Blaming the machine was pointless — the machine was only
a machine. Blaming the law was pointless — the law was being kept.
Blaming a person was pointless — I didn't even know who that person
would be.

*That was the first time I understood there is a grief with no one to
blame. It's far heavier than a grief with someone to blame. With
nowhere to send your anger, that anger, in the end, turns back inward.*

---

Walking home, I thought again of the old man's words. *No point.* I'd
let it pass, at the time. Now I knew exactly what it meant.

I tried not to cry, walking. Whether I managed it, I don't know. My
face was hot.

*I won't say, in this passage, whether I cried or not. There's a
reason. Whether I cried or not isn't what matters here. What mattered
was whether I would go back to that place again. That day, I walked
home without having decided that yet. I kept holding that one grain in
my hand the whole way.*

---

I came home to find Pikra waiting at the door.

"…Where were you?" Pikra asked. Worry was plain in her voice.

I slipped the cloth in my hand into my pocket.

"…Just went for a walk," I said.

Pikra studied my face. She looked as if she wanted to ask something,
and held it back.

"…Dinner's ready," Pikra said. "Let's eat."

We ate together again that evening. I said nothing. Pikra asked
nothing. We ate dinner like that, and lay down like that. As if
nothing had happened at all.

I set the few handfuls, wrapped in cloth, down quietly in one corner
of the room. I don't know if Pikra saw them. Even if she had, she
wouldn't have said anything. I didn't eat that handful that night. Only
the next morning did I mix it into the gruel. The amount was so small
I could barely tell it was there at all.

*At the time, I thought that was consideration. I learned later. It
wasn't consideration. It was that Pikra didn't know the answer either.
It had been too long since she'd left this city. She only knew the
corner clause was still there. She didn't know what was left in the
corner.*

---

I lay awake in the dark, eyes open. Beside me, Pikra's breathing was
steady.

I went back over the day in my mind. Waking before dawn. Walking half
an hour. Seeing the stake. Seeing there was nothing there. Gathering a
few handfuls. Walking home again.

The law had been kept. That was the strangest part. I thought I would
rather have been deceived by someone — then I could have hated the one
who deceived me. But no one had deceived me. The corner really had
been left. There was simply nothing inside it.

I lay in the dark thinking whether to go again tomorrow. At first I
leaned toward not going. The old man had been right. *No point.* It had
been a wasted trip. But thinking a little further, another question
rose. If I don't go, then what will I do. Sit in this room, taking my
half-portion from whatever Pikra shares out, day after day?

I found no answer to that question. Without an answer, I made up my
mind to go again. Not because the hope had returned. Whatever hope I'd
felt that day, I'd already spent it, entirely, in that field. I decided
to go again simply because going would shame me less than not going.

*I thought little of this decision, at the time. Now I know. This
decision made the months that followed. I didn't go out because hope
was there — I went out to test whether I could go, even with no hope
at all. I didn't know the difference, then. Now I think it's the most
important difference in this whole book.*

I turned onto my side and looked at Pikra's back. I had come this far,
following her. When she told me three times to go back, I hadn't gone.
If someone asked whether I regretted that choice, I would have said no,
even that night. Only that night was the first time I paid the price
of that choice, in my own body.

*I close the first part of this book on this chapter. Part One was the
story of my following this woman into the city. Following my mother,
into a place I knew would cost me my standing. I didn't know, then,
exactly what that price was. Now I do. The price wasn't expulsion. It
was standing, every day, before an empty corner.*

*A different story begins from here. The story of going out, day
after day, to a field where nothing falls. I set down that first day
today. I went out the next day too. And the day after that. Why, I'll
let come clear, little by little, in the part that follows. For now
I'll only set down this much — I came home empty-handed, and said
nothing, and the next day, went out again.*
