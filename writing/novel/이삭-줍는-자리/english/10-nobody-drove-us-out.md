# Chapter 10. Nobody Drove Us Out

> _Previously_ — Xene's standing computes to zero. The administrator
> explains: it isn't expulsion, only a refusal to renew. He was
> courteous. That courtesy frightened Xene more than anything else
> had.

Inside the gate was another city again.

Seen from outside, the city had been one great, distant mass. Inside,
that mass became roads, became houses, became people. Among all of it
I lost my footing for a moment.

Pikra took my hand and led me.

"…Housing first," Pikra said.

---

The housing office wasn't far from the gate. Another device stood
there. That was the third device I'd seen that day alone.

Pikra held up her confirmation card. Something appeared on the screen.

"…Alone?" the person at the device asked.

Pikra glanced at me. Very briefly.

"…Yes," Pikra said.

I said nothing to that answer. I knew it wasn't a lie. On paper, Pikra
really was alone. I was a person who existed nowhere on paper.

*I remember that moment still. It would be a lie to say it didn't
hurt. But I didn't resent Pikra for it, even then. She'd only stated a
fact. It was the fact that hurt me, not her.*

---

The room was small. One room. Near the wall, in an old building, on
the third floor. The stairs were narrow and dark.

Inside, Pikra set down our things first. Then she looked at me.

"…I was worried whether we could live here at all," Pikra said. "Even
if the paper only says me, no one comes in and tells you to leave. No
one checks inside a room."

"…Are you sure?"

"…I'm sure," Pikra said. "That isn't what this city does. This city
only asks at the door. It doesn't ask what's inside."

Strangely, that gave me comfort.

*I would learn how shallow that comfort was, later. No one checking
inside the room wasn't kindness. It was indifference. Not thinking
something worth checking is also a form of indifference.*

---

That evening, Pikra came back with our first ration, using her
confirmation card. One sack. A little flour mixed with dried beans.

"…This is one person's share," Pikra said, setting the sack down.
"There isn't a separate share for you."

I heard that and tried not to think about being hungry. It didn't
work well.

We shared that one person's share between the two of us, that night.
The amount was cut in half. Pikra kept pushing more of her own portion
toward me. I let her, pretending I wasn't giving in easily.

*That night I learned hunger all over again. There'd been hungry days
at the settlement too. But that was from a failed harvest. It wasn't
that here. The fields were overflowing with wheat. That wheat simply
had no road that led to me.*

---

The next day, I went out alone. Pikra had come down with a fever and
was lying in. No surprise, after a full day's walk.

I just walked. No destination. I only wanted to see what shape this
city took.

The streets were clean. That was the first thing I noticed. Back at
the settlement, the road was always mixed with dirt and animal dung.
Here there was none of that. The pavement was smooth. No litter in
sight.

*Now I know. It wasn't that there was no trash — it was that there
were people who cleaned it. I didn't see a single one of them that
day. They worked in hours no one watched.*

At the edge of the market, I saw people carrying loads. Sacks on their
backs, hauling. They didn't seem to be paid on any regular wage. When
the work was done, the owner of the goods pressed something into their
hand and walked off. A few coins.

I watched that scene a long while. The one who'd carried the load
didn't even count the coins before pocketing them. I found that
strange. If the money were precious enough to count, you'd count it.

*Now I know. There was no need to count. Whatever the amount, they
already knew it was all they'd get that day. Where there's no set
wage for a job, there's no room to dispute it either.*

I watched that porter walk off looking for the next load. No one
asked his name. Once the work was done, he simply became a nobody
again.

In another alley, a few children were picking up things off the
ground. Fruit peels, wood scraps, unidentifiable things. At first I
thought it was a game. Coming closer, I saw it wasn't. Some of the
things they picked were genuinely useful, and went into a sack.

I watched those children, and my eyes met one child's for a moment.
The child looked at me briefly, then turned away without any
expression. Not wariness, not welcome. Just a look that knew I was
nothing to it at all.

At the mouth of one alley, I saw someone like myself for the first
time.

An old man. Sitting against a wall. His clothes were worn but clean.
He was thin, but not the wasted look of someone starving to death.

I passed by and slowed my step. The old man looked up at me.

"…First time here?" the old man asked.

"…Yes," I said. "…How did you know?"

"…Your shoes," the old man said. "Worn different from ours."

I didn't understand and stayed quiet.

"…We all wear our shoes about the same way," the old man said. "We
walk the same rounds, over and over. Yours are still shoes that go
anywhere."

---

"…What do you do for food?" I asked.

"…Go by the market around closing time," the old man said. "What
didn't sell, what's a shame to throw out — some people set it aside.
Not bad people. Kind, if anything."

"…If kind people are giving it to you, isn't that all right?"

The old man looked at me a moment, then laughed. A bitter laugh.

"…Giving from kindness and giving you a place are two different
things," the old man said. "They'll give food. But no one registers
you here. No one gives you a room. It just keeps you from going hungry
that one day."

"…Where do you sleep?"

"…Here," the old man said. He gestured at the spot he sat in. "This
wall blocks the wind. When it rains I go to that eave over there."

"…What about the cold days?"

The old man was quiet a moment.

"…Some people didn't make it through the cold ones," the old man
said. Calmly. As though it weren't about himself. "But that wasn't the
city killing them, either. The city didn't do a thing."

*I only found that strange, that day. Now I know. That's the sentence
that became this chapter's title. Nobody drove us out. Nobody killed
anyone. And yet people die. With no one having done anything.*

---

Just then, two people who looked like patrol officers passed through
the alley. Tidy clothes. An unhurried walk.

One of them stopped in front of the old man. I tensed for a moment.
Outside, that kind of stopping usually meant someone about to be
driven off.

"…Good morning, sir," the officer said. Genuinely wishing a good
morning, by the sound of it.

"…So it is," the old man said. As if used to this.

"…Nothing happened overnight?"

"…Nothing."

"…Good to hear." The officer smiled and walked on. Talking with their
partner about something else entirely, in a voice that had already
moved on.

I watched their backs for a long while.

"…What were they here for?" I asked.

"…A patrol," the old man said. "Comes by a couple times a day. Asks if
anything's wrong, and goes."

"…That's all?"

"…Yes, that's all," the old man said. "They're not bad people. They
really do ask because they're concerned. But after asking, they don't
do anything about it. There's nothing for them to do. That isn't their
job."

*I remember that moment precisely, even now. That was the first time
this city's kindness gave me a chill. Those people were sincerely
kind. They genuinely wanted to know if the old man was all right. And
that kindness changed nothing at all. That kindness and indifference
could wear the same face — I saw that for the first time, that day.*

---

"…I've lived here going on fifteen years," the old man said. "In all
that time, no one's ever once told me to leave. Truly. Not once."

"…Then why live this way, here?"

"…Because it's the same everywhere you go," the old man said. "It
isn't that this city is cruel to me. This city does nothing to me at
all. It just doesn't give me anything either. How long that can go on,
you'll find out for yourself soon enough."

I found that frightening. That word, *soon*.

"…Were you born here?"

"…No," the old man said. "I came from somewhere too. Doesn't matter
where, anymore. It's been too long since I came."

"…What's your name?"

The old man smiled a little at that.

"…I have one. No one asks it here. No reason to," the old man said.
"You asked, so I'll tell you sometime."

"…What was it like at first, for you?"

The old man looked up at the sky for a moment.

"…Everyone was kind to me at first," the old man said. "Gave me bread
in passing, told me directions. I thought that was a good sign. But
that was all there was to it. Plenty of kind people. No one gives you
a place."

---

The old man held out half a piece of bread to me.

"…It's all right," I said. "I still have food."

"…Today," the old man said. "Tomorrow, who knows. Take it."

I took it, unable to refuse. Holding it, my hand shook. Not from
hunger.

*I thought a long time that night about why my hand had shaken. I'd
just received help from someone. And it gave me no comfort at all. The
city hadn't driven me out. And this old man had fed me. Both were true
at once. Between those two truths, I felt a chill. This city's
kindness came from people like this old man. It didn't come from the
city itself. The city only hadn't stopped this old man from feeding
me.*

---

I sat a while longer, watching people pass. The old man kept talking.

"…See that one there." He pointed to someone walking quickly, device
in hand. "People like that don't see people like us. Not that they
won't look — they truly don't see. To someone with standing, we look
about the same as a road, or a wall."

"…Because they're bad people?"

"…No," the old man shook his head. "If they were bad, that'd be
better, even. A bad person at least sees you. Those people don't. No
reason to."

I turned that over. I thought of what Pikra had said, on the road.
*Strange isn't strange here.* I'd taken that, then, for nothing at
all. Now I was learning how heavy that nothing could be. Not being
seen, and being chosen not to be seen — the results looked the same,
but they were different things. From then on I tried to tell the two
apart.

I stayed with the old man a little longer. The sun was going down.
People's steps quickened around us. Everyone was on their way back
somewhere.

"…Aren't you going back somewhere?" I asked.

The old man smiled.

"…This is where I go back to," the old man said. "While everyone
else is going back to their own place, I'm already at mine. Sometimes
that's a good thing. Sometimes it's a sad one."

I had nothing to say to that.

"…It's right there in the law, you know. That article," the old man
said, suddenly.

"…Which article?"

The old man looked at me, then waved a hand.

"…Next time," the old man said. "I've talked too much today."

*I kept thinking about that, that night. Some article. I didn't know
what it was, then. I only felt, from the old man's eyes, that it
wasn't a bad thing. For the first time, a different light passed
through his eyes. Not resignation, not kindness. Something else. I
had no name for it, then.*

---

I came home to find Pikra awake.

"…Where were you?" Pikra asked.

"…Just walking," I said. I didn't mention the old man. I didn't know,
even then, why I didn't.

Pikra studied my face.

"…Did something happen?"

"…No." I quietly hid the half a loaf in my hand. "…Just seeing the
city."

*I ate that bread that night, in secret, while Pikra slept. Guilt
came with it. I didn't quite know why. Maybe it was that this was the
first thing I'd received in this city, and it hadn't come from the
city at all, but from some stranger, an old man. That fact left me
strangely ashamed.*

Pikra pushed herself up against the wall.

"…Xene," Pikra called.

"…Yes."

"…If it's hard, say it's hard," Pikra said. "You can, with me."

I couldn't answer that. Saying it wasn't hard would be a lie, and
saying it was hard would only make Pikra feel worse. In the end I said
nothing at all.

*Looking back, I should have said something, then. But I was already
learning, by then, that in this city, sometimes not speaking is
safer. I didn't know, then, how long that lesson would follow me.*

That night, before sleep, I thought again of the old man's words. An
article in the law. I would learn what it was within a few days.
