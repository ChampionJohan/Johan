# Chapter 15. Lytron

> _Previously_ — Xene went to the corner every day. She stopped counting
> the days. Then, one day, she saw a stranger on the far side of the
> field. She didn't know, at the time, that he had been watching her too.

On the twelfth day, that man walked over to me.

The morning was unusually clear. The whole field was visible at a glance — wheat ripened the same gold color out to the far edge, and above it three harvesters moved, small and distant. I was sitting in my usual spot at the corner, sifting the dirt as always.

I heard footsteps. At first I assumed they were only passing by. But they were heading straight for me. I hesitated over whether to stand or stay seated, and in the end just stayed as I was. There was nowhere to hide my dirt-covered hands.

Up close, his clothes were plainly different — no fading anywhere, good material. His shoes weren't worn at the toe either. In his hand was something flat, and it was only then that I learned what it was: a Roll terminal. He looked a year or two older than me. His expression was calm in a way that didn't match his age. Nothing hurried about him at all.

*That was the first time in this city that I saw someone walking straight toward me. Until then, people had always passed beside me or gone out of their way around me. No one had come toward me. So it took a few seconds before I even recognized that the steps were meant for me.*

---

"…What are you doing there?"

His voice wasn't loud. Not angry either. Just the voice of someone curious.

"…Gleaning." I said.

"…What?"

I opened my palm to show him. Two grains. One of them cracked.

He looked at it a moment. His expression didn't change. Not surprised, not mocking. He simply looked.

"…What do you do with that?"

"…Eat it."

He looked at me again. Longer this time. As if he were repeating what I'd said to himself, more than once.

"…Two grains?"

"…I gather this much every day." I said. "Some days not even this much."

He tilted his head slightly. Less confused than someone hearing an unfamiliar sum for the first time.

"…That must be hard." he said.

There was no pity in it. It was the tone of someone confirming a fact. I found that easier, actually. If it had been pity, I wouldn't have known how to answer.

---

"…You've been coming here for days." he said. It wasn't a question. It sounded like confirmation.

I found that strange. I thought this was my first time seeing him, but it meant he'd already known about me for days.

"…How do you know?"

"…It's in the records." he said, lifting the terminal slightly. "Entry logs for this zone. If the same person comes in and out at the same time every day for more than ten days, it gets flagged."

*That was when I first learned that my going out every day hadn't been mine alone to know. It had all been recorded somewhere. I found it strange more than frightening. I'd thought no one paid me any attention — but a machine had. Before any person did. I didn't know whether that record was kept under my name or just a spot number. I didn't have the nerve to ask.*

"…I'm assisting with management of this zone." he added. "I check the traffic, the entry logs, the loss rate, that kind of thing, every day. Things end up catching your eye that way. Like someone coming in and out of the same spot at the same hour for more than ten days."

"…There are other people who come every day too, besides me."

"…I know. There are records for them too." he said, unconcerned. "But yours was a little different."

"…What was?"

"…The others taper off after a while. A few days coming, then gone, then a few more, then gone again. You didn't miss a single day."

Hearing that, I felt all over again the fact that I'd been under observation. And at the same time I could tell he hadn't said it to make me out as especially wrong for it. It was simply the tone of someone remarking on a figure that stood out.

---

"…Why do you come every day to a place with nothing in it?"

He asked it without any malice at all, and that was what unsettled me more. If it had been asked unkindly, I'd have found it easier to answer — I could have gotten angry, or ignored him. But he was genuinely curious.

I had no answer.

"…Is it because of the corner clause?" he went on. "The one that says people without standing can glean there."

"…Yes."

"…That is what that place is for." he said. "I know that. Article 9. Only…" he chose his next words. "Only, these days there isn't much left there. Efficiency's improved."

I noticed he said "efficiency's improved" in a tone that was neither boasting nor apologizing. Simply stating a fact.

---

"…Do you know?" I asked. "How high the loss rate is?"

My voice came out sharper than I'd expected, sharp enough to surprise even me. He looked slightly startled. For the first time his expression moved.

"…0.02 percent." he answered. Without hesitation. "It's the figure for the zone I manage."

"…Then you'd know. That I can come every day and get nothing."

"…I know." he said. "But separate from that, I'm still curious. Knowing that, why you still come."

I had no answer to that question. Truly none. Not then.

*Writing this now, I find myself wondering what would have happened if I'd made something up that day. Something like, "I still think there might be something someday," or "It's a right the law gives me." Those kinds of lines did cross my mind at the time. I said none of them out loud. Because they weren't true.*

The silence went on a while. Lytron didn't press. He didn't seem to be waiting for an answer either — he looked more like someone considering his next question.

"…Have you thought about trying a different field?" he asked. "There might be zones with a higher loss rate than this one, not this one."

That question surprised me a little. That he knew loss rates differed from field to field, and that he'd think to tell someone like me.

"…I didn't know there was such a thing." I said.

"…It's not a big difference." he added right away. "They're all similarly low. It won't be very different wherever you go."

I couldn't tell whether that was meant to comfort me or was just information. Thinking about it now, I'd say it was both. In him, the wish to comfort and the wish to state facts precisely didn't seem to be two separate things.

"…Have you been to other fields yourself?" I asked.

"…I manage this one." he said. "I only see the other zones as numbers. I don't go out to them myself."

"…Then how do you know they're all similar?"

Lytron went quiet a moment. It was the first time an answer hadn't come right away.

"…From the reports." he finally said. Something about the answer must have sounded insufficient even to him, because he glanced down at his terminal for a moment after saying it.

*I remember that brief pause. It was the first time I saw that even he didn't know everything. What he knew was numbers, and numbers could be known without ever going out to the field. But what those numbers turned into, once they were sitting in a person's palm — numbers alone couldn't show him that.*

---

He looked at me a moment, then wrote something on the terminal. I felt a stab of unease — wondering what sort of record he was making about me.

"…What are you writing?"

"…Oh, this is something else." he said. "Time of today's zone inspection."

The answer was so ordinary that I felt myself sag a little. I'd thought something significant was happening. It wasn't. To him, I was just one person he'd crossed paths with during that day's inspection.

"…What's your name?" I asked. I don't know why I asked.

"…Lytron." he said. Then he asked back, "And you?"

"…Xene."

Lytron didn't write the name down anywhere. He just nodded once.

"…Xene." he repeated it once. Not the face of someone curious about what it meant. Just the face of someone committing a name to memory.

"…What's your standing?" he asked. Casually, the way you'd ask about the weather.

"…None."

"…Ah." he said, shortly. He didn't look surprised. He might already have known — with an entry log on the terminal, he could hardly have missed it.

He didn't ask anything further. He didn't wear the expression that usually followed hearing someone had no standing — not pity, not wariness. He simply took in one more piece of information. Up to that point, I'd watched people's faces change, however slightly, at the words *no standing.* Some looked away. Some walked faster. Lytron did neither. His was the face of someone confirming what he'd already understood.

*I turned that "ah" over in my mind for a long time. There had truly been nothing in it. I couldn't decide, for a long while, whether that nothing frightened me or was a relief.*

And then he went on his way.

---

I watched his back for a long time.

His walk was unhurried — the walk of someone with nowhere urgent to be. Watching it, I thought: he probably won't think of me again for the rest of today. For me, that conversation had been the first time in twelve days that I'd exchanged words with anyone. For him, it had been one thing among many during an inspection.

*I thought about this a long time that night. How many words does a person exchange in a day? Around then, I rarely crossed ten words in a whole day. Someone like Lytron probably exchanged hundreds. So the few words we'd traded took up a completely different amount of room in his day than they did in mine. I'm not writing this to resent him for it. I'm only recording that it was true.*

*I won't write that this was a bad thing. He hadn't done anything wrong. He'd asked what he was curious about, heard the answer, and gone off to his work. The trouble was that his day and my day didn't rest on the same scale — and that this was no one's fault.*

I sat at the corner a little longer than usual that day. I stopped sifting the dirt and watched the direction he'd walked. Far off, the three harvesters were still moving at the same steady pace. Lytron walked up to one of them and bent over his terminal. He seemed to be checking something, correcting something. The motion looked entirely familiar. Clearly something he did every day.

I thought about how well he must know those machines. If I'd never touched a sickle in my life and were handed one of those, I'd have been afraid. Lytron wasn't afraid of it at all. He handled it the way a person handles their own hand.

*That difference stayed with me a long time. I knew the field by hand. Lytron knew the field by numbers. The same field, and yet we were looking at two different fields.*

---

At home, I told Pikra what had happened. It was the first time I'd told her anything that had happened out in the field.

"…I met someone named Lytron."

Pikra's hands went still.

"…Lytron?"

"…Yes. Why?"

"…Nothing." She started moving again. "The family name sounds familiar. I heard it when I lived here."

"…What kind of family is it?"

Pikra thought for a moment.

"…A good family." she said. "Very high standing. Strange, that a son from a family like that would ask about you."

"…What's a family like that doing out in the field?" I asked. "He didn't look like the people who work out here."

"…High-standing families put their sons through a stretch of that hands-on work sooner or later." Pikra said. "It's supposed to teach them, so that when they end up in a higher seat later, they understand what's below them. That was already the talk when I lived here."

"…So he really does understand it, then?"

Pikra looked at me a moment.

"…Knowing and living through it are different things." she said. "That boy has a house to go home to in the evening. Not sleeping here, the way we do."

I thought she was right. And at the same time it was also true that Lytron hadn't seemed like a bad person. That both of those could exist together — I learned that for the first time that day.

*I lay awake a long time that night. That Lytron wasn't a bad person, and that what Lytron lived through was not what I lived through — these two things didn't clash against each other. I'd believed, up to then, that only bad people made bad things happen. From that day I began, slowly, to learn how wrong that was.*

I didn't quite understand, at the time, why that should be strange at all.

*I understand now. A meeting of two people at the corner of a field, one with the highest standing and one with none, was not a common thing in this city. Rare enough that even he had been curious. I didn't know that then, and so I simply fell asleep thinking I'd met an odd sort of person. I found myself, that night, repeating his name to myself a few times, silently. A name I'd never heard before, and yet, oddly, it fixed itself in my memory the first time.*
