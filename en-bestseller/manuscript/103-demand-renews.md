## The shop whose customers never come back

There's a shop that sells things for babies.

Customers usually come once and then stop coming after a few years.
The child grows and there's nothing left to buy there.

Almost no regulars. The owner rarely has occasion to remember a face.

**That shop has been in the same spot for twenty years.**

The café across the street is the opposite.
There are people who come every day and the owner knows their names.

The owner changes every few years.

Odd. The one with no regulars is still there.
The one with regulars keeps closing.

## The obvious reading — you need regulars to last

**Half right.**

Regulars are good. **They aren't the condition for lasting.**

There's only one condition for lasting.

> **New customers keep appearing.**

The same customers coming back is one way.
Different customers coming is another way.

The second way is much less discussed.
And most steady sellers live on the second way.

## Two kinds of demand

Demand comes in two kinds with different properties.

**Demand that accumulates.** Customers stay and add up.
This month's customers sit on top of last month's.

**Demand that renews.** Customers change over.
This month's customers are not last month's.

| | Accumulating | Renewing |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | Pile up | Turn over |
| When it's going well | Grows fast | Doesn't grow |
| When it's going badly | Shrinks fast | **Doesn't shrink** |
| If you stop | They leak away | It stays the same |
| What it requires | Holding on to them | **Still being there** |

The middle two rows are the point.

Accumulating demand has to grow to live. Stopping growth causes a problem.
**Renewing demand doesn't shrink even though it doesn't grow.**

Not growing sounds like a flaw.
It also means not falling.

## The door

Renewing demand doesn't appear just anywhere.
It appears **where life repeats itself.**

This book calls those places **doors.**

Living alone for the first time.
Starting work for the first time.
A child being born.
Managing people for the first time.
A parent starting to get ill.
Moving house.

These doors open every year.
And every year **a different person** stands in front of one.

Whoever stands there has three things in common.

**One, it's their first time, so they don't know.**
They can't choose for themselves, so they ask.

**Two, they're in a hurry.**
The baby is already born and winter already came. They don't compare for long.

**Three, being wrong is frightening.**
First time through, they don't know what's risky, so they pick the safe thing.

Put those three together and the nature of the competition changes completely.

> **At a door, the better thing does not win.
> The thing that comes to mind first wins.**

## Becoming the default

This book calls the first-to-mind position **the default.**

It's an enormously strong position.
It doesn't sell because it beat the competition; it sells because it is the answer.

And it has a strange property.

**It can't be bought.**

A default is not built by advertising.
It's built by **what the people who already went through that door said.**

Whoever went first tells whoever comes next.
Those remarks pile up, and then it's the default.

So it takes time.
**And that is exactly why it's hard to take.**

A newcomer can build a better object.
A newcomer cannot manufacture ten years of remarks from people who went ahead.

## Why is such a good position empty?

Worth stating plainly.

If the position is that good, why isn't everyone crowding into it?

**Because it's boring.**

Renewing demand doesn't explode.
This year and next year look almost the same.

In numbers there's no growth rate.
Nothing to announce, nothing to boast about.

People who put up money want to see something getting bigger.
So that kind of money rarely arrives here.

> **This position isn't uncontested because the barrier is high.
> It's uncontested because it looks unappealing.**

Hold on to that sentence.
The last argument of this book, in Chapter 20, comes out of it.

Where large capital doesn't come, **whoever stayed longest wins.**
And staying a long time costs less capital, not more.

## When this demand ends

Renewing demand ends too. It ends differently.

It rarely ends because a competitor beat it.
**It ends because the door closes.**

It ends when people stop going through that situation.
It also ends when they still go through it but stop solving it with your thing.

The sentence from the last chapter returns.

**The need doesn't change; the method does.**

Did the door close, or is the door open and something else became the answer?
Telling those two apart is the work of Part Two.

So what a steady business checks each year is not the competition.

> **The question isn't "who is the competitor"
> but "is that door still open."**

## The short version

| Question | The answer for renewing demand |
|---|---|
| Who is the customer | **Whoever stood at that door for the first time this year** |
| Why do they buy | First time, in a hurry, afraid of being wrong |
| What wins | Not the better thing — **the first thing to mind** |
| How do you get that spot | Remarks from everyone who went through before |
| Why is it uncontested | It doesn't grow. **It looks boring** |
| When does it end | Not competition — **when the door closes** |

## One Line Today

Think of something you did **for the first time** in the past year.
It doesn't have to be large. First time is enough.

Write down what you bought then.
Then write down how you found out about it.

- You saw an advertisement
- You searched
- **You asked a person**

Which of the three was it?

If you found out by asking a person, that thing currently holds the default position.

And one last question.

> **Is the thing you're making something people would tell each other about
> when one of them is doing something for the first time?**

If not, you haven't found the door yet.
**Finding a door takes time, not money.**
