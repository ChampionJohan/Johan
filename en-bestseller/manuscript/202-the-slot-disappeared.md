## The drawer of cables

Every house has one drawer with nothing but cables in it.

Take them out and look. About half of them, you can't tell
what machine they went with.

Something is odd here.

**Those machines weren't thrown out because they broke.**

Most of them went into the drawer working,
and then one day got discarded.

Different from the last chapter.
Those things got boring because the talk ended.

**These never got boring.**
There was just nowhere left to use them.

## The obvious reading — something better came along

They lost a competition.

**Half right.**

Some did lose. Look at what vanished and the story doesn't fit.

There was a small, tough music format called the MiniDisc.¹
It sounded good and it didn't break easily.
It kept being improved for years.

Very cheap small laptops sold well for a stretch. They were called netbooks.²
They didn't disappear because something was wrong with them.
**At that price there was nothing comparable.**

Dedicated in-car navigation units too.
Being purpose-built, the screen was larger and the response quicker.

None of the three lost. **The slot they stood in disappeared.**

And there are three ways a slot disappears.
The three differ completely in how predictable they are.

## First way · Absorption — whatever is already in your pocket wins

Navigation units, music players, flashlights, voice recorders, calculators, cameras.

That whole list got pulled into one place. The phone.

Note what happened.

**The absorber didn't win by being better.**

The dedicated navigation unit had the bigger screen.
The dedicated camera took better pictures.
The dedicated recorder captured better sound.

They lost anyway. One reason.

> **Whatever is already in your pocket wins.**

When people choose, they don't only count performance.
They also count **how many objects they have to carry.**

When one does the job, carrying a second means that second thing
can't be merely "a bit better." **It has to be "can't do without."**

So the places where dedicated devices survive are fixed.
Where professionals work. Where lives depend on it. Where conditions are rough.

**In those places "a bit better" becomes "can't do without."**

This way has one piece of good news.

**Absorption is predictable.**

Look at what people always carry.
What that will swallow next is mostly guessable.
What can be swallowed is **whatever can become a component.**

## Second way · Skipping — what was built as a bridge

The MiniDisc sat somewhere slightly different.

It was better than what came before it.
And as an object it was better than what came after.

The problem is where it stood.

Between an era of **carrying music on a physical medium**
and an era of **not needing to carry it at all.**

A house built on a bridge.

While people are crossing, it's useful.
**Once they've crossed, nobody needs the bridge.**

Objects like this have a signature trait.

They solved the problem of their era extremely well.
Solving it well made them refined within that problem,
and they went when the problem went.

**Being well made ties you in deeper.**

The sentence from Chapter 2 comes back.

> **Grab the method and you go when the method goes.**

The MiniDisc was holding not "listening to music"
but "a way of carrying music."

And this way is **hard to predict.**

Whether the far bank will appear, and when, is difficult to know in advance.
Chapter 19 comes back to this.

## Third way · Pushing — what nobody asked for

The third is a very different animal.

Home television you watched wearing glasses, in three dimensions,
was pushed hard for several years.³
Manufacturers pushed together, it took the front of the shop floor,
broadcasts were prepared.

Then it quietly went away.

A personal vehicle you stood on and rode went a similar way.⁴
The claim that cities would be redesigned around it followed it everywhere.

For this one there's a confirmable number.

Around the time production stopped, that machine accounted for
**a little over one percent** of the company's total revenue.⁴

Everyone knew the name and the revenue wasn't there.
**Recognition and demand can diverge that far.**

These two differ decisively from the first two.

**They never had a slot before losing it.**

Absorption and skipping are slots that existed and then went.
Pushing is **a failed attempt to create a slot.**

And here is the most useful test in this chapter.

> **Did people ask for this,
> or did we make it and show it to them?**

If it's the second, there is no slot yet.

Making it and showing it isn't wrong.
Plenty of things nobody asked for have changed the world.

But then **the cost of creating the slot has to be in the budget.**
That cost is usually larger than the cost of making the thing.

The real danger of pushing is elsewhere.

**The units sold do not prove demand.**

Somebody came in to buy a big screen and the 3D function went out attached to it.
The number climbs while few people ever turn the function on.

And the reason the manufacturers gave when they dropped the function
was exactly that. **There was no demand.**³

It's the reason stated by the people who had been doing the pushing,
at the moment they stopped.

**When units sold and units used diverge, that number is not a signal.**

And this way is **the easiest of the three to predict.** You can know at the start.
That nobody asked for it is a fact available on day one.

## What didn't disappear under the same absorption

A counter-example has to go here.
Without it this chapter becomes a chapter about giving up.

The phone swallowed the watch too.
For the purpose of telling the time, a wristwatch has nothing left to do.

**Wristwatches did not disappear.**

The reason is the core of this whole book.

A wristwatch **stopped being a tool for telling the time.**
It became something you wear, and something you hand down.

It stepped off the ground where it competed on function
and sat down somewhere else.

That's **changing clocks**, from Chapter 2.
And it used two of the four bridges. **Shape** and **Handoff.**

> **What gets absorbed is a function, not an object.
> Only an object that is nothing but its function goes when the function goes.**

Invert that sentence and the work appears.

**Count what your object has besides its function.**
If the count is zero, building a better function is not the answer.

All of Part Three is how to do that counting.

## The short version

| Way | What happened | Predictable? | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Absorption** | What people always carry ate the function | **Easily** | Build value outside the function |
| **Skipping** | People stopped passing through that stage | Hard | Hold the need, not the method |
| **Pushing** | There was never a slot | **Easiest of all** | Ask first whether anyone asked |

The three share one thing.

**The product was fine to the end.**

So none of the three can be stopped with quality.
**Raising quality is especially dangerous in the face of absorption.**
The better it's made, the deeper it's tied to that function.

## One Line Today

Pick one machine you use that **does exactly one job.**
A dedicated device of any kind.

Write down two things.

**First,** is something already in your pocket capable of that job?

**Second,** if so, why do you keep using this machine?

If the only answer to the second is **"because it does it better,"**
that machine is headed for the drawer.

If the answer contains something other than function — it fits your hand,
it was handed down, you like the time you spend using it —
that machine has already moved.

> **What does the thing you make have besides its function?**
