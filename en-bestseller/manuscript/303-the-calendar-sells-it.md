## Forgotten for eleven months, then it returns

Every December the same songs come back.
In shops, on the street, on the radio.

They aren't new songs. They came out a long time ago.¹

Something strange here.

Songs usually get heard less as time passes.
New ones arrive, people move on, and they quietly go.

**These songs get heard more as time passes.**

And the stranger part is what happens in the other eleven months.

Nobody listens to them for most of the year.
They are completely forgotten.

**Forgotten, and not dead.**

## The obvious reading — seasonal goods are at a disadvantage

**Half right.**

The selling window is short. True.
There's something bought with that.

> **The calendar does the advertising.**

An ordinary product has to keep spending so people don't forget it.
Stop, and you're forgotten; forgotten, and it's over.

A seasonal product doesn't need that.
**When the time comes, people remember on their own.**

December arrives and carols come to mind; the cold arrives and heavy clothes do.
Nobody has to tell them.

This is the same class of asset as shape in the last chapter.
**An asset maintained without a monthly payment.**

## Repeat has three sources

Repeat is the first bridge, named in Chapter 2.
It gets opened up here.

There are only three ways to make someone buy again.

**One. Consumption — it runs out, so you buy more.**

Food, detergent, fuel, supplies.
The most common and the most reliable.

**Two. Cycle — the time comes, so you buy again.**

Seasons, holidays, the start of school, birthdays, scheduled maintenance.

**Three. Stage — once at each stage of a life.**

The doors from Chapter 3 belong here.

The second is the strongest of the three, for one reason.

> **Nothing has to be remembered.**

Consumption requires noticing that you've run out.
Not noticing means not buying. So you need a device that says it's empty.

A stage can't be scheduled. It depends on that person's circumstances.

**A cycle is announced by the calendar.**
Both the buyer and the seller know in advance.

## Four things seasonal demand gives you

Four advantages, put together.

**One, the time to promote is fixed.**
You don't have to spend all year. A few weeks will do.

**Two, being forgotten is survivable.**

This one is decisive.

For an ordinary product, being forgotten is the end.
**A seasonal product can afford it.** Being forgotten is partly what makes
December feel welcome.

**Three, you can plan.**
You roughly know when and how much will sell. Stock and staffing can be set in advance.

**Four, new people arrive every year.**
The renewing structure from Chapter 3 attaches by itself.
Every year there are people going through it for the first time.

Put the four together and nearly all the conditions of a steady seller are met.
**Which is why so many of the things that lasted are seasonal.**

## But there are three costs

Seasonal demand is not free.

**One. Miss a year and you wait a year.**

If preparation was late, or stock ran short, or there was a quality incident,
that year is gone entirely.

An ordinary product can make it back next month.
**A seasonal product has no next month.**

So in this business **a mistake costs twelve times as much.**

**Two. No cash comes in for eleven months.**

This is what actually kills companies.

The classic case of a successful product inside a failing company is here.
Materials have to be bought early, people have to stay,
rent leaves every month.

**Without the cash to sit through the season, don't run a seasonal product.**

That's a financing problem, not a product problem.
And the financing structure is rarely examined when the product is chosen.

**Three. The season can move.**

The least visible risk.

A season looks like it's on the need clock. Winter arrives every year.
**But what people do in that season sits on a different clock.**

What gets exchanged at a holiday, what gets prepared for the start of school,
where people go in winter.

These are on the generation clock. They change over ten or twenty years.

> **The season is fixed. What people do in that season is not.**

So what a seasonal business checks each year is not the weather.
**It's whether what people do on that day is still the same.**

## Can you make a season?

Some seasons were made.

An example belonged here. Certain customs of exchanging things on a particular
date are widely said to have been created within living memory.

**The example was cut because its origin could not be confirmed against a
primary source.** Accounts differ on which company did what, and when.

Only the conditions, then. Three of them.

**One, there has to be a date.**
The date is the memory device. No date, no custom.

**Two, there has to be someone to exchange with.**
A thing you do alone rarely becomes a custom.
**There has to be a handing-over motion for it to spread to the next person.**

**Three, a first-timer has to know exactly what to do.**
Complicated rules don't spread.

But something has to be said plainly here.

**Whoever makes a season does not own it.**

The moment it becomes a custom, everyone sells on that day.
The company that made it sells, the followers sell, the corner shop sells.

> **Making a season means making a market, not taking one.**

So anyone trying to make a season has to take one more thing with it.
**The default position** from Chapter 3.

The place that comes to mind when people ask "what do I get?" on that day.
**The season gets shared. The default is one.**

## Attaching a cycle to something with no season

Most objects have no season.
Four things can still be done, in order of cheapness.

**One. Attach to a date that already exists.**

Far cheaper than making a season.
Find a day when people already do something and make a place there.

**Two. Design the consumption rate.**

Decide how long it takes to use up, and build to that.
Too long and you're forgotten; too fast and it's a burden.

**Three. Let the object announce its own replacement time.**

A colour changes, a mark appears, the wear becomes visible.
**The point is not leaving it to human memory.**

**Four. Make something that runs in yearly units.**

Something with the year on it, something only made that year,
something filled in over time.
**Anything collected acquires a cycle on its own.**

All four cost design rather than money.
And all four only work **if you don't change them after building them.**

## One Line Today

Open a calendar. Paper is better, a screen is fine.

**Mark every day in the year on which you always buy something.**

Holidays, birthdays, the start of a school year, the turn of a season,
scheduled maintenance, anniversaries.
More of them appear than you'd expect.

When they're marked, look at two things.

**First,** write down what you buy on each day.
If you buy the same thing every year, that is your steady seller.

**Second,** are there months with no marks?

> **An empty month is a seat nobody has taken yet.**

Then one last question.

> **Which day on the calendar is the thing you make attached to?**

If it's attached to no day, it means you have to pay, every single time,
**to manufacture the reason for anyone to think of it.**
