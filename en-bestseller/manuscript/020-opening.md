## Five hundred in two years

There was a dessert franchise.

It sold fruit coated in hardened sugar, on a skewer.
At some point it spread everywhere. Every street had one. People queued.

Two years after the franchise began, the company announced
its **five hundredth store**.¹

**Seven months later there were four hundred and ninety.**¹

Not more. Fewer.

In one three-month stretch that following spring and early summer,
**a hundred and twenty-seven** shops in that category closed across the country.²

The closing rate climbed every month.
Eleven in one month, twenty-eight two months later, forty-nine the month after.²

**It took seven months from the party for the five hundredth store.**

## One thing for a thousand years

There is another shop.

It sits on the approach road to a shrine in Kyoto, Japan,
and it sells one thing: grilled rice cakes.³

It has been in that spot since around the year 1000.³

The person running it now is the **twenty-fifth generation**.³

The most striking thing about this shop is not what it sells.
**It's what it doesn't do.**

No counter in a department store. No stalls at events.
No added items. No branches.

The family's explanation is simple.
**The policy is not to make the business bigger.**³

And why the shop exists at all contains everything this book is about.

**There is a shrine in front of it, and people come to pray there.**³

Worshippers come every year. And every year they are different people.
That flow has not broken in a thousand years.

## Put the two numbers side by side

**Five hundred, and a thousand.**

The first is large. The second is slow.
The first took two years to make. The second took a thousand.

Here is the question people usually ask.

> Which one was better at business?

**That this question is wrong is where this book starts.**

Put the two on one line and no answer comes out.
One earned a lot and one earned for a long time,
and those are not values you can compare.

> **They are not better and worse at the same work.
> They were doing different work.**

One sold **what people were talking about.**
The other sold **what people keep needing.**

Talk ends. Need doesn't.

So one earned a lot and finished,
and the other earns a little and hasn't finished.

## What this book does

Two things.

**One, it separates the two.**

It pulls apart two words people use interchangeably.
Once they're apart, you can see which one you're building.

The same object, handled as one or the other, produces opposite decisions.
When to add locations and when adding locations kills you.
When you have to keep advertising and when you don't have to at all.

What that franchise got wrong was not adding stores.
**It was adding stores without knowing which one it was.**

**Two, it builds a bridge from one side to the other.**

This is the real content of the book.

Trace back the things that sell steadily now and something odd appears.

**Most of them were not built to last.**

They were once a huge topic of conversation. Everybody talked about them.
And everything else that was talked about at the same time is gone.

**They changed lanes at some point.**

Where that change happens is what this book is trying to teach.

## This book does not take a side

One thing, stated before we start.

**It does not say that lasting is right and flaring is wrong.**

Breaking out big gives you things no other method gives.
It's fast, people learn you exist,
and sometimes it is the entrance to the lasting kind.

Breaking out is not the problem.

> **Preparing only to break out,
> and not deciding where to go after — that's the problem.**

What those queued-up shops got wrong was not making people queue.
**It was changing nothing while the queue was there.**

## Who this is for

It was written for people who make something and sell it.
A shop, an object, a piece of writing, anything being started.

But it isn't only for them.

**Anyone curious about why the things they own are still in their hands**
can read it.

This book measures books and clothes and appliances and food
and film and music with the same ruler.
A ruler where thermal underwear and a book on raising a child
land in the same column.

Once that ruler is in your hand you'll use it walking into a bookshop,
opening a closet, standing in a kitchen.

## The conclusion, stated early

This book has one conclusion.

Holding it back for the last chapter would be more fun.
**It's more useful to read knowing it.**

> **Most things that lasted
> were not planned. They just never disappeared.**

That thousand-year-old shop did not start out planning a thousand years.
It grilled rice cakes for people walking to a shrine.

And it never disappeared.

If that sentence is true, then anyone reading this book
who has not broken out yet gains one advantage.

**Never disappearing takes time rather than talent.**
And time can start being spent today.

That's what the last chapter is about.
