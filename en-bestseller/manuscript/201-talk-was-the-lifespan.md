## "Barely used it"

Search for a certain kind of object on a resale app
and sometimes a strange screen comes up.

Hundreds of the same thing, listed at once.
Even the camera angles are similar.

The descriptions are nearly identical.

> Barely used it. Just taking up space, so I'm clearing it out.

The sellers don't know each other.
They bought the same object at the same time and are selling it at the same time.

**This object was not discarded because it broke.**

Not one line complains about quality. Several insist it works perfectly.
It works perfectly and nobody uses it.

Odd. Not long ago you couldn't get one.
What got cut?

## The obvious reading — fad products vanish because they're badly made

Cheap to make, sell fast, get out.

**Not even half right.**

Plenty of fad products are well made.
The dessert that made people queue was not bad.
The machine everyone bought was not unusable.

And this reading has a larger problem.

If bad quality were the cause, **the answer is simple.** Make it well.
If that answer were right, Part Two would be three pages.

The well-made ones vanished too.
**So that isn't the reason.**

Three cases, sorted by what got cut first.

## Eaten talk — when the queue was the product

For a while the same signboard appeared on every street, selling a dessert.
Taiwanese-style castella did it, and after that, sugar-coated fruit skewers
sold under the Korean name *tanghulu*.¹

**The franchise from the opening — five hundred stores in two years — is the second one.**

Both were selling the **talk** from the previous chapter.

Something needs care here.
What people bought was not only the food.

**The queue was part of the product.**

Having queued. Being able to photograph having queued.
That was in the package.

Which gives this business an unusual property.

> **The moment supply increases, half the product disappears.**

When you can buy it anywhere the queue is gone,
and when the queue is gone there's no reason to take the picture.

This kind of food has one more trait in common.

**It isn't hard to make.**

Buy a machine, or learn it in a few days.
There's no way to block it. Put a trademark on it and a similar name opens next door.

So supply grows much faster than demand.
**And you learn you've passed the line only after you've passed it.**

## Owned talk — when it's worthless once everyone has one

For one stretch, a palm-sized electronic device spread widely among children.
A toy for keeping a digital pet.²

This one isn't food. It doesn't get eaten.
It ended faster.

The reason is in the previous chapter.

**An object on the fashion clock is no fun to own alone.**

Everyone in the class has to have one for it to be a conversation.
When the conversation ends, what's in the hand loses its value too.

**The object is unchanged and its value sits in other people's hands.**

This produces a strange timetable.

The moment everyone has one is **the peak and the terminus at once.**
The best-selling month is the most dangerous month.

## Heard talk — when repetition is consumption

There's a song that was everywhere that summer.
By the next year you barely hear it.

Here the structure of this chapter shows most clearly.

**Listening is using it up.**

Clothes don't shrink from being worn; machines remain after being used.
Talk decreases when consumed.

To someone hearing the song for the twentieth time, it isn't the same song.
The song didn't change. **The remaining newness ran out.**

The same thing was happening in the other two cases.
The queue and the toy were also shrinking as they were used.

## The autopsy — what got cut first

Put the three in one table.

| | What was sold | What got cut first | The product was |
|---|---|---|---|
| The queued dessert | Food + **the queue** | Supply rose and the queue went | Fine |
| The palm-sized toy | A toy + **the classmates** | The classmates moved on | Working |
| That summer's song | A song + **newness** | Repetition wore the newness out | Unchanged |

Read the middle column downward and the answer to this chapter appears.

> **Talk decreases when used. And using it is the same act as selling it.**

That's the structural problem of a fad product.
The more you sell, the less there is to sell.

It's the opposite of the manufacturing rule.
Usually selling more lowers unit cost and hardens your position.
**Here selling more shortens the remaining lifespan.**

## The real risk is somewhere else

Everything so far is known. Nobody thinks a fad lasts forever.

**The real risk isn't that it ends. It's when it ends.**

A fad curve is not symmetrical.

Going up takes months. People find out one at a time.

Coming down doesn't work that way.
**It becomes uninteresting all at once.**

Why everyone loses interest simultaneously is simple.
The signal to lose interest is also talk.
"That's over now" spreads exactly as fast.

So in this business **the last one in loses everything.**

Someone reads that it's selling best, and by the time they've built out
and opened the doors a few months later, the market in front of them is dark.

> **Losses in a fad business don't come from chasing the fad.
> They come from confirming the fad and then chasing it.**

## So should you not make fad products?

You should. Chapter 1 said a bestseller gives you things nothing else does.

The problem isn't making them. It's **how they're accounted for.**

Treat a fad product as a fad product and it's fine.
Most people treat a fad product **like a need product.**

The difference is in fixed cost.

| | Handled as a fad | Handled like a need product (risk) |
|---|---|---|
| Space | Short lease · pop-up | Long contract |
| Production | Outsourced | Buy the equipment |
| People | Added when needed | Hired permanently and trained |
| Stock | Deliberately short | Comfortably long |
| Payback | **All of it within months** | Over several years |

The last row is the point.

**On a fad product, the payback period has to be shorter than the fad.**

Write that calculation down at the start and most of the disasters are avoided.
And there's only one reason people don't.
**When it's going well, nobody wants to think about the ending.**

## And one more — where the money goes

Money made on a fad has two places to go.

**One is making the same fad bigger.** More stores, more production.
Chapter 7 covers the result.

**The other is buying a bridge.**

Spend it on one of the four named in Chapter 2 — Repeat, Shape, Patina, Handoff.
They can only be bought while the talk is alive.

Most of the things in Part Three chose the second.

> **Spend fad money on fad and it ends.
> Spend fad money on a position and it stays.**

## The short version

| Question | This chapter's answer |
|---|---|
| What was being sold | The object + **the talk** |
| Why did it end | Talk decreases when used, and using is selling |
| Was the product at fault | No. So making it better stops nothing |
| When is it most dangerous | **The best-selling month** |
| Who loses most | The last one in, who confirmed the fad first |
| What to do | Payback shorter than the fad. Buy a bridge with the proceeds |

## One Line Today

Open a resale app.
**Search the name of one object in your home.**

See how many are listed. Then read three of the descriptions.

- Mostly "it broke" or "I upgraded" — that object still holds its place
- Mostly **"barely used it"** — that object was talk

Then one last question.

> **When people resell what you're making now, what will they write?**

If "I stopped using it" comes to mind, you haven't found the place yet.
**Finding it can be done late. It's cheaper while the talk is still alive.**
