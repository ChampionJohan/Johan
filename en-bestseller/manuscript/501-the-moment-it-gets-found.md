## The owner doesn't know why

One day a queue forms outside an old shop.

The shop changed nothing.
Same menu, same sign, same owner.

**And the owner doesn't know what happened.**

The same line always follows this scene.

> We've been doing what we always do.

Odd.

In Part Four the world took ten years to arrive.
Here it arrives in a few days.

Same object, same place. Why does it take ten years sometimes and one night others?

## The obvious reading — a sudden break-out is luck

**Half right.**

It is luck. It isn't something you can plan.

But there's an optical illusion hidden in the word.

**It didn't break out suddenly.**

That object had been in that spot for ten years, twenty.
**What appeared suddenly wasn't the object. It was the attention.**

So put precisely:

> **A discovery isn't an event. It's a connection.**

Somebody moved it into a place where other people look.
That moving happens in an instant, so it looks sudden.

**The object didn't move. The gaze moved.**

## Four carriers of discovery

Chapter 11 sorted the carriers of late arrival into three.
Here they split differently. These are the fast ones.

**One. It appeared on a screen.**

An object gets used in a drama or a film and people who watched go looking.
A song used in one scene returning to the charts is the same structure.¹

What matters is that **it wasn't advertising.**

An object that appeared in an advertisement gets processed as advertising.
An object that was inside a story gets processed as part of that story.

**Two. A different generation saw it as new.**

This one happens most often and is understood least.

To someone seeing it for the first time, an old thing is a new thing.

Cameras that use film, music on a disc, tools used by hand.
To someone who grew up with them they're old objects;
to someone meeting them for the first time they're **something never tried.**

> **What separates old from new isn't in the object. It's in the age of the person looking.**

The generation clock from Chapter 2 comes full circle here.
When a generation turns, its things go down.
One more generation and those things are new again.

**Three. One person carried it.**

Someone in a place many people watch is seen using it.

This carrier is faster than the other two, and shallower.
When that person's attention moves, it often moves with them.

**Four. Circumstances made it necessary again.**

When prices rise, when supply breaks, when people start wanting something else,
an older way becomes the answer.

Repairing rather than replacing, using something for a long time, making it yourself.
When conditions get harder, the value of these rises.

Tie the four together in one line.

**None of the four was done by the maker.**

Chapter 8 said the user moves the object.
Chapter 12 said the person who finds the use is not the person who made it.

**That's the third time.** Not a coincidence — it's the spine of this book.

But here Part Five's own difference appears.

> **The maker didn't do it, and it doesn't happen without the maker.**

## A wave is not only good news

Here the real content of this chapter starts.

What happens when a wave arrives is rarely discussed.
Everyone aims at that moment, so nobody looks past it.

**A wave breaks whatever wasn't ready.**
Four things arrive at once.

**One. Orders exceed what you can make.**

In Chapter 7 things died by growing. This is the opposite direction.
Ten times the volume arrives and the equipment and the people are unchanged.

**Two. A long stock-out moves the attention on.**

A wave doesn't wait.
Someone who couldn't buy rarely waits until next month.

**Three. Scaling up in a hurry shakes the quality.**

And this is the part that hurts.

**The value of that object was its quality.**

The reason it lasted was consistency,
and consistency breaks while you're scaling to catch the wave.

**Four. New customers collide with old ones.**

The quietest and the deepest loss.

The people who always came can't get in. No table, no stock, a long queue.
And they don't complain. **They just stop coming.**

A few months after the wave, the new customers are all gone.
And the old customers who should still be there left some time ago.

> **This is how the place after a wave ends up worse than before it.**

## Three rules for when a wave arrives

Three things have to be decided in advance.
Deciding after the wave arrives is too late.

**One. Take care of the old customers first.**

The wave passes; the old customers are what remains.

Concretely, it means setting aside their share.
Holding reservations, holding quantity, keeping a separate channel open.

**Turning away a new customer feels wasteful.**
Bearing that feeling is the whole of this rule.

**Two. Scale up, but reversibly.**

Chapter 7's arithmetic applies exactly.

Whether this wave is need demand or talk demand can't be known now.
And it's usually talk demand.

So **scale up the reversible things first.**
More people, longer hours, outside help.
Buying equipment and adding space comes last.

**Three. Buy a bridge with what the wave pays.**

The third time this prescription appears in this book.

Chapter 5 said to buy a position with fad money.
Chapter 7 said that when it's going well, add bridges rather than locations.

Here it comes again.
**Three appearances means this is the only thing this book actually says.**

## Nobody knows why it broke out

To close this chapter honestly.

After it breaks out, explanations are everywhere.

**They are almost all attached afterwards.**

Several places had the same conditions at the same time and one broke out.
**Then those conditions are not the cause.**

So copying a case that broke out is dangerous.
**What you'd be copying is a condition, not a cause.**

What this book can say ends here.

**What happened can be recorded.**
**That it was the cause cannot.**

And because of that limit, the next chapter is necessary.

If the cause is unknown, the question has to change.

> **Not how to make it break out,
> but what it takes to be standing there when it does.**

## One Line Today

Think of an object or a shop you've been seeing a lot of lately.
Ideally one that seems new, that younger people talk about.

Then **find out when it was made.**

Most people are surprised. It's usually old.

Then write two things.

**First,** where had it been all that time?

**Second,** what moved it in front of people?
A screen, a different generation, one person, or circumstances?

Then one last question.

> **Why hadn't it disappeared before then?**

The answer to that question is the next chapter.
**Nobody knows how to make a wave.
How to be standing there when one arrives can be known.**
