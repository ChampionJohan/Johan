## Inside one house, the objects are different ages

The iron pan in the kitchen might be twenty years old.
The phone in your pocket is about two.
The yogurt in the fridge is three days old and will be gone in three more.

All three were paid for.
Their lifespans differ by a factor of a thousand.

It isn't price. The phone cost more than the pan.
It isn't durability either. The phone usually gets replaced before it breaks.

**Different clocks are running on different objects.**

This chapter counts how many clocks there are. There are three.

## The obvious reading — make it well and it lasts

Quality decides lifespan.

**Half right.**

What quality decides is **how long the object holds up.**
What decides lifespan is **how long people keep wanting it.**

Every house has something that works perfectly and never gets used.
It wasn't thrown out because it broke. It was put away because the wanting stopped.

So the question changes.

> **Not "how long will this hold up"
> but "how long will the wanting hold up."**

That wanting has three cycles.

## One · The fashion clock — months

This clock runs on **what other people are doing right now.**

So the cycle is short.
The moment everyone has it, it stops being worth having.

Things on the fashion clock share one trait.

**They're no fun to own alone.**

The value isn't inside the object. It's in your relation to everyone else.
When everyone else loses interest, what's in your hand loses value too.

Objects on this clock make money fast.
And nobody knows when they'll stop.

**Not knowing when it stops is the single decisive risk of this clock.**

## Two · The generation clock — ten to thirty years

Some things get into the body early.
A familiar sound, a familiar taste, a familiar cut of clothing.

These don't turn over in months like fashion.
They also aren't permanent.

**They age as the people who learned them age.**

The dangerous part is elsewhere.

> **Anyone standing on this clock doesn't believe they're standing on fashion.**

If you think it's fashion you prepare for it.
If you think it's taste you don't.

It hasn't changed in over ten years, so it looks like it never will.
That judgment is usually proved wrong in year fifteen.

The generation clock is **the easiest one to be fooled by.**

## Three · The need clock — it doesn't move

Cold. Hungry. Sleepy. Hurting.
Bored. Lonely. Wanting to be seen. Wanting to pass something on.

This list was the same a hundred years ago.
It probably won't differ much in another hundred.

**This clock doesn't turn.**

One qualification matters more than anything else on this page.

> **The need doesn't change. The method of meeting it does.**

Keeping out the cold doesn't change. What you keep it out with keeps changing.
Getting word to someone far away doesn't change.
What you send it with has changed several times.

So sitting on the need clock requires care.

**Grab the method and you go when the method goes.**
**Grab the need and you stay as long as the need stays.**

That sentence comes back more often than any other in this book.
It explains the vanished things in Part Two and the revived ones in Part Four.

## Inside one category, all three clocks are present

Here a common confusion clears up.

The category doesn't set the clock.
Clothing isn't fashion because it's clothing; food isn't need because it's food.

**All three clocks exist inside every category.**

| Category | Fashion clock | Generation clock | Need clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clothing** | This season's silhouette | What a generation wore like a uniform | Thermal layers · socks |
| **Food** | The dessert with the queue | The taste learned as a child | Staples · water · salt |
| **Appliances** | A newly introduced format | The kitchen a generation learned | Fire · water · cold · light |
| **Media** | The song of that summer | A generation's music and actors | Lullabies · holiday songs · old stories |
| **Books** | This year's talked-about title | A generation's standard works | Child-raising · dictionaries · scripture · cookbooks |

**Read the table across, not down.**

Clothing and books are entirely different categories.
But things in the same column behave the same way.

Thermal underwear and a book on raising a child sit on the same clock.
Neither is ever advertised much. Neither has ever been a topic of conversation.
Both sell every year.

**The clock decides the fate of an object, not the category.**

## What happens when you misread the clock

Two mistakes, pointing in opposite directions.

**One. Making need-clock investments on a fashion-clock object.**

Adding equipment, opening locations, buying things meant to last.
When the talk ends the revenue is gone and the equipment isn't.

That mistake is Chapter 7.
**It is the most common way to fail, and the most energetic.**

**Two. Selling a need-clock object like fashion.**

Pushing hard, changing it often, making limited editions.

The value of a need-clock object comes from **the fact that it doesn't change.**
That what you bought last year and this year are the same thing
is the asset.

Keep changing it and you're spending your own asset.

The first mistake loses money.
**The second loses time.** And time can't be bought back.

## Clocks can be changed

That's the diagnosis. A diagnosis alone doesn't produce work.

Fortunately clocks aren't fixed.

**Some things that started on the fashion clock moved to the need clock.**

There are only four bridges across.
Their names go here; how to use them is Part Three and Part Six.

| Bridge | The question |
|---|---|
| **Repeat** | When it's used up, do you buy it again? |
| **Shape** | Do people recognize it without being told? |
| **Patina** | Does time add value or take it away? |
| **Handoff** | Does it pass to the next person? |

**Anything holding two or more of the four tends to last.**
One is a season. None is a quarter.

## The short version

| | Fashion clock | Generation clock | Need clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cycle** | Months | Ten to thirty years | It doesn't turn |
| **Runs on** | What others are doing | The years you grew up in | The body and life events |
| **Value sits in** | Your relation to others | Memory | The trouble of that moment |
| **Biggest risk** | You don't know when it stops | **You don't know you're on fashion** | Mistaking method for need |
| **Stop promoting** | It goes out at once | It fades slowly | Very little changes |

> **The three clocks aren't good and bad.
> Deciding without knowing which one you're on is what's bad.**

## One Line Today

Walk through the house and pick five objects.
Include the oldest one and the newest one.

Beside each, write when you got it and which clock it's on.

Then one last question.

> **Will the newest one still be in this house in ten years?**

If you answered yes, write one line on why you think so.
**If what you wrote is one of the four bridges, the judgment is probably right.**
