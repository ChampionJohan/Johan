## Everyone is looking at the same screen

Chapter 7 had three of the same signboard on one street.
The three people who opened them didn't know each other.

**They were all looking at the same screen.**

There are screens that tell you what's doing well.
Rankings, search terms, summary articles.

All three looked. All three concluded the same thing.

Something is odd here.
**How can a decision made from information anyone can see be better than anyone else's?**

## Three to ignore

**One. Rankings.**

A ranking shows what sold last month.
**It's an instrument for looking backwards, not forwards.**

And there's a bigger problem. Everybody looks at the same ranking.
A judgment made from it is the same judgment everyone else made.

**Two. Anything that already has a name.**

Something having a name means many people already recognized it.
It also means it's been discussed enough times to be worth naming.

**The moment a name appears is already late.**

**Three. What someone else already succeeded at.**

As Chapter 14 showed, looking at a success shows you conditions, not causes.
There's no record of the other places that met the same conditions.

The three have one thing in common.

> **All three are things that already happened.**

## Three to watch

**One. What people are patching by hand.**

The most useful tool in this chapter.

People don't endure inconvenience. **They solve it badly.**

Tape stuck over something. Two things used together.
A step done in a strange order. Somebody's object used for something else.

**Where there's a patch, there is a gap.**

And this signal has a large advantage.
**Unlike a ranking, it is happening now.**

**Two. What suddenly got cheap.**

The first carrier from Chapter 11.

Hold a list of what has to fall and by how much for something to work,
and you'll recognize it. Without the list, the price falls and you don't notice.

**Three. Where a rule changed.**

A permission is granted, something is prohibited, a standard changes,
and a slot appears or disappears.

Rule changes have an unusual property.
**They're announced in advance.** This is reading, not forecasting.

## What reading means

Put together:

Reading the present is not guessing what will rise.

> **It's seeing what is blocked right now.**

The difference matters.

Guessing what will rise means **being faster than others.**
The fast side is the side with money and information.

Seeing what's blocked doesn't require speed.
**It requires looking closely.** And looking closely costs nothing.

Chapters 19 and 20 come out of that sentence.

## The checklist

```
1. For one week, write down what people say they're annoyed by — ten of them
   Count your own. Count what you overheard

2. Beside each, write how they're currently coping

3. Mark the ones with a hand-made patch
   → that's a gap

4. Among those, mark the ones with no name yet
   → that's a gap you're not late for

5. For what's left, ask two things
   What would have to get cheaper for this to be solved?
   What rule would have to change for this to be solved?
```

None of the five costs money.
**They cost a week and one sheet of paper.**

## The short version

| | Ignore | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| One | Rankings | **What people are patching by hand** |
| Two | Anything already named | What suddenly got cheap |
| Three | Someone else's success | Where a rule changed |
| Timing | Already happened | **Happening now** |
| What it takes | Fast information | **Close observation** |

> **A ranking is the past. An annoyance is the present.**

## One Line Today

For one day, count one thing.

**Scenes of people patching an inconvenience by hand.**

At home, on the street, at work.
Tape, rubber bands, sticky notes, doing something twice, a strange order.

Three is enough.

Then write one line for each.

> **Why has nobody made this properly yet?**

The answer is usually one of three.
**Too small, no money in it, or nobody looked.**

If it's the third, the seat is empty.
