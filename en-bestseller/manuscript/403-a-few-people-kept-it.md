## When sales and influence come apart

In the back rack of an old record shop there's a record that doesn't sell.

It didn't sell the year it came out. It doesn't sell much now.

**And almost everyone who makes music knows it.**

This isn't only a music phenomenon.
There are books few people read, films few people saw, tools few people used,
that sit in the same position.

By sales figures they might as well not exist,
and things made out of them keep appearing.

Odd. **Why do sales and influence come apart?**

## The risk in this chapter, stated first

This chapter has to be written more carefully than the others.

Stories attach themselves to objects like these.
Stories along the lines of "everyone who bought one went on to make something."

Those stories are well made, widely spread, and **have no source.**

Often a remark somebody made in passing got repeated until it hardened into fact.
Go looking and the person who first said it never had a basis either.

> **Widely known does not mean true.**

So in this chapter, unverifiable anecdotes were **cut whole rather than reworded.**
A hedged sentence gets read as a fact.

**Which makes the chapter flatter.**
That was judged the better price to pay.

What follows is structure without stories.

## The obvious reading — good things get recognized eventually

**Not even half right.**

The number of things that went unrecognized and disappeared is overwhelming.
And no list of them exists.

The unnamed box from Chapter 4 opens widest here.

What we call "examples of things eventually recognized"
are **a list assembled only from the ones that were recognized.**

So what to look for here isn't comfort.
**It's the difference between selling little and staying, and selling little and vanishing.**

## Width and density

Put two situations side by side.

**A million people who liked it once.**
**A thousand people who liked it for life.**

Sales are a thousand times larger on the first.
So is revenue, so is the talk, so is the ranking.

And twenty years later the one still standing is sometimes the second.

One reason.

> **Width makes revenue. Density makes lifespan.**

Someone who liked it shallowly doesn't pass it on.
There isn't enough reason inside them to.

Someone who liked it deeply does.
Not once, but repeatedly, over years.

## Three things density does

Broken out.

**One. It transmits.**

Chapter 3 said a default is built not by advertising
but by what people who went through before said.
The ones who speak are the ones who liked it deeply.

**A thousand deep beat a million shallow on volume of speech.**

**Two. It preserves.**

More important.

Someone who liked it deeply doesn't throw it away.
They keep the original, repair it when it breaks, hunt for it when it's gone.

Chapter 11 said the late discoveries had **the original still existing** in common.
**The few are the ones who kept it.**

So density is a precondition for rediscovery.
**Something nobody liked deeply isn't even still around.**

**Three. It makes.**

Some of the people who liked it deeply use it as material for something new.

Then the original keeps getting cited.
Every time something new appears, the original's name gets said once more.

> **What gets cited doesn't die.**

That's the structure behind sales and influence coming apart.
A sale happens once. A citation keeps happening.

## Three traps in density

Read critically here.
Read as romance, this chapter becomes a harmful one.

**One. Density can't be manufactured.**

There's no method for making people like something deeply.
If there were, everyone would be using it.

**It can, however, be prevented.**

Make it too easy and density tends not to form.
Attachment doesn't stick to things that took no effort.

Chapter 8 said patina converts early discomfort into later attachment.
The same structure shows here.

**We don't know how to create density. We know how to destroy it.**
Make everything comfortable, change it often, let anyone in.

**Two. A few and zero look the same.**

Especially from the inside.

Chapter 11's test applies again. Are they saying it, or using it?
One more indicator can be added.

**Do they come back?**

If the number is small but the same people return, that's density.
If only new people come and never return, that isn't density. That's curiosity.

This number is easy to count and costs nothing to count.

**Three. Density usually doesn't feed you.**

The passage in this chapter that has to be most honest.

Among examples in this territory, the maker was often poor.
By the time the influence had grown, that person was gone or doing something else.

**Density gives lifespan. It doesn't give a living.**

So "aim for density" cannot be this chapter's lesson.

## Then what is being asked?

Three things.

**One. Count the density.**

Knowing whether it's there comes first.
Returns, repeat purchases, people who contact you directly. All easy to count.

**Two. Don't break the line to those few.**

The most practical sentence in this chapter.

When a rediscovery happens, those few are the bridge.
If the line is broken, the world can arrive and there's no way across.

Keep the list, send them something, make a place to meet.
**The smaller the number the easier this is, and if it isn't done while it's small
it can't be done later.**

**Three. Keep the order.**

The conclusion.

> **Width can be bought later. Density cannot.**

When money appears, advertising buys width. That's what advertising is for.
Nobody can buy people who like something deeply.

So the order is fixed.
**Density first, width second.**

Go in the reverse order and when the width goes out nothing remains.
Most of what's in Part Two was in the reverse order.

## Sources for this chapter

This chapter names no specific case.

Several widely told examples exist for this pattern,
and the central claim of those stories could not be confirmed against a primary source.
Unconfirmed anecdotes were removed entirely rather than softened.

For a name to go into the text, the following would have to be confirmed:
actual sales at release, when the influence grew,
and a primary basis for the event said to connect the two.

## One Line Today

Think of one thing you like **deeply** that most people don't know.
A book, music, a shop, a tool. Anything.

Count two things.

**First,** how many times have you told someone about it?

**Second,** did anyone actually try it because you told them?

The first number is usually larger than expected.
**People talk about what they like deeply. Nobody has to ask them to.**

Then ask the same question about your own work.

> **Not counting me, how many people have spoken about what I made
> more than once?**

If none, there's no density yet.
**If there's even one, save that person's contact details now.**

When the world eventually arrives, that person is the bridge.
