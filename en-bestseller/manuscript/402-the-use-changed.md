## The adhesive that doesn't stick well

Someone developing an adhesive produced one that doesn't stick well.¹

It holds, but weakly. Pull and it comes off.
As an adhesive, a failure.

A few years later that substance became something in every office.
A note you stick on and peel off.¹

Something needs checking.

**The substance was not fixed.**

It wasn't made to stick better. It was used exactly as it was.

Odd. The cause of the failure became the core of the product unchanged.

## The obvious reading — it was luck

Read as a story about good fortune.

**Half right.**

It was made by accident. True.
But accidents happen everywhere.

Getting something other than what you expected is common in a laboratory.
Most of it is discarded that day.

So the question isn't whether the accident happened.

> **Was there anywhere for the accident to land?**

If that failure had come out somewhere else,
it would have left one line in a record and ended.

Look at the things that survived by changing use and there are three routes.

## First route · Where the flaw is the requirement

The adhesive belongs here.

**Weak adhesion** is a flaw in the adhesive market.
In a place where things have to be stuck and unstuck, it's the specification.

The order of thinking can be inverted here.

Usually it goes:
**find the flaw → fix it.**

There's another order:
**find the flaw → find the place that needs that property.**

Using the second order changes the definition of a flaw.

> **A flaw is not a property. It's a mismatch between a property and a place.**

A property in itself is neither good nor bad.
Heavy, slow, doesn't stick, takes a long time, small, not attractive.

Almost nothing on that list is useless everywhere.

There are places that need heavy and places that need slow.
**Drying slowly is a flaw in one market and a requirement in another.**

And this is work you can do immediately.

Write down five flaws in your product.
Beside each, write one more line.

> **Where is this property actually required?**

Leave it blank if it's blank. It might take years to fill.
**But if it isn't written down there's no chance of it filling at all.**

## Second route · From the rough place to the comfortable one

A boot made for a worksite is on a stage.²
A food made for army supply became home cooking.³

The direction here is consistent. **Always from rough to comfortable.**

The reverse is rare. Something made for daily use
rarely becomes the worksite standard later.

The reason is structural.

**A rough environment demands more than everyday life does.**

It can get wet, it can be dropped, it has to last years, it has to be repairable.
Meeting that makes it overbuilt.

And when that object comes down into daily life, the overbuilding comes with it.

**Excess quality becomes two things in daily life.**

One is **patina**, from Part Three. Used for years, it takes one.
The other is **shape.** A form demanded by a rough place is a form from function.

The condition from Chapter 9 is already met.
**A shape that came from function doesn't age.**

One more thing attaches.

**The origin is the story.**

"What miners wore." "What soldiers carried." "What rescue crews used."
That line cannot be manufactured by advertising. It has to have been true.

There's a condition. Not everything from a rough place makes it.

**It has to be visible in daily life.**

Boots, outerwear and bags are visible, so the story attaches.
A component inside a machine, however remarkable, gets no story.

## Third route · An urgent need moved it

War or disaster sometimes moves an object to another country.³

There was no culture of using it there,
and then urgency brought it in by the ton, and it got used differently on the spot.

And it stayed after the urgency ended.
**Because to the people who grew up then, it was always part of the food.**

The generation clock from Chapter 2 is at work.
A taste learned young lasts as long as that person does.

This route has to be stated plainly.

**The maker cannot plan it.**

What's to be learned here isn't a method. It's a fact.
**Where an object goes is not decided by the person who made it.**

The sentence from Chapter 8 appears for the third time.
The one who moves it is always the user.

## So what can be done?

All three are outside your control. Three things still raise the odds.

**One. Don't throw it out.**

Keeping the failures.
A **failure drawer** inside the organisation is enough.

Discarded prototypes, unsold stock, results that came out wrong.
A list alone will do. Writing down what is where is sufficient.

**Two. Show it outside.**

The cheapest and the most effective.

Looked at only from inside, the use doesn't get found.
People in the same field all imagine the same slot.

**The person who finds the use is not the person who made it.**

Show it to someone in a different line of work and they imagine a different slot.
And that slot is usually one the maker never considered.

**Three. Count the strange uses.**

One question to ask customers.

> **What do you use this for?**

When an answer comes back that isn't the intended use, write it down.
When the same answer comes back three times, it isn't a quirk. It's a slot.

All three cost almost nothing.
**What they require is an organisation that isn't ashamed of failure.**
Where failures get hidden, the first one is already impossible.

## How this differs from Chapter 8

The two look similar, so they get separated here.

**Chapter 8 is the same property going to a different place.**
The game shoe's toughness and shape were good on the street too.

**Chapter 12 is going to a different place because of the property that was a flaw.**
Not sticking well was the requirement somewhere else.

So Chapter 12 is more dramatic and **harder to plan for.**

Chapter 8 gives signals you can follow. Rising repair requests will tell you.
Chapter 12 shows nothing until the signal arrives.

**So the prescription in Chapter 12 isn't how to find. It's how not to discard.**

## The short version

| Route | What happened | Plannable? | What you can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flaw became requirement** | Found a place where the property fits | Partly | Write the place beside each flaw |
| **Rough → comfortable** | Excess quality became patina and shape | **Yes** | Build to the rough standard from the start |
| **Urgency moved it** | Used differently in another country | No | Nothing |

> **A flaw is not a property. It's a mismatch between a property and a place.**

## One Line Today

Fold a sheet of paper lengthwise to make two columns.

**In the left column**, write **five flaws** in what you're making now.
Start with the ones you wanted to fix and couldn't.

**In the right column**, write one line beside each.

> **Where is this property actually required?**

Four of the five can stay blank.
**If even one fills in, that one is a place worth testing.**

And there's one more thing you can do today.

**Show that sheet to someone in a different line of work.**
Not someone in your industry. Someone doing something else entirely.

> **The person who finds the use is not the person who made it.**
