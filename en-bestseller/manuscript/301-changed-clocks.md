## The basketball shoe nobody wears on a basketball court

Watch a game and the shoes change every year.
Light, holding the ankle, absorbing impact.

Nobody on that court is wearing a shoe from twenty years ago.

Step outside the arena and it's a different story.

On the street, plenty of people wear shoes whose design is twenty
or thirty years old.¹
Built for the game, and pushed out of the game long ago.

**The same shoe was retired from one place and kept in another.**

Odd. How does something that lost on performance live longer?

## The obvious reading — what lasted was a good product from the start

**Half right.**

Go and read the original descriptions of things that lasted
and something strange happens.

**The reason you use it now isn't written there.**

Built for the game, worn on the street.
Built for a worksite, worn on a stage.
Built as a children's toy, and now adults buy more of them.

The use the maker wrote down and the use it has now are different.

So it's more accurate to say this.

> **What lasted wasn't a good object. It was an object that moved.**

## The maker doesn't change lanes

The most important passage in this chapter.

Moving is not decided in a meeting room.

**The user does it first.**

It wasn't the company that started wearing the game shoe on the street.
It wasn't the factory that started wearing the work boot on the stage.

Somewhere, someone used it outside its purpose, it spread,
**and the maker found out afterwards.**

That order almost never reverses.

The maker tries to hold the original purpose.
They built the plant for it, they've sold on it, they're the expert in it.

So the maker's actual job sounds strange.

> **Not making it change lanes. Not blocking it from changing lanes.**

There are several ways to block, and all of them sound well-intentioned.

"That isn't what it's for."
"Using it that way voids the warranty."
"It doesn't fit the brand."

All three are correct inside the company.
**And all three close the door on moving.**

## Three signals that it's moving

To not block it you have to notice. There are three signals.

**One. You hear about it being used somewhere it wasn't meant for.**

It often arrives as a complaint.
When "can it also do this?" keeps repeating, it has already started.

**Two. The kind of person buying changes.**

Ages change, regions change, occupations change.
When revenue holds steady but the buyers have shifted, the slot is moving.

**Three. People ask you to repair it.**

This is the strongest signal.

Nobody repairs an object they can replace. They just buy another.
**Asking for a repair means the object has become irreplaceable.**

Repair requests usually get filed as cost.
**They are not a cost. They are a measurement.**

If repair requests are rising, that object is changing lanes right now.

## First bridge · Patina — when time adds value

The first of the two landing bridges.

Time takes value off most objects.
New is the most expensive; it gets cheaper with use.

Some objects run the other way.

Leather, heavy cotton, cast iron, wood, instruments.
Things where the marks of use are **value**, not damage.

There are conditions. Not everything takes a patina.

**One, it has to change without breaking.**

The colour fades and the shape settles while the function holds.
Plastic can't do this. It's fine and then it cracks.

**Two, the changed form has to be yours.**

Yours and someone else's have to age differently.
If they age the same way, it's just worn out.

**Three, the beginning has to be slightly uncomfortable.**

Here comes the uncomfortable part.

Things that take a patina are usually stiff at the start.
Leather is hard; cast iron is heavy and needs attention.

**A patina is a device that converts early discomfort into later attachment.**

Which runs directly against where the market is going.
Most improvements are aimed at making the beginning easier.

> **Putting a patina in requires the nerve to make the beginning less comfortable.**

And that decision reduces early sales.
**So most don't do it.** That's why Part Three is short.

## Second bridge · Handoff — when it passes to the next person

The second landing bridge.

Among building-block toys there is an old one.²
What matters isn't the toy. It's **the coupling.**

Blocks made decades ago fit blocks made now.²

That single fact does a great deal of work.

A parent can bring out their own childhood box and give it to a child.
And buy a new box and mix them.

**Handing it down doesn't stop anyone from buying new.**

Three things are required for a handoff.

**One, it doesn't break.** Obvious, and therefore often forgotten.

**Two, the old and the new get used together.**

This is decisive.
Break compatibility and what was handed down becomes a memento with no use.
**An object with no use gets thrown out no matter who gave it to you.**

**Three, there has to be a reason to hand it over.**

This is the hardest.

What gets handed down isn't the object.
**It's the time spent with the object.**

So the object needs somewhere for memory to attach.
Somewhere to write a name, somewhere to put a date,
somewhere the traces of shared use can stay.

An object memory can't attach to doesn't get handed down however sturdy it is.
**It sits in storage and then it's discarded.**

## The ones that moved, in one table

Three cases, marked with the bridges they used.

| | From → To | Bridges used | Who moved it first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Game shoe | Arena → street | Shape · **Patina** | The user |
| Building blocks | Toy → **standard** | **Handoff** · Shape | Former users who became parents |
| A talked-about novel | Front table → classroom | **Repeat** · Handoff | Teachers and parents |

Read the right-hand column down and the chapter's answer returns.

**In none of the three did the maker do the moving.**

## But changing lanes reduces revenue

Look at this critically.
If changing lanes is so good, why doesn't everyone do it?

**Because it loses money. Immediately.**

**One, the price can fall.**
What you charged for professional use may not hold for everyday use.

**Two, handing down means not buying new.**
The better the handoff works, the lower the new sales.

**Three, keeping compatibility means not changing.**
To keep fitting the old parts you give up a better design.

**Four, patina lengthens the replacement cycle.**
Things used for a long time sell less often.

All four are true.
So refusing to change lanes always has a plausible basis.

**Judged by the quarter, refusing is always right.**

> **The value of changing lanes isn't calculated in current revenue.
> It's calculated by whether you exist in ten years.**

And ten years from now doesn't appear in this quarter's report.
**The split between Part Two and Part Three is exactly in that calculation.**

## One Line Today

Pick one object in your home you've used **for a long time.**
Five years or more will do.

Check two things.

**First,** was it better new, or is it better now?

If you answered **now**, that object has a patina.
Write one line on what did it. The material, the shape, or what you did with it.

**Second,** could you hand this to someone?

If yes, write why it's worth handing on.
If no, write what's missing. Is it broken, does it not fit, or does no memory attach?

Then one last question.

> **Is the thing you make better after five years than it is new?**

Answering no is normal. Most objects are like that.
**It only means that object can be sold as new,
and in the contest of newness a newer thing always arrives.**
