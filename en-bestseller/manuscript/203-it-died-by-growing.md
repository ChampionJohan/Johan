## Three of the same sign on one street

Three of the same signboard, within a two-minute walk.

All three opened the same year.
Two closed the following year.

The remaining one has no customers.

The strange thing isn't the closures.

**It's that all three opened at the same time.**

The three people who opened them didn't know each other.
All three looked at the same figures and reached the same conclusion.
**This is what's doing best right now.**

All three were correct. At the time it really was doing best.

And all three went under.
**Which means there is a structure in which a correct judgment kills you.**

## The obvious reading — if it sells, add capacity

There's demand, so there's no reason not to supply it.

**Half right.**

Some businesses can be scaled and some die from being scaled.
From the outside the two look identical.

Same revenue, same queue, same requests to come back.
The numbers on the screen are the same.

What separates them isn't a number.

> **It comes down to what was actually being sold.**

The distinction from Chapter 1 changes the decision here.

## Talk demand is a single nationwide quantity

The most important sentence in this chapter.

**If need was being sold, demand exists separately in each area.**

Winter comes to every neighborhood and babies are born in every neighborhood.
Selling out in one place doesn't reduce demand in the next.
**So adding locations adds revenue.**

**If talk was being sold, there is one quantity for the whole country.**

"That's what everyone's doing now" happens once, nationally.
The number of people who respond to that sentence is the entire market.

Open ten locations and you divide that one quantity by ten.
**You divide it, and the cost is ten times.**

Here is the arithmetic of rapid rollout.

| | Need demand | Talk demand |
|---|---|---|
| Open 10 locations | Demand ×10 | **Demand unchanged** |
| Revenue per location | Holds | **One tenth** |
| Total cost | ×10 | ×10 |
| Result | It grows | **It grows and dies** |

**The same decision is growth on one side and suicide on the other.**

The number from the opening is the last row of that table.

That franchise announced its five hundredth store two years in,
and **seven months later there were four hundred and ninety.**²

What's worth noticing isn't that it shrank.
**It's that nobody said stop while five hundred were being opened.**

Each of them had no reason to stop.
That reason is the third way in this chapter.

## The lease outlasts the fad

Crueler than the arithmetic is the time.

Confirm the fad, find a site, sign, fit out, open.
Several months at best.

**During those months the fad is still running.**

And as the last chapter showed, the fad curve isn't symmetrical.
It comes down all at once.

Nobody knows where on the curve opening day falls.

The contract, however, is already signed.

> **A fad runs for months and a lease runs for years.**

That line is the heart of this chapter.

Failure isn't from being bad at the trade.
**It's from tying together two things with different lifespans.**

Put the long thing on top of the short thing, and when the short thing ends
the long thing is still there. And money leaves every month for it.

## Second way — selling scarcity, then giving it away

The second route to dying by growth happens in the product, not the locations.

For a while there was a snack that was hard to find.¹
Finding one meant going to several shops, and having found one
was itself worth mentioning.

A question has to be asked here.

**Did people buy that flavour, or the fact that it was hard to get?**

Both, probably. And that's where the trouble starts.

Being hard to get is **an asset that disappears when you increase supply.**

But when the signal says it's selling, you want to increase supply.
Someone not being able to buy looks like a loss.

**So production goes up. And the moment it does, half the product is gone.**

Put that next to the queued dessert from the last chapter and it's the same structure.
A queue and a shortage are both **value that requires somebody to go without.**

Which leads to an uncomfortable conclusion.

> **A business built on scarcity ends the moment it satisfies everyone.**

And whoever runs that business is tempted daily,
because the people who couldn't buy keep complaining.

**This is the one case where doing exactly what customers ask kills you.**

Don't misread it. This isn't advice to keep things short on purpose.
**It's advice to know from the start that a business built on scarcity is short-lived,**
and to build another position while it runs.

## Third way — when someone else carries the risk

The place in this chapter that most needs a critical eye.

Rapid rollout usually gets called a misjudgment.
The head office read the fad wrong.

**That explanation is usually false.**

Look at the structure of a franchise and the reason appears.

The head office's customer is not the consumer. **It's the franchisee.**
Head office revenue comes from fees and from supplying materials.

Which produces this.

**When a store opens, the head office earns immediately.**
**When that store closes two years later, the head office returns nothing.**

The party carrying the risk and the party earning the money are different.

So there is no brake.
Even if the fad is visibly cooling, head office has no reason to slow openings.
**Opening as many as possible before it cools is rational for head office.**

> **Rapid rollout is not a misjudgment. It's a placement problem.
> This always happens when the person deciding isn't the person carrying the risk.**

That sentence isn't only about franchising.

A business run on other people's money. A role where the reward comes first
and the outcome later. An organisation where whoever decided has moved on
by the time the result lands.
**Same structure, same result.**

Invert it and you get a test for anyone about to start something.

**"Does the person recommending this business lose anything if I fail?"**

If the answer is no, what they're saying is not information. It's sales.

## So what should you do when it's going well?

After three ways it feels airless.
Add capacity and you die; don't and you miss the moment.

Here this book's prescription appears for the first time.

**When it's going well, add bridges, not locations.**

There are things you can only do while the talk is alive.

| Bridge | What the talk lets you do |
|---|---|
| **Repeat** | Build in a reason to buy again when it's used up |
| **Shape** | Fix the form and the colour. Right now people are looking |
| **Patina** | Put in the property of improving with use |
| **Handoff** | Make it easy to give, easy to hand down |

All four are **far more expensive once the talk is over.**
Fix a shape when nobody's looking and nobody learns it.

> **The real value of a fad isn't the revenue.
> It's that for a while, people are looking.**

What to show them during that window is Part Three.

## Three questions before adding capacity

Reduced to three so they can be used in the room where it's decided.

**One. Where are the buyers coming from?**

From the neighborhood, and it may be need demand.
**From far away, and it's talk demand.** People travel because they heard something.

**Two. Is anyone here for the second time?**

Lots of first-timers and few returners means talk.
This number is easy to count and costs nothing to count.

**Three. Is the contract shorter than the expected lifespan?**

You have to write down the expected lifespan even though you don't want to.
**If you don't, the default becomes "indefinitely," and that default is what ruins people.**

## The short version

| Way of dying by growth | What was increased | What disappeared |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapid rollout** | Locations | Demand per location. There was only one nationally |
| **Giving up scarcity** | Production | The people going without. That was half the product |
| **Separated risk** | Franchisees | The brake. The decider carried no risk |

All three differ from the last two chapters in one way.

In Chapters 5 and 6 something arrived from outside.
The talk ended; the slot vanished.

**In Chapter 7 nobody arrived. It was done from inside.**

> **All three of these were decisions made while things were going well.
> Which makes them the easiest to prevent, and the least prevented.**

## One Line Today

Think of something that's going well right now.
It doesn't have to be yours. Something you're watching from the side is fine.

Write three lines.

**First,** where do the buyers come from? Nearby or far away?

**Second,** what share of them are here for the second time?

**Third,** when do you think this ends? **Write a date.**

The third is hard, and because it's hard most people skip it.

Being wrong is fine. Writing it does something on its own.

> **Once the ending date is written down, what has to be done
> before then becomes visible alongside it.**

And that "what" is Part Three of this book.
