## Two questions

Someone trying to see ahead usually asks this.

> What will be different in ten years?

There's a problem with that question.

**Nobody knows the answer.**

And a larger problem.
Even getting it right, you can't win the contest of getting it right.

Knowing first requires more information than others and moving faster than others.
**That contest is won by whoever already had both.**

Invert the question and the board changes.

> **What will people still be doing exactly the same way in ten years?**

That question has an answer. And the answer **is not a secret.**

## The obvious reading — seeing the future means guessing right

**Not even half right.**

Look at the people who made the things that lasted
and almost none of them guessed what was coming.

They did something else.

**They picked one thing people would keep doing, and sat on it.**

So it has to be written this way.

> **Seeing the future isn't knowing what will come.
> It's knowing what won't go.**

Those two differ completely in difficulty.

Nobody knows what will come.
**Everybody knows what won't go.**

## The list of what won't change

Start from the need clock in Chapter 2.

Cold. Hungry. Sleepy. Hurting.
Bored. Lonely. Wanting to be seen. Wanting to pass something on.

A few more can be added.

Short of time. Short of money. Not sure what to choose.
Wanting to see people. Wanting to be alone. Wanting a child to do well.
A parent is ill. Doing something for the first time is frightening.

The list has a quality.

**It's dull.**

Nothing is new, nothing is announceable, nobody is surprised.

And the things that lasted are standing on that dull list.

## One test

Whether something belongs on the list is settled by one question.

> **Did people a hundred years ago want this?**
> **Will people a hundred years from now want it?**

Yes to both is a **need.**
No to either is a **method.**

This is the distinction that recurs most often in this book.

The vanished things in Chapter 6 were holding methods.
The surviving things in Chapter 8 moved across to needs.

> **The need doesn't change. The method of meeting it does.**

## Build one table

The actual work of this chapter is one table.

| The need that doesn't change | The current method | When does the method change? |
|---|---|---|
| Getting word to someone far away | | |
| Keeping food from spoiling | | |
| Learning something for the first time | | |
| Getting a child to sleep | | |
| Deciding what to buy | | |

The left column doesn't change.
It was there a hundred years ago and will be there in a hundred.

The middle column has changed repeatedly and will change again.

Fill it in and two things become visible.

**One. Which column your work is in.**

In the left column, it can last.
In the middle column, that method's lifespan is your lifespan.

**Two. What the middle column could turn into.**

Trying to fill the right column usually stalls.
**Stalling is normal.** As Chapter 6 showed, skipping is hard to predict.

One thing can still be written.
**Write the most inconvenient part of the current method.**
When a method changes, it usually changes there.

## So what do you make?

The answer becomes simple.

**Pick something people will still be doing the same way in ten years.**
**Make that thing slightly better.**

"Slightly" matters.

Try to change it greatly and you're changing the method,
and changing the method easily becomes the third case in Chapter 6.
The place where something nobody asked for gets pushed.

**Making something slightly better is work people already asked for.**

The hand-made patches in Chapter 16 are the signal.
That someone is patching it also means **they're still doing that thing.**

## The limit of this method

Stated plainly.

Sitting on what won't change is no guarantee of doing well.

Someone may already be sitting there.
The default from Chapter 3 may be held by somebody else.

**And it takes a long time.**

Earning a place on something unchanging takes time.
It isn't fast.

So what this method gives isn't success.

> **It gives a state in which you don't have to guess right.**

You don't have to be anxious every January about what's coming.
**Sit on what won't go, and whatever comes, you live underneath it.**

## The short version

| Question | This chapter's answer |
|---|---|
| The usual question | What will be different in ten years |
| This book's question | **What will people still be doing the same way** |
| Seeing the future is | Not knowing what will come — **knowing what won't go** |
| Why this side | **Nobody knows what's coming; everybody knows what won't go** |
| The test | Wanted a hundred years ago and a hundred years from now? |
| What to make | Make that thing **slightly** better |
| What it gives | Not success — **not having to guess right** |

> **The list of what won't change isn't a secret.
> Everybody knows it, and it's dull, so nobody uses it.**

## One Line Today

Draw a table with three columns.

**Left column:** five needs that don't change.
Use the test. Wanted a hundred years ago, wanted a hundred years from now.

**Middle column:** what you currently do each of them with.

**Right column:** write only one thing.
**The most inconvenient part of the current method.**

When it's filled, mark which column your own work is in.

> **Left column, or middle column?**

Being in the middle column isn't wrong.
Most work is there.

**You only have to know that the lifespan of that method is your lifespan.**
And how to move across to the left column is in Part Three.
