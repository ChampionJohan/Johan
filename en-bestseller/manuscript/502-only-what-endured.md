## The other nine endured too

There's a street with several old shops on it.

Say a queue forms at one of them.
People come and take photographs, an article appears, the story spreads.

And this line follows.

> So holding on does work.

The other shops on that street held on too.
The same years, in the same place, with the same diligence.

**Nothing happened to them.**

This chapter has to start there.

## The obvious reading — hold on and it comes eventually

Other chapters in this book said "half right."

That can't be written here.

**This one is barely right at all.**

Far more things hold on and quietly disappear with nothing happening.
And nothing gets written about them.

The unnamed box from Chapter 4 is at its widest here.

So are Parts Four and Five a lie?
No. The sentence just has to be written accurately.

> **Holding on doesn't make it break out.
> But only what held on can catch it.**

The difference between those two sentences is this entire chapter.

## It has to be there, and being there isn't enough

There are two kinds of condition.

Conditions **without which it cannot happen,**
and conditions **which make it happen.**

Holding on is the first kind.

Without it, never. With it, not necessarily.

Why the distinction matters.

**Take it as the second kind and a person receives a false comfort.**
They're holding on, so surely one day. And that thought carries the day.
And when ten years pass and nothing has happened, they break.

**Take it as the first kind and the question changes.**

Holding on is the baseline, so **what else has to be there?**

If one in ten of the ones who held on caught it, what was different about that one?
The rest of this chapter is the answer.

## Why new products can't catch it

The title's question first.

What people look for when a wave comes is **something old.**
Three of the four carriers in Chapter 14 have oldness as a condition.

And oldness can't be manufactured.

What if you make a new one in the old way?
The old shape, an old name, the feeling of that era.

**People mostly tell the difference.** There's a reason.

> **Oldness comes with evidence, and something newly made has none.**

The evidence is things like this.

Objects actually made back then, still surviving.
The memories of people who used it a long time.
Something that hasn't changed.
The fact that the same thing is in a photograph from ten years ago.

All of that **only comes into existence with the passage of time.**

However much money there is, passed time can't be bought.
So oldness is the one asset a newcomer can never take.

**The drawback is that it takes ten years to make.
The advantage is that nobody can overtake you.**

## What separates the ones that held on

The core of this chapter. Four conditions.

### One. It was there and it was findable

Being there and not findable is the same as not being there.

A wave is fast.
When somebody hears about it and goes looking, **it has to come up right away.**

And the longer something has held on, the worse this tends to be.

The name changed a few times, the location moved several times,
nothing is written down anywhere.

Chapter 13 said not to break the line to the few.
Here it becomes **leave one line open to the world.**

Not changing the name, not moving, being findable when searched.
**All three cost almost nothing and are fatal if skipped.**

### Two. It was there and it was recognizable

Shape, from Chapter 9, pays here.

When a wave comes, people don't look for long.
It has to be recognizable while they go past.

Places that changed a little each year are at a disadvantage.
Nothing about them is old, because everything was changed a little.

**There has to be something that shows that thing then is this thing now.**

### Three. The story was still there

When a wave comes, people look for the story.

Why it's old, who started it, what hasn't changed.
That story spreads and the wave gets bigger.

**Without a story it's just an old shop.**

And a story can't be made afterwards.
That's why Chapter 11 said to leave a record.

Old photographs, old ledgers, the first thing ever made, the record of changes.
**Storing them costs almost nothing, and skipping it can't be undone later.**

### Four. There was a body able to receive it

The cruellest condition.

Shrink too far and a wave arrives and you can't take it.
No people, no capacity, no way to process the orders.

And too large doesn't work either.
As Chapter 7 showed, a large body can't sit through a quiet decade.

So the actual task is this.

> **Setting a size small enough to survive a quiet ten years,
> and large enough to receive one sudden one.**

That size is calculable.
Write down what has to leave every month, and lower it.

**And that is the most practical number in this entire book.**

## Redefining what holding on means

One direction has to change here.

**Think of holding on as waiting and you can't do it.**

Almost nobody can wait ten years.
Waiting shaves a person down a little at a time.

So how did the ones that lasted do it?

**They weren't waiting.**

> **Not holding on while waiting for a wave,
> but holding on in a state that doesn't require one.**

Building a state that turns by itself, however small.

The renewing demand from Chapter 3.
The annual cycle from Chapter 10.
The few who come back from Chapter 13.

None of the three is large. None of the three goes out either.

**Build that state and holding on stops being endurance and becomes just living.**

And in that state, no wave is not a loss.

Aim at a wave and ten years without one is a total loss.
Treat a wave as a bonus and ten years without one is just ten years.

**That difference is what separates people who actually last from people who don't.**

## When it doesn't come

The honest ending.

You can have all four and no wave comes.
**And usually none comes.**

So was the preparation wasted?

No. And the reason is what this chapter most wants to say.

Look at the four again.

Being there and findable. Being there and recognizable.
Having the story. Having a body that fits.

**All four are worth something with no wave at all.**

Findable means people come in ordinary times.
Recognizable means less spent on advertising.
A story means whoever came once comes again.
A fitting body means not being short of breath every month.

> **Because preparing for a wave and simply being in good shape
> are the same thing, it isn't wasted when no wave comes.**

This is why this part isn't comfort.

Comfort changes nothing and improves the mood.
**These four have work attached to them today, and they pay with or without a wave.**

## The short version

|---|---|
| Does holding on bring it | **No.** Only what held on can catch it |
| Why can't new products | Oldness comes with **evidence.** New has none |
| Condition one | **Findable** — name, place, searchable |
| Condition two | **Recognizable** — the shape didn't change |
| Condition three | **The story survived** — the records weren't discarded |

## Sources for this chapter

This chapter names no specific case.

The number of places that met the same conditions and were never rediscovered
can't be counted. Listing only the successes without knowing that number
would reverse this chapter's argument.

The four conditions are traits observed in cases that were rediscovered,
**not a claim that those conditions cause rediscovery.**

## One Line Today

Write down one number.

> **What has to leave every month to keep the thing you do alive?**

Rent, people, minimum materials, the costs that can't be dropped.
Count only what leaves when revenue is zero.

That number is the most important number in this book.
**That number decides how many years you can last.**

When it's written, do one more thing.

> **To cut this number to a third, what would have to be given up?**

Write the list. You don't have to give anything up.
**Written down, it means you've decided in advance what goes first
if things get hard.**

Then one last question.

> **Can you last ten years on that number?**

If not, what needs fixing now isn't the product. It's the number.
**Making a good object matters less than making sure the object is still here in ten years.**
