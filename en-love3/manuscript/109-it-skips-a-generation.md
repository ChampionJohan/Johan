## A Square Skipped

The last chapter said what we receive goes sideways or down. And it often jumps a
square.

What came from a grandmother does not pass through the mother to the
grandchild; it goes from the grandmother straight to the grandchild, and the
mother in between may not have received much of it. Someone strict with their
own child is different with a grandchild. Same person, different.

Why the skipping happens is what this chapter looks at.

## The Raising Place and the Not-Raising Place

One part is circumstance. A parent has to raise the child and a grandparent does
not.

The raising side has to teach, and they have to say no to what must not be
done, they have to feed, they have to get them to school. The person doing
that work takes the dislike. Raising has dislike built into it.

The not-raising side does none of that work. So they can give and only give. The
same person becoming a different person with a grandchild is not a change of
character. It is **a change of position.** Position makes people like that.

## And Not Only Circumstance

Circumstance does not account for all of it. There is one more thing.

Grandparents know what they failed to do when raising their own children. They
know it was because they were busy staying alive. What they failed to do is
still in them, and they do it for the grandchild, and the thing being done for
the grandchild is in fact the thing they meant to do for their own child.

So the middle generation watching this feels complicated. The thought *they never
did that for me* is available, and that thought is not wrong either. And through
that act the parent is doing something late. Both are true, so it will not tidy
up in either direction.

## What Skips Travels Two Squares

Something interesting shows from this angle. The love that skipped lasts longer.

To a grandchild, a grandmother is someone who almost never scolded them. So the
memory stays clean. Decades later, when that grandchild is good to somebody, they
use that shape. **One block of something received with no condition on it is
something a person draws on for a lifetime.**

Twice already it has been written that some people who received little give a
great deal. Ask those people and one person turns up surprisingly often. Not a
parent. One person. A grandmother, an aunt, a teacher, a woman next door. One
seems to be enough.

## Is One Enough

Here is what this chapter cannot solve. The sentence *one is enough* is hard to
write as it stands.

I want to write it. It is hopeful and I have seen such people. And writing it
closes the road on anyone who had none. There are people who had none. For them
this chapter is useless.

And one more thing. There will be cases where there was one and it did not
work out, and the people I met were the ones it worked out for, which is
probably why it looks this way to me. The ones it did not work out for do not
tell this kind of story. So what I have seen is not all of it. Knowing that
while writing is different from not knowing it, so it is recorded.

## Someone Comes to Mind

Is there someone other than a parent who was good to you with no condition on it.
If one person came to mind, you might say their name once, to yourself.

## Where This Chapter Came From

Nothing in this chapter was brought in from outside. Observation and things I was
told, with inference marked as inference.

There is research, as far as I know, on what one person outside the family does
for a child. I confirmed from public sources that the field exists and did not
bring it into the text. Prop up the sentence *one is enough* with a source and
the sentence becomes a conclusion, and I wrote inside this chapter that I cannot
use it as one.

That the people I met are a skewed sample is stated in the text. That is the most
honest line in the chapter.
