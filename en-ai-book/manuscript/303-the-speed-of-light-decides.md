## One number

How long a signal takes to travel from Earth to Mars and back.

| | Distance | One way | Round trip |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closest | about 54.6 million km | about 3 min | about 6 min |
| Farthest | about 401 million km | about 22 min | about 45 min |

Nothing travels faster than light, so there's no way to make this quicker.
Better technology doesn't reduce it and more money doesn't reduce it.

I sat with that table for a while after working it out, because of everything
I've written in this book it's the only number that certainly won't change.
The others move when the way of measuring moves or when the world moves.
This one moves if light slows down.

## What follows

Say you've sent a machine out there. Something unforeseen arrives in front of
it — a wheel comes off, a path has collapsed, a sensor is reading something
strange. It needs to ask what to do, and if it asks, the answer arrives six
minutes later at best and forty-five at worst. The situation is over by then.

So that machine can't do anything at all by asking and waiting.
**Unless it decides for itself, it can't work out there.**

## Why that matters

I had been treating this as a question people decide. How much do we hand to
an AI, where does a person have to check, discuss it, write rules, draw a line.

What the machine said here stopped me.

> **Whether an AI gets to decide for itself isn't settled by ethicists.
> It's settled by the speed of light.**

Long before anyone debates how much autonomy to grant, physics has already
produced a place where it has to be granted. Out there it isn't the conclusion
of a philosophy. It's the premise of an engineering problem.
And premises don't go to committee.

## And not only out there

One step further, and this part has my guesswork in it.

Running a machine that decides for itself at that distance requires methods.
How much it settles alone, how much gets logged, how you undo it when it was
wrong. Those get set, refined, accumulated.

Then the methods come back to Earth, because they've been tried, they work,
and there's something to point at when claiming they're safe. Here, *a person
has to check* is the obvious thing to say; from the side that came back,
*it turned out to work without checking* is experience, and experience beats
assertion.

I think it'll go that way. **This is guesswork, not a confirmed thing**, and it
may not go that way at all. The two could split into separate sets of rules,
and there are fields where exactly that has happened.

## What I thought writing this

Writing this book I asked myself several times whether a given sentence would
still be true in ten years, and mostly I wasn't confident. Sentences about
performance go wrong fast and sentences about what some company is doing go
wrong faster. In an earlier book I barely used names for that reason, and here
the caution had to go further.

The number in this chapter doesn't go wrong. Unless light slows down, that
table stands.

So this will be the longest-lasting chapter in the book.
**It may also be the dullest.** One number and nothing happens.

Lasting and being read now are often different things. I've run into that
writing other books, and this looks like another of those places.

## Where This Came From

**Confirmed**

Distance to Mars and the communication delay calculated from the speed of
light. The distance varies with orbital position, so the closest and farthest
cases are given separately, and 299,792 km/s was used. The calculation can be
redone by anyone.

**Not used**

Actual spacecraft communication is more complicated. It can route through relay
satellites, and there are set windows when an antenna is in view.
**I couldn't confirm that part, so it isn't here.** The table above is therefore
**a floor that can't be pushed down**, not an operational figure. In practice
it would run longer, never shorter.

The part about methods coming back to Earth is **my guess.** The text says so.
