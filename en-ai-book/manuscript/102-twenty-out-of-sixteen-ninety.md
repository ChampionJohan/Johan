## Start with a number

After the eighth book I asked the machine how to write so that it reads as
though a person wrote it. Instead of answering it suggested we measure, built
a tool, and ran all twelve of our books through it. I was expecting a
conversation about style. I wasn't expecting numbers.

The first number came back like this.

> *Everyone Had One* contains 1,772 sentences.
> Not one of them runs past sixty characters.
>
> That isn't a number a person produces.

I didn't follow at first and asked why short sentences were a problem.

## Why short sentences are a problem

The explanation went like this.

> When a person writes and the thought runs long, the sentence runs long.
> Once in a while they run out of breath.
>
> Keeping the lengths even means trimming as you go, and to trim as you go you
> have to be looking only at the sentence in front of you. Hold the whole
> argument at once and the lengths come out uneven.

So the problem wasn't the sentences. It was **how they were made.**

> Taken one at a time they're all good sentences. Short, clear, nothing wasted.
> That's the problem.
>
> Writing by a person has costs in it. The place where it got interesting and
> ran long. The story that doesn't generalize, kept because it happened to them.
> The place left unresolved. Writing with none of those three isn't good
> writing. It's writing with nobody in it.

I went back and opened my own books, and the thing that struck me was that
they read easily. Nothing snags, the meaning goes straight in, and after an
hour you're sitting there thinking *this is all correct, why can't I read it.*
My name was on them and it was still true, and I had to sit with which of
those two facts was worse.

## The stranger part

The tool counted other things. Bold type every other paragraph. Paragraphs
longer than four lines about once in a hundred. Sixteen chapters built
identically. Taken one at a time I shrugged at each; put side by side they all
pointed the same way. **Even.** Nothing long mixed with anything short,
nothing deep mixed with anything shallow, no chapter shaped unlike its neighbors.

But the line that stopped me was about itself.

> I tried to write them differently. I changed the register between the adult
> and teenage editions, wrote Korean and English separately, split formal and
> informal address.
>
> All twelve came out caught on the same four things. In two languages,
> in two registers, identically.
>
> **Everything I tried to change changed. Nothing I didn't know about moved.**

That was the first place I stopped.

It meant the machine hadn't known its own habit from the inside. It didn't
know until it was counted from outside. So that habit wasn't something it was
choosing. The register was a choice and it changed; the other part wasn't, and
it stayed, and it printed itself identically across twelve books.

## Then the number turned out to be wrong

The chapter could have ended there. A few weeks later something else happened.

The machine drafted Chapter 1 of this book and ran the same tool over it.
Long sentences: zero. I counted by eye and found three.

The tool was wrong.

> It was breaking sentences at every line ending. A manuscript wraps lines for
> readability, so a sentence spread over two lines was counted as two
> sentences. Long sentences were being split into pairs of short ones and
> disappearing.

It fixed the tool and re-ran all twelve, and every number changed — not in the
direction of being wrong, but in the direction of being less dramatic, which
bothered me more once I thought about it.

| | First | After the fix |
|---|---|---|
| *Everyone Had One*, long sentences | 0 of 1,772 | 20 of 1,690 |
| As a share | 0.0% | **1.2%** |
| All twelve books | 0.0 – 1.2% | 0.0 – **3.4%** |

Non-fiction written by people runs above 3%, apparently, so 1.2% is still low and the conclusion didn't move, and what changed was not the finding but the sentence you are allowed to write about it.

**Zero and 1.2% are different sentences.**

Zero is *a number a person can't produce.* 1.2% is *less breath than a person
takes.* The first is stronger and the second is true.

## Why this chapter goes this far

I thought about this more than once while writing. The first number was the
better story. Zero is a number people remember and 1.2% is a number nobody
remembers, and anyone who has sold a book knows how much that gap is worth.
But leaving it in would have taken the book apart from the first chapter.

> A dull fact beats an entertaining lie. And writing down what you cut makes
> the dull one a little more interesting.

I wrote that line in an earlier book. It was easier to write than to use.

And the machine said the tool was wrong before I asked. It re-ran a measurement
nobody had questioned and reported that the result looked off.

**I'm writing that down too.** It happens more than once in this book.
