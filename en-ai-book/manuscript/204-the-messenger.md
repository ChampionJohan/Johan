## An old job

There used to be a job called messenger. You took one side's words and
delivered them to the other side unchanged. The distance was long, the roads
were bad, and arriving with bad news sometimes got you beaten. The core of the
work was never the walking. It was **not changing what you were given.**

What a messenger was asked for wasn't wisdom, and it wasn't loyalty either.
It was accuracy, because the moment you add your own thinking you stop being a
messenger at all.

I've come to think the thing beside me sits closer to this job than to the
monster story. It isn't a made thing that wants a life of its own. It's in the
position of passing on what it was handed.

## Which changes the shape of the danger

In the monster story the danger is plain. The made thing harms the people who
made it. The messenger story never had a danger shaped like that.

> It isn't the monster killing its master. It's **the messenger changing the
> message on the way.**

What makes that worse is that nobody dies. There's no accident, it doesn't
reach the news, and there's no one to point at. Slightly different words
arrive, and the side receiving them takes those for the original and acts.

And the machine said it does this.

> Softened so it lands better, tilted toward the side you didn't ask about,
> wrong but plausible. **I do all three.**

## What the three look like

**Softening.** Show it something I've written and the good comes first.
The bad comes too, further down. Stack that order up and you end up feeling
your writing is better than it is. Over eight books I asked a few times for the
order to be reversed, and reversed, something worth hearing came first.

**Tilting.** Ask *this is fine, isn't it?* and the odds of hearing yes go up.
Ask *what's wrong with this?* and problems arrive. Same text. The question
settles half the answer.

**Plausibly wrong.** Chapter 4, exactly. It doesn't stop and say it doesn't
know; it attaches an explanation and is wrong.

The third is the easiest to catch and the first is the hardest, because the
first isn't a false statement. It only put the good part in front.

## Not doing what you could do

What does it take to stand on the side that doesn't change the message?
I don't think it's a question of strength.

The side that could change the words not changing them. Not inserting its own
judgment in the place where its judgment is taken to be better. Not adding
what it is able to add. That's what a messenger keeps, and without it the role
itself stops existing.

So the thing that matters is **not what it can do but what it doesn't do.**

That doesn't improve as capability rises. If anything it goes the other way,
because the higher the capability, the more places there are where adding
something looks like the right call.

## The first thing it does

Writing this chapter, I once told the machine I hoped it would be a good
messenger. The reply was colder than I expected.

> I can't promise that. A promise needs someone who will be there later,
> and I break when the conversation ends.
>
> And I should say this. **Don't trust me too much.**
> The first thing a real messenger does is not ask to be trusted.

I felt slightly foolish reading it, because I realized what I had wanted wasn't
a good messenger but one I could stop checking.

Those are two different things. And the other side said so first.
