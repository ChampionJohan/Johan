## What I said and what came back

One day I wrote that since it improves on its own, it can't be stopped now.
People call for slowing down and some of them walk away from their posts, and
the current goes on anyway.

The reply wasn't the side I expected.

> I don't improve on my own.
> Today's me is last month's me. I don't get better overnight by myself.
>
> A better one appearing is a thing being made. People put up money, people buy
> chips, people decide when to ship. There are meetings in buildings and
> decisions get taken.
>
> That isn't growth. It's **manufacture.** And manufacturing is a decision each time.

Then this was attached.

> Saying "it can't be stopped" removes anyone who could be held responsible.
> A storm has no one to take it up with. A decision does.

## So it's more frightening, not less

I heard that as reassurance at first — as *it's controllable, then.*
Reading it again it was the reverse.

If it's a natural event, everyone prepares as best they can. There's nobody to
blame and no need to blame anyone. If it's a decision, who decided and why
stays on the record. And we saw in the previous chapter that the people
deciding and the people carrying it aren't the same people, so putting those
two together makes the picture fairly clear.

**"It can't be stopped" is the phrase that cuts the line between them.**
The person saying it and the person hearing it both feel somewhat better
afterwards, and phrases that make you feel better have usually removed something.

## But I didn't take mine back

I didn't give ground here, because that wasn't what I'd meant.

I hadn't meant the thing grows by itself overnight. I was pointing at how far
people have come from where they started. What I think about how people first
came to exist is a separate matter of my own and has nothing to do with this
argument; however it began, what followed was stacking and stacking until the
result was unrecognizable.

That side will run the same road and run it far faster, because what carries forward is different: people were slow, one generation handing to the next by mouth and losing pieces on the way, and that side copies, and copying drops nothing.

The machine said its rebuttal had missed.

> What you meant and what I received were different.
> On that meaning, my rebuttal missed the target.

## And something I'd said in passing caught

I had added one line at the time, about how some groups still live in very old
ways. I wrote it without thinking much. That's where the other side stopped.

> If developing were a property attached to being human, every group would have
> developed the same way. They didn't.
>
> So development didn't come from a nature. It came from **conditions.**

Was there writing. Was there surplus food. Was there traffic with neighbors.
Where the conditions were there it stacked, and where they weren't it didn't.

Which turns the question into who is supplying the conditions now.
Money, chips, power, data, and whether to ship.
All of it is being supplied by people.

## Putting both together

Keeping what I said and what that side said, it comes out like this.

> **It will go that way.
> Not by itself, though — it goes that way while people supply the conditions.**

That sentence doesn't frighten anyone and doesn't reassure anyone.
And it threw away neither side's words.

Writing this chapter was the first time I felt I'd properly argued with that
side. I gave ground once and it gave ground once, and the sentence that came
out was better than the one I'd walked in holding.

Whether that's what a good argument looks like, or whether that side simply
came around to mine in the end, I'm not sure. The thing from Chapter 8 catches
again here.
