## Basketball shoes nobody plays basketball in

Watch a game and the players' shoes change every year.
Lighter, more ankle support, better cushioning.

**Nobody on that court is wearing a shoe from twenty years ago.**

Step outside the arena and it's a different story.

Plenty of people on the street wear shoes whose design hasn't changed
in twenty or thirty years. Made for the court, and pushed off the court
a long time ago.

**The same shoe was retired in one place and stayed in another.**

Strange. How does something that lost on performance live longer?

## The usual thought — what lasted was a good product to begin with

**Half right.**

Go and read the original description of things that lasted
and something odd happens.

**The reason people use it now isn't written there.**

Made for competition, worn on the street now.
Made for workshops, worn on stage now.
Made as a children's toy, bought mostly by adults now.

**The use the maker wrote down and the use it has now are different.**

So the accurate sentence is this:

> **What lasted wasn't a good product. It was a product that moved.**

## The maker isn't the one who moves it

The most important part of this chapter.

Moving spots doesn't get decided in a meeting room.

**The users do it first.**

The company didn't start people wearing sports shoes on the street.
The factory didn't start people wearing work boots on stage.

Somewhere somebody used it outside its intended purpose,
it spread, **and the maker found out later.**

That order almost never reverses.

Makers try to defend the original use.
They built for it, they sold on it, they're the experts in that field.

So the maker's job sounds a little strange:

> **Not making it change clocks. Not blocking it from changing clocks.**

There are many ways to block it and they all sound sensible.

"That isn't what it's for."
"Using it that way voids the warranty."
"It doesn't fit the brand."

All three are correct inside the company.
**And all three close the door on moving.**

## How to tell it's moving

Three signals.

**One. You hear about use outside the intended purpose.**
"Can it also do this?" repeating means it already started.

**Two. The kind of buyer changes.**
Different ages, different regions. Same revenue, different people — it's moving.

**Three. People ask to have it repaired.**

That's the strongest signal.

**People don't repair things they could replace.** They buy a new one.
**Asking for a repair means the thing became irreplaceable.**

Repair requests usually get filed as a cost.
**They aren't a cost. They're a measurement.**

## First bridge · Patina — when time adds value

For most things time subtracts. New is the most expensive; use makes it cheaper.

Some things run the other way.

Leather, heavy cotton, cast iron, wood, instruments.
**Things where marks of use are value, not damage.**

There are conditions. Not everything takes a patina.

**One, it changes without breaking.**
Color fades, shape presses in, function stays. Plastic can't do this.
It's fine and then it cracks.

**Two, the change has to be yours.**
Mine and yours have to age differently. Aging identically is just wear.

**Three, the beginning has to be a bit uncomfortable.**

Here's the uncomfortable bit.

Things that take a patina usually start out stiff.
Leather is hard. Cast iron is heavy and needs looking after.

**Patina is a device that converts early discomfort into later attachment.**

Which runs exactly against where markets go.
Most improvement goes toward making the beginning easier.

> **Building in patina takes the nerve to make the start less comfortable.**

And that decision reduces first-time sales.
**So most people don't.** That's why so little survives into Part Three.

## Second bridge · Handoff — when it passes to the next person

There's an old block toy.
What matters here isn't the toy. It's **the way the pieces clutch.**

That clutch system was patented more than sixty years ago.
**Blocks made then fit blocks made now.**

To keep that one fact true, the company gave something up.
**It didn't switch to a better clutch system for sixty years.**

A parent can pull out the box from their own childhood and hand it over.
And buy a new box and mix them.

**Handing down doesn't block buying new.**

Handoff needs three things.

**One, it doesn't break.** Obvious enough that it gets forgotten.

**Two, old and new work together.**

This is decisive.
Break compatibility and what was handed down becomes a memento with no use.
**A thing with no use gets thrown out soon after it's inherited.**

**Three, there's a reason to pass it on.**

This is the hard one.

What gets handed down isn't the object.
**It's the time spent with the object.**

So the thing needs somewhere for memory to attach.
A place to write a name, a date, a mark left by something done together.

A thing memory can't attach to doesn't get passed on however tough it is.
**It sits in storage and gets binned.**

## The ones that moved, in a table

| | From → To | Bridges built | Who moved it first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sports shoes | court → street | Shape · **Patina** | users |
| Block toy | toy → **a standard** | **Handoff** · Shape | the old kids, now parents |
| Wristwatch | telling time → wearing | Shape · Handoff | users |

Read the right column down and the answer appears again.

**In all three, the maker didn't do the moving.**

## But changing clocks costs money

Look at this sharply.
If changing clocks is so good, why doesn't everyone?

**Because it loses money. Immediately.**

**One, the price can fall.** What a professional pays, an everyday buyer won't.

**Two, handed-down units aren't bought new.** Better Handoff, fewer new sales.

**Three, keeping compatibility blocks change.** Fitting old pieces means
giving up a better design.

**Four, patina lengthens the replacement cycle.** Things used long sell less.

All four are true.
So refusing to change clocks always has a plausible reason behind it.

**Judged by this month, refusing is always correct.**

> **The cost of changing clocks isn't measured by what you earn now.
> It's measured by whether you exist in ten years.**

And ten years from now doesn't appear on this month's statement.
**That calculation is exactly where Parts Two and Three split.**

## The short version

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who moves the spot | **users.** The maker finds out later |
| The maker's job | not making it move — **not blocking it** |
| How you notice | odd-use questions · buyer shift · **repair requests** |
| Patina needs | changes without breaking · ages individually · **uncomfortable start** |
| Handoff needs | doesn't break · **old and new work together** · memory attaches |
| Why nobody does it | all four reduce what you earn right now |

## Do This Today

Pick one **thing you've used for a long time** in your house.
Five years or more is enough.

Check two things.

**One,** was it better new, or is it better now?

If you answered **better now**, that thing has Patina on it.
Write one line on what did it. The material? The shape? What you did with it?

**Two,** who could you hand this to?

If you can, write why it's worth handing on.
If you can't, write what's missing. Broken? Doesn't fit? No memory on it?

Then the last question.

> **The thing you make — is a five-year-old one better than a new one?**

Answering no is normal. Most things are like that.
**It just means that thing can only sell new,
and in the new-thing competition something newer always arrives.**
