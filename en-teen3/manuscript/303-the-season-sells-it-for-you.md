## Songs forgotten for eleven months that come back

Every December the same songs turn up again.
In shops, in the street, everywhere.

They aren't new songs. They came out a long time ago.

Something odd here.

Normally a song gets played less over time.
New songs arrive, people move on, it fades out quietly.

**These songs get played *more* as time passes.**

And the stranger part is the other eleven months.

For most of the year nobody plays them. **They're completely forgotten.**
**Forgotten and not dead.**

## The usual thought — seasonal goods only sell once a year, so they lose

**Half right.**

The selling window is short, that's true. There's something you get for it.

> **The calendar does your advertising.**

Normal products need continuous spending so people don't forget.
Stop and they're forgotten, and forgotten is over.

Seasonal products don't need that.
**When the time comes, people remember on their own.**

December brings carols to mind; cold weather brings thick coats to mind.
Nobody has to tell them.

It's the same kind of asset as Shape in the last chapter.
**An asset maintained without a monthly payment.**

## There are only three ways to make someone buy again

Chapter 2 named Repeat, the first bridge. Here's the full version.

**One. It runs out, so you buy more.**
Food, detergent, pencils, consumables. The most common and most reliable.

**Two. The time comes round, so you buy again.**
Seasons, holidays, term starts, birthdays, regular servicing.

**Three. Once at each stage of life.**
The doors from Chapter 3 are here.

Of the three, **the second is the strongest.** One reason:

> **Nobody has to remember.**

Running out requires noticing that it ran out. Miss it and there's no purchase.
Life stages arrive unpredictably and depend on the person.

**The calendar announces the time.** Buyer and seller both know in advance.

## Four things a season gives you

**One, the advertising window is fixed.** You don't spend all year.

**Two, being forgotten is survivable.**

This is decisive.

Normal products are finished when they're forgotten.
**Seasonal products are allowed to be forgotten.**
They're better for it — being forgotten is what makes December welcome.

**Three, you can plan.** You roughly know when and how much will sell.

**Four, new people arrive every year.**
The rotating customers from Chapter 3 attach automatically.
Every year somebody hits that moment for the first time.

Put the four together and almost every condition for lasting is met.
**Which is why so many long-lived things are seasonal.**

## And three prices

Seasons are not free.

**One. Miss a year and you wait a year.**

Late preparation, not enough stock, an accident — and the whole year is gone.

Normal products can make it up next month.
**A seasonal product has no next month to make it up in.**

So in this business **the cost of a mistake is twelve times larger.**

**Two. No money comes in for eleven months.**

This one actually kills people.

**This is the classic case of the product succeeding and the business failing.**
Materials get bought in advance, staff have to stay, rent goes out monthly.

**If you can't afford to wait out the season, don't sell a seasonal product.**

That isn't a product problem. It's a money problem.
And people rarely look at the money structure while choosing the product.

**Three. Seasons can move.**

The least visible risk.

A season looks like the need clock. Winter comes every year.
**But what people *do* in that season is on a different clock.**

What gets exchanged at a holiday, what gets prepared for a new term,
where people go in winter.

Those sit on the generation clock. They change over ten or twenty years.

> **The season is fixed. What people do in it isn't.**

So what a seasonal business checks each year isn't the weather.
**It's whether people still do the same thing on that day.**

## Can you make a season?

Some seasons were made.

An example belonged here, **but it was cut because it couldn't be confirmed
from primary sources.** Which company did what and when is recorded
differently depending on where you look.

Here are the conditions. Three.

**One, there has to be a date.** The date is the memory device.
Without it there's no custom.

**Two, there has to be someone you give to.**
Things done alone rarely become customs.
**A handing-over motion is what spreads it to the next person.**

**Three, a first-timer has to know exactly what to do.**
Complicated rules don't spread.

And an honest note:

**Whoever makes a season cannot own it.**

Once it's a custom, anyone can sell on that day.
The company that made it sells, the copiers sell, the corner shop sells.

> **Making a season buys you a market, not a share of one.**

So anyone trying to make a season has to take one more thing.
The **default** position from Chapter 3.

The spot that comes to mind when someone asks "what do I get?"
**The season is shared. The default is one.**

## Building a cycle into something with no season

Most things have no season. There are four things you can still do,
cheapest first.

**One. Attach to a day that already exists.**
Much cheaper than making a new season. Find a day people already do something
on and build a spot there.

**Two. Design the rate at which it runs out.**
Too long and it's forgotten; too fast and it feels expensive.

**Three. Let the object announce the replacement time.**
Color changes, a mark appears, wear becomes visible.
**Not leaving it to human memory is the point.**

**Four. Make something that runs on a yearly unit.**
Something with a year on it, something only made that year,
something you fill in year by year.
**Things people collect get a cycle for free.**

All four cost design rather than money.
And all four only work if **you don't change them once made.**

## The short version

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What a season gives | **the calendar does the advertising** |
| Why "the time" is strongest | unlike running out, **nobody has to remember** |
| If it's forgotten | seasonal products **come back from being forgotten** |
| Price one | miss a year, wait a year. Mistakes cost twelve times |
| Price two | eleven months of no money. **Product succeeds, business fails** |
| Price three | the season is fixed; **what people do that day isn't** |

## Do This Today

Open a calendar. Paper is good; a screen is fine.

**Mark every day in the year when something has to be bought.**

Holidays, birthdays, term starts, season changes, exams, anniversaries.
More come out than you expect.

Then look at two things.

**One,** write what gets bought on each of those days.
If it's the same thing every year, that's a keeper for you.

**Two,** are there months with nothing marked?

> **An empty month is a seat nobody has sat in yet.**

And a last question.

> **What day on the calendar is the thing you make attached to?**

If it isn't attached to any day, it means you have to manufacture
a reason to think of it **every single time, with money.**
