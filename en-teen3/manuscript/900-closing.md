## Back to five hundred and a thousand years

The opening had two shops.

One that hit five hundred in two years and shrank seven months later.
One that has sold grilled rice cakes, one item, for a thousand years.

Sixteen chapters on, what looks different?

**The five hundred was on the fashion clock.**

When the clock stops, it stops with it. The more shops, the bigger the stop.
Exactly Chapter 6. **It died by growing.**

**The thousand years was on the need clock.**

There's a shrine in front, people come to pray every year,
and every year they're different people.
That's the spot from Chapter 3. **Where customers are new every year.**

And that shop didn't expand.
No department stores, no new items, no branches.

That wasn't laziness.

> **It stayed small enough to survive a quiet thousand years.**

## One thing this book never said

Something deliberately left unsaid.

**This book doesn't decide which side you want to be on.**

It never said earning big and finishing is bad.
It never said earning small for a long time is right.

Both are valid choices, and **both cost something.**

- Go big and it's fast, and **you don't know when it ends**
- Go long and it doesn't go out, and **the prize comes late or never**

Which one you pick depends on your situation and your temperament.

**Choosing without knowing and choosing while knowing are different.**

The people who opened those five hundred shops mostly worked hard.
**What was missing wasn't effort. It was knowing which clock they were on.**

## Sixteen chapters in one sentence

> **Don't try to guess what will take off.
> Find what won't go away, sit on it,
> and spend your time on not disappearing.**

None of the three needs special talent.

Guessing needs **faster information than everyone else.** You lose that one.
Finding what won't go needs **looking closely.** That one is free.
And not disappearing needs **not quitting.**

Which is why the last chapter is called "A Game You Can Start Late."

## But doing something for a long time is the hard part

Honestly: the third is the hardest.

Tell someone to wait ten years and nobody can.
Waiting shaves a little off a person every day.

So Chapter 16 said it differently.

> **It isn't waiting through for a wave.
> It's being in a state where you don't need one.**

The thousand-year shop didn't wait a thousand years.

**It grilled rice cakes every day. And each day ran itself.**

Build a state that runs itself, even a small one,
and staying stops being endurance. **It becomes just living.**

How to build that state is Parts Three and Five.

## One last thing to do

Before you close the book, just this.

**Find the oldest object in your house.**

It doesn't have to be yours. Your parents'. Your grandmother's.

A bowl, a pair of scissors, a blanket,
a strange box nobody has thrown out.

Hold it and ask one line.

> **Why is this still here?**

An answer will come. Usually one of these:

- something makes you use it again (**Repeat**)
- you know it by looking (**Shape**)
- long use made it better (**Patina**)
- it was handed down (**Handoff**)

**This book was about those four.**

And the object in your hand right now
**has most likely never once been famous.**

Never on a chart. Nobody talked about it.

**It just didn't go away.**

## And one more

The last question. This one really is the last.

> **What will still be in my hands in ten years?**

Ask it about work, not only objects.

Something you'd still be doing in ten years.
Something nobody credits you for and you keep noticing anyway.

If you have one, do that.

**If you don't, that's fine. You're still looking.**
And the time it takes to look isn't a loss.

The staying game counts from today.

## What was confirmed while writing this

One last thing to put down.

Looking at a lot of long-lived things while writing this,
one thing was common to them.

**Almost none of them planned to last.**

The thousand-year shop didn't plan a thousand years.
The failure became a product by accident twelve years later.
The thirty-seven-year-old song had a drama scene.

None of it was planned.

> **Old things weren't born special.
> They just never grew a reason to disappear.**

So what you have to do today isn't make something great.

**It's removing reasons to disappear, one at a time.**

You can start that today.
