## A bookshop has two spots

Walk into a bookshop. Right at the entrance there's a big table
with books lying flat, covers facing up.

**Most of those books are gone in three months.**

Twenty steps further in there are shelves.
Books standing upright, only their spines showing.

**Some of those have been in the same slot for ten years.**

Twenty steps between the two spots.
**And they are two different businesses.**

The entrance is how a bookshop **earns.**
The back is how it **survives.**

Same building, same product. Why does it split?

## The usual thought — the ones that sold well survived

The idea is that the best of the big sellers stick around
and become the long sellers.

**That's less than half right.**

Some do survive, sure. The rate is very low.
And there's something more important.

> **A lot of long sellers didn't sell at all at first.**

If that's true, you can't put the two on one line.

**They're not first place and last place. They're different tests.**

## What a trend actually sells

Look closely at something selling in huge numbers.
It isn't only the object moving around.

**People are talking about it.**
To be in that conversation you have to have one.

**A trend sells the right to be in the conversation.**

That has a funny property:

> **Selling a lot is itself the reason it sells more.**

"Everyone's buying it" becomes the thing people say,
and saying it makes more people buy it.
Past a certain point it rolls by itself.

**And it ends for exactly the same reason.**

Talk **loses value the more times you hear it.**
To the person hearing it for the third time, it's nothing.

When the conversation ends, the reason to buy ends.
**The object didn't change. The reason disappeared.**

## What a keeper sells

Long sellers are usually not conversation.
Almost nobody talks about them.

What they have instead is this:

**There's a specific moment when not having one is a problem.**

Winter arrives. Exams arrive. You move. You do something alone
for the first time.

Those moments don't pick favorites.
**Every year the same moment arrives for different people.**

A keeper is the thing standing in that spot.

It sells not because it's being discussed,
but because **the moment came and it was there.**

> **A trend bets on what people are talking about now.
> A keeper bets on when people get stuck.**

## Draw them

Draw the two and the shapes are nothing alike.

**A trend is a peak.** Straight up, straight down.
Tall and narrow.

**A keeper is a plain.** You can't see it rise.
Low, wide, and nobody remembers when it started.

Here's where people get it wrong.

Peaks are visible and plains aren't, so peaks look bigger.

**The area under the plain is often larger.**

The problem is that **you only find that out ten years later.**
So at the moment of choosing, the peak always looks bigger.

## The money runs the opposite way

More important than the shapes:

**A trend costs more the more it sells.**

Talk is its fuel, and cutting fuel kills it immediately.
So you have to keep spending while it sells.
**Peak sales are peak spending.**

**A keeper barely costs more when it sells more.**

People come looking for it.
It just has to be sitting where the question gets asked.

Stop promoting it for a year and not much changes.
**That one line is what separates what the two businesses keep.**

## So are trends bad?

No. This book doesn't say that.

There are three things only a trend gives you.

**One, speed.** It builds in three months what a plain takes ten years to build.

**Two, visibility.** People find out the thing exists.
However good it is, nothing happens if nobody knows.

**Three, it's a doorway into being a keeper.**

A lot of today's long sellers were once enormous trends.
They didn't come down off the peak. **They moved onto the plain.**

Part Three is entirely about that.

So the problem isn't aiming for a trend.

> **The problem is preparing to take off
> and not deciding where you're going afterwards.**

## The short version

| | Trend | Keeper |
|---|---|---|
| **Sells** | conversation | the answer at a moment |
| **Why it sells** | it sold, so it sells | the moment came round again |
| **Who buys** | whoever's in the conversation | whoever hit that moment this year |
| **Selling more** | wears the talk out | sets the name harder |
| **Stop promoting** | dies immediately | barely moves |
| **Shape** | tall narrow peak | low wide plain |
| **Why it ends** | the conversation ended | **the moment stopped existing** |

Read the last row again.

A keeper usually doesn't end because something better showed up.
**It ends when the situation it was standing in disappears.**

Part Two uses that sentence as its ruler.

## Do This Today

Pick up three objects within reach.
Not expensive ones — near ones.

Write one line on each: why you got it. Then tag it:

- **Talk** — everyone was talking about it
- **Answer** — something came up and you needed it

Then one last question.

> **Of the three, which ones do you actually still use?**

They usually cluster on one side.
**That side is the side you should be making.**
