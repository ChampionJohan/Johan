## Everything in your house is a different age

The iron pan in the kitchen might be twenty years old.
The phone in your pocket is about two.
The yogurt in the fridge is three days old and gone in three more.

All three were bought with money. Their lifespans differ by a factor
of over a thousand.

**It isn't price.** The phone cost more than the pan.
**It isn't durability.** Phones usually get replaced before they break.

**Different clocks are running inside different objects.**

This chapter counts them. There are three.

## The usual thought — make it well and it lasts

The idea is that quality decides lifespan.

**Half right.**

Quality decides **how long the thing holds up.**
Lifespan is decided by **how long people keep wanting it.**

There's something in your house that works fine and nobody uses.
It wasn't put away because it broke.
**It was put away because the wanting stopped.**

So the question changes.

> **Not "how long does this last" but "how long does wanting this last."**

That wanting runs on three cycles.

## One · The fashion clock — months

This clock runs on **what other people are doing right now.**

So its cycle is short. The moment everyone has one, the value is gone.

Things on the fashion clock share one property:

**Having one alone is no fun.**

The value isn't inside the object. It's **in your relation to everyone else.**
When they stop caring, the one in your hand loses value too.

**That thing that went round your class and then suddenly nobody did —
that's this clock.**

Things on this clock make money fast.
**And nobody knows when they'll stop.**

Not knowing when is the real danger of this clock.

## Two · The generation clock — ten to thirty years

Some things get into you when you're young.
A familiar sound, a familiar taste, a familiar shape of clothing.

This doesn't turn over in months like fashion. It also isn't forever.

**It ages as those people age.**

Here's the frightening part:

> **Nobody standing on this clock thinks they're standing on fashion.**

If you think it's a fad you prepare for the end.
If you think it's taste, you don't.

It hasn't changed in over ten years, so it looks like it never will.
That belief usually gets proved wrong around year fifteen.

**The generation clock is the easiest one to be fooled by.**

Think about the music your parents like.
To them it isn't a fad. **It's good music.**

## Three · The need clock — it doesn't turn

Cold. Hungry. Sleepy. Hurt.
Bored. Lonely. Wanting to show someone. Wanting to pass something on.

That list was the same a hundred years ago.
It won't be very different in a hundred more.

**This clock doesn't turn.**

There's one very important condition attached:

> **Needs don't change. The way a need gets met changes constantly.**

Keeping warm doesn't change. What you keep warm with keeps changing.
Getting news to someone far away doesn't change.
What you send it with has changed many times.

So sitting on the need clock takes care.

**Hold onto a method and you disappear with the method.**
**Hold onto the need and you stay with the need.**

That sentence appears more than any other in this book. Mark it.

## All three clocks live inside every category

This clears up a common mistake.

The category doesn't set the clock.
Clothes aren't fashion because they're clothes.

**All three clocks exist inside one category.**

| Category | Fashion clock | Generation clock | Need clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clothes** | this year's cut | what that generation wore | thermals, socks, underwear |
| **Food** | the queued-for dessert | the taste of being small | rice, water, salt |
| **Music** | the song of that summer | that generation's sound | lullabies, holiday songs |
| **Books** | this year's talked-about title | that generation's required reading | dictionaries, cookbooks, scripture |
| **Devices** | the new standard | the way that generation learned | fire, water, light |

**Read that table across, not down.**

Clothes and books are completely different categories.
Things in the same column behave identically.

**Thermal underwear and a dictionary are on the same clock.**
Both sell every year with no advertising. Neither has ever been news.

**The clock decides an object's fate, not the category.**

## Reading the clock wrong

Two mistakes. They point in opposite directions.

**One. Spending long money on the fashion clock.**

Buying equipment, signing a lease, buying things meant to last years.
The trend ends, the revenue goes, and the equipment stays.

That's Chapter 6. **It is the most common way to go under.**

**Two. Selling a need-clock thing like a trend.**

Pushing urgency, changing it often, making limited editions.

The value of a need-clock thing comes from **the fact that it doesn't change.**
The belief that this year's is the same as last year's is the asset.

Keep changing it and you're shaving down your own asset.

**The first mistake loses money. The second loses time.**
And you can't buy time back.

## You can change clocks

That's the diagnosis. A diagnosis alone doesn't tell you what to do.

Luckily clocks aren't fixed.

**Things that started on the fashion clock have moved onto the need clock.**

There are only four bridges across.
The names go here; how to build them is Part Three.

| Bridge | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Repeat** | Do you buy another when it runs out? |
| **Shape** | Do people recognize it without being told? |
| **Patina** | Does it get better with use? |
| **Handoff** | Can it pass to someone else? |

**Things with two or more of the four last.**
One bridge is a season. Zero is this month.

## The short version

| | Fashion clock | Generation clock | Need clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cycle** | months | 10–30 years | doesn't turn |
| **Runs on** | what others do | when you grew up | the body and life |
| **Value sits in** | your relation to others | memory | the trouble of a moment |
| **Main danger** | no idea when it stops | **not knowing it's fashion** | mistaking method for need |

> **None of the three clocks is good or bad.
> Deciding without knowing which one you're on — that's the bad part.**

## Do This Today

Walk through your house and pick five objects.
**Include the oldest one and the most recent one.**

Next to each, write when you got it and which clock it's on.

Then the last question.

> **The most recent one — will it still be in this house in ten years?**

If you answered yes, write one line saying why.

**If that line names one of the four bridges,
your answer is probably right.**
