## Three identical signs on one street

Within a two-minute walk there are three identical signs.

All three opened the same year. Two closed the next year.
The one left has no customers.

The strange part isn't that they closed.

**It's that three of them opened at the same time.**

The three people who opened them don't know each other.
All three looked at the same thing and reached the same conclusion.
**This is what's working right now.**

All three were correct. It genuinely was working best at the time.

And all three went under.
**Which means there's a structure where a correct judgement ruins you.**

## The usual thought — if it's selling, expand

The idea is that there's no reason not to add supply where there's demand.

**Half right.**

Some businesses can expand. Some die when they do.
And from outside they look identical.

Same revenue, same queue, same requests. **The numbers match.**

What splits them isn't the numbers.

> **It's what was being sold.**

The Chapter 1 split changes the decision here.

## Fad customers come in one national helping

The most important sentence in this chapter.

**If need was selling, the customers are separate in every neighborhood.**

Winter comes to every neighborhood; term starts in every neighborhood.
Selling out in one doesn't reduce the next one's customers.
**So expanding expands.**

**If talk was selling, the customers are one national helping.**

"That's the thing now" happens once, nationally.
The number of people who respond to it is the entire business.

Open ten shops and you divide that one helping by ten.
**You divide it and spend ten times as much.**

| | Need customers | Fad customers |
|---|---|---|
| Open 10 shops | 10× the customers | **same customers** |
| Revenue per shop | unchanged | **one tenth** |
| Total cost | 10× | 10× |
| So | you grow | **you die while growing** |

**The same decision is growth on one side and suicide on the other.**

The number from the opening is the last row of this table.

The chain announced its five-hundredth shop two years in,
and **seven months later there were four hundred and ninety.**

What matters isn't that it fell.
**It's that nobody said stop while five hundred were being opened.**

## Contracts outlive fads

Crueller than the numbers is the time.

Confirm the fad, find a site, sign, fit it out, open.
Months at best.

**The fad is still running during those months.**

And as the last chapter showed, fads come down all at once.

Nobody knows where on the curve opening day lands.
The contract is already signed.

> **Fads run for months. Contracts run for years.**

That line is the heart of this chapter.

The reason people go under isn't bad trading.
**It's tying together two things with different lifespans.**

Put a long thing on top of a short thing and the short one ends
while the long one stays. And the long one costs money every month.

## Two · Selling scarcity and then killing the scarcity

The second road to dying by growing happens in the product, not the shop.

There was a snack that was hard to get for a while.
Finding one meant going to several shops, and having found one
became something to talk about.

Question:

**Did people buy the taste or the fact that it was hard to get?**

Both, probably. And that's where the problem starts.

Being hard to get is **an asset that disappears when you add supply.**

But a strong sales signal makes you want to add supply.
People who couldn't buy look like lost money.

**So production goes up. And half the product disappears with it.**

That snack did go through a long stretch of being sold out,
then a new plant doubled supply,
**and its popularity declined afterwards.**

Put that beside the queued-for dessert from the last chapter and it's
the same structure. Queues and shortages both
**need somebody who couldn't get one in order to have value.**

That gives an uncomfortable conclusion:

> **A business built on scarcity ends the moment it satisfies everyone.**

And the person running it is tempted every single day,
because the people who couldn't buy keep complaining.

**This is the one case where giving customers what they ask for kills you.**

Don't misread it. Nobody is telling you to withhold supply on purpose.
**The point is to know from day one that a business built on scarcity
has a short lifespan** — and to build another position in the meantime.

## Three · When somebody else carries the risk

This is the part to look at hardest.

When shops swarm in and vanish, people call it "bad judgement."

**That explanation is usually wrong.**

Look at how a franchise is built and the reason appears.

Head office's customer is not you. **It's the people who open the shops.**
Head office revenue comes from franchise fees and selling supplies.

Which produces this:

**Head office earns the moment a shop opens.**
**If that shop closes two years later, head office doesn't give the money back.**

The side carrying the risk and the side earning are different sides.

So there's no brake.
Even when the fad is visibly fading, head office has no reason to slow down.
**Opening as many as possible before it dies is rational for head office.**

> **The swarm isn't a mistake in judgement. It's a problem of arrangement.
> Whenever the decider isn't the risk-carrier, this is what happens.**

This isn't only about franchises.

Work done with other people's money. Places where the reward comes first
and the result comes later. Decisions made by people who'll be somewhere
else when the result lands.
**Same structure, same outcome.**

Flip it and you get something you can use right now.

**"Does the person telling me to do this lose anything if I go under?"**

If not, what they're saying isn't information. It's a sales pitch.

## So what do you do when it's going well

After all three of those, it feels hopeless.
Expand and die; don't expand and miss it.

This is where this book's prescription first appears.

**When it's going well, what you expand is bridges, not shops.**

There are things you can only do while the fad is alive.

| Bridge | What the fad lets you do |
|---|---|
| **Repeat** | build in a reason to buy another when it runs out |
| **Shape** | fix the form and color — people are looking right now |
| **Patina** | build in something that improves with use |
| **Handoff** | make it easy to give away and to pass down |

All four get **much more expensive once the fad ends.**
Fixing a shape while nobody's looking means nobody learns to recognize it.

> **The real value of a fad isn't the money.
> It's that people are looking at you for a while.**

What to show them in that window is Part Three.

## Three questions before expanding

**One. Where do buyers come from?**

From the neighborhood, they might be need customers.
**From far away, they're fad customers.** Coming far means they heard about it.

**Two. Is there a second visit?**

Lots of first-timers and few returns means fad.
This number is easy to count and costs nothing.

**Three. Is the contract shorter than the expected lifespan?**

Write down the expected lifespan even though you don't want to.
**Not writing it makes the default "forever," and that default ruins people.**

## The short version

| How it died by growing | What got expanded | What disappeared |
|---|---|---|
| **The swarm** | shops | customers per shop — there was one national helping |
| **Killing the scarcity** | production | the people who couldn't buy — half the product |
| **Split risk** | franchises | the brake — the decider carried no risk |

All three differ from the previous two chapters in one way.

In Chapters 4 and 5 something arrived from outside.
**In Chapter 6 nobody arrived. They did it to themselves.**

> **All three are decisions made while things were going well.
> Which makes them the easiest to prevent, and the least prevented.**

**No specific company's fees or supply prices are given.**

The three signs on one street is **a scene, not a real case.**

## Do This Today

Think of something that's going well right now.
It doesn't have to be yours. Something you're watching is fine.

Write three lines.

**One,** where do the buyers come from? Nearby or far?

**Two,** how many came a second time?

**Three,** when do you think this ends? **Write a date.**

The third is hard, and because it's hard nobody writes it.

Being wrong is fine. Writing it does something on its own.

> **Write down the day it ends
> and you also see what has to be done before then.**

And that "what" is Part Three.
