## Take all the labels off

Put ten objects on a desk. Take every label off.

Most become unidentifiable. No maker, no idea if it's expensive.

**A few you still recognize.**

The curve of a bottle. The tread on a sole. One color on a wrapper.
The angle of a handle.

The name is gone and the name is still there.

Strange. **You erased the name. Why is the name still there?**

## The usual thought — a brand is a logo

The idea is that a good logo, shown enough, becomes a brand.

**Half right.**

It's missing the order in which people recognize things.

**A logo has to be read. A shape is known before reading.**

Recognizing letters means stopping your eyes and focusing.
Shape gets processed before that. From far away, small, blurred — still known.

That difference has large consequences.

| | Logo | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| To recognize | takes reading time | **known before reading** |
| If you can't read the script | you can't | you can |
| In another country | needs translation | **works as is** |
| Far away, small | invisible | visible |
| On a shelf | you have to pick it up | **you know it walking past** |

The last row is what this chapter is worth.

> **Shape works while you're spending nothing on advertising.**

Sitting on the shelf is itself the advertisement.
And that advertisement has no monthly bill.

Chapter 1 said a keeper costs almost nothing extra when it sells more.
Shape is one of the devices that does that.

## Three conditions for a shape becoming a name

Not every shape becomes a name. Three things are needed.

**One. It has to be drawable.**

This is a test you can actually run.

Hand people paper and ask them to draw it from memory.
**If the drawings come out similar, it has a shape. If not, it doesn't.**

Drawable shapes share something. **They're simple.**
Describing one takes less than a sentence.

**Two. It can't change.**

Shape accumulates. The same form has to be seen for a long time
before it becomes a name.

Change it yearly and nothing accumulates.
And the urge to change comes **from inside.**

**The maker looks at their own object far more than anyone else.**
Looking a lot makes you sick of it. Being sick of it makes you want to change it.

The buyer sees it a few times a year. The maker sees it every day.

> **The one who got bored is the maker, not the buyer.**

**Three. It has to come from function.**

The most important one and the most often missed.

Shapes that lasted mostly became that shape for a reason.

Tread patterns came from not slipping.
The curve of a bottle came from gripping it with a wet hand.
A handle's angle came from using less force.

A shape that came from function has a big advantage.

**It needs no explanation.** You look and you see why.
And **it doesn't age.** If the function hasn't changed, the shape can't look odd.

A shape made as decoration ages with its era.

So there's a question to ask:

> **Did this shape come from function, or from the taste of its time?**

Fix a shape that came from taste and it passes when the taste passes.
**The shape stops being an asset and becomes a dated look.**

## Shape isn't only shape

Widen the scope here.
The core of shape isn't form. It's **not going through language.**

Which means five other things do the same job.

**Sound.** The sound of opening, of closing, the short note on switching on.

**One color.** Recognized by a single specific color.
Color gets processed even faster than form.

**Proportion.** The ratio of the sides. The form can change and the ratio stays.

**Texture.** How it feels in the hand. This one doesn't need eyes at all.

**Smell.** The longest-lasting in memory and the least used.

None of the five needs reading.
And none of the five **can be peeled off like a logo.**

A logo can be removed, covered, copied.
Sound and texture can't.

**Copying them means changing the object itself.**
At which point it isn't a cheap knockoff anymore.

## What having a shape costs you

Time to be honest.

**Shape is an asset and a shackle.**

Fix a form and that form starts blocking things.

You could make it lighter, but the silhouette would change, so you can't.
You could make it cheaper, but you'd lose that texture, so you can't.
You found a better structure, but it wouldn't be recognizable, so you can't.

That list gets longer with time.

So companies with a strong shape are **late to technical change.**
Not from incompetence. From protecting the asset.

**You cannot have a shape without paying that price.**

And there's one especially dangerous case.

**When that shape was on the fashion clock to begin with.**

Fix a form built from the taste of its era
and the object passes when the taste passes.
But it's already fixed, so it can't be changed.

**The shape ties the object to its own decade.**

## So how do you change it — erase and test

There will come a time to change while keeping the shape. There's a method.

**Keep the skeleton, change the flesh.**

First you have to know **what the skeleton is.**
Most makers don't know what people actually recognize. They guess.

The way to find out is simple. **Erase things one at a time.**

Show it with the color removed. Still recognized?
Keep the form, remove the pattern. Still recognized?
Keep only the proportion, remove everything else. Still recognized?

**Whatever survives all the erasing is the skeleton.**

Protect that one thing and everything else can change.

This work costs almost nothing. **Paper and ten people.**
Not many places have done it.

Most do the opposite. They change everything slightly without knowing
what the skeleton is.
Recognition fades slowly,
**and one day nobody recognizes it and nobody can explain why.**

## The short version

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why is shape strong | **no language step.** Known before reading |
| What it saves | advertising. Sitting there is the ad |
| Three conditions | drawable · unchanging · **from function** |
| Is shape only form | sound, color, proportion, texture, smell do the same |
| What it costs | you can't change. **Late to technical shifts** |
| Most dangerous case | when the shape came from **the taste of its era** |
| How to change | find the skeleton by **erasing**, protect that one thing |

> **Shape isn't a free asset.
> It's an asset you buy by selling your freedom to change.**

## Where This Came From

A bottle's own shape can be registered as a trademark.
And the same company's attempt to register a **new, contour-less bottle shape**
as a shape trademark was not accepted.

**The registration years are not given.** Sources differ on them.
No year gets printed until the registry record itself is checked.

That tread patterns and bottle curves came from function is written
only as a general explanation.

## Do This Today

Get paper and a pencil. Don't look at the objects.

**From memory**, draw three things you like.
They don't have to be good drawings. Recognizable is enough.

Then compare with the real ones.

- Anything that came out similar is **an object with a shape**
- If nothing came to mind at all, you know that one only by its logo

And one more.

> **If people drew the thing you make from memory, what would appear?**

If the answer is nothing, that's the job right now.
**Shape doesn't cost much to make.
The work is fixing it and then not changing it for a long time.**
