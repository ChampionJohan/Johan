## How a failure survived twelve years

A company set out to make a very strong adhesive.
What came out was the opposite. **An adhesive that barely sticks.**

It sticks, but weakly. Peel it and it comes away.
It leaves no mark.

Measured against the goal, a complete failure.

Normally it ends there. It didn't.

The person who made it didn't throw it away.
**He walked around the company asking.**
"Is there anything this is good for?"

He asked for years and the answer kept being no.

Then another researcher thought of something.
The paper markers in his hymn book kept falling out.

**An adhesive that peels off and leaves no mark.**
That was exactly the answer to that problem.

From discovery to a proper product took **twelve years.**

There's probably one on your desk right now.

## The usual thought — throw failures away

**Half right.**

Most failures should be thrown away.
Otherwise the storeroom fills with rubbish.

There's one kind of failure you don't throw away.

> **When the goal was missed and one strange property appeared
> that you weren't aiming for.**

The weak adhesive is that.
**"Weak" was a defect, and in the right spot it was a feature.**

Which gives this chapter's sentence:

> **Defect or feature isn't decided by the object. It's decided by the spot.**

## Four shapes of a defect becoming a feature

**One. When weak is good.**
Too weak to use, until the spot requires peeling.

**Two. When short-lived is good.**
Disappearing fast is a flaw, until the spot requires leaving nothing behind.

**Three. When ugly is good.**
Not finished smoothly, until the spot wants the look of a human hand —
then the price goes up.

**Four. When stiff and crude is good.**
Not made comfortable, until the spot is rough use — then it outlasts everything.

All four have the same structure.
**The property didn't change. The verdict flipped.**

## Sometimes the users find the spot first

Sometimes the maker doesn't find it and **the users do.**

There was a cheap work boot.
Air in the sole meant less pain from standing all day.
So at first it was worn by postal workers and factory workers.

It had nothing to do with fashion.

Then group after group wore it.
Musicians wore it, then another group, then another.

**Nobody had built it to sell that way.**

There's something to learn here.

> **If it's being used somewhere odd, that's the hint.**

The most common mistake sellers make is this:

**"That isn't what our product is for."**

Saying that throws away the next ten years.

## So what do you watch — three things

**One. Something people do that nobody told them to.**

If people use it in a way that isn't in the instructions, watch that.
One person is a quirk. Several people arriving at it separately
**is a spot.**

**Two. Whether they're putting up with inconvenience.**

If it doesn't quite fit and they use it that way anyway, the signal is strong.
**Putting up with inconvenience means there's no substitute.**

**Three. Whether they're buying something else alongside it.**

If people buy extra things to make your thing work, that's the real spot.

## But changing the use changes the customers

Not just good news. Here's the price.

When the use changes, **the original customers often don't stay.**

When a work boot becomes a fashionable boot,
the person who wore it for work leaves — price up, or hard to find.

So that company **lost its old business and gained a new one.**
Not added. **Swapped.**

Count it as "customers went up" without noticing and you get a surprise later.

And one more.

The new arrivals might be **on the fashion clock**,
while the original customers were on the need clock.

Think back to Chapter 2.
**Change the clock and you change the lifespan.**

## Where This Came From

Public sources cross-checked.

1. The weak adhesive: that it came out of an attempt at a strong one,
   that its maker spent years looking for a use inside the company,
   that another researcher thought of a use from the hymn-book marker problem,
   and that it took **twelve years from discovery** to reach a full release
   after test marketing. The maker's published company history carries this.
2. The air-cushioned boot: that it began as a cheap work boot for postal and
   factory workers, and that various subcultures wore it afterwards.

**Not used**

A military processed food that became an everyday dish in one region
belonged here, but **the country and product couldn't be confirmed,
so the names were cut.** Only the structure is written.

## The short version

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Throw every failure away? | keep the one that **grew a strange property** |
| Defect or feature | the object doesn't decide. **The spot decides** |
| What to watch | uninstructed use · tolerated inconvenience · what's bought alongside |
| The most dangerous sentence | **"That isn't what it's for"** |
| The price | changing the use **swaps your customers** |

## Do This Today

Find three objects in your room or bag that you're **using for something
other than what they're for.**

There definitely are some. Everyone has some.

Write one line on each.

> Why did this fit this spot?

Then the real part.

**Check whether the three have something in common.**

Cheap? Right size? Doesn't matter if it breaks? Stiff? Light?

If one shared property appears,
that's **what you actually value in objects.**

People mostly don't know what they choose on.
**The things you're using wrongly will tell you.**

Words can be dressed up. How you're already using something can't.
