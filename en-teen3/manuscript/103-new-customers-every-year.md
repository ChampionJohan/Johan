## A shop nobody returns to is twenty years old

There's a shop that sells baby things.

A customer usually comes once and then stops coming after a few years.
Once the child grows, there's nothing there to buy.

**Almost no regulars.** The owner rarely learns a face.

That shop has been on that spot for twenty years.

The cafe across the street is the opposite.
There are people who come every day and the owner knows their names.

**And the owner changes every few years.**

Strange. The one without regulars stays. The one with regulars closes.

## The usual thought — lasting requires regulars

**Half right.**

Regulars are good. **They are not the condition for lasting.**

There's only one condition for lasting:

> **New customers keep appearing.**

The same people coming back is one way.
**Different people coming is also a way.**

The second way gets talked about far less.
And most things that last are living on the second way.

## Stacking customers and rotating customers

There are two kinds of customer.

**Stacking.** They stay and add up. This month sits on top of last month.

**Rotating.** They turn over. This month isn't last month's people.

| | Stacking | Rotating |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | pile up | turn over |
| When it's going well | grows fast | doesn't grow |
| When it's going badly | shrinks fast | **doesn't shrink** |
| If you stop | they leak out | it stays put |
| What it needs | holding on | **being there** |

The two middle rows are the point.

The stacking side has to grow to live. Stop growing and trouble starts.
**The rotating side doesn't grow, and it doesn't shrink.**

Not growing sounds like a flaw. **It also means not shrinking.**

## Think about doors

Rotating customers don't appear anywhere.
They appear at **the places in a life that repeat.**

This book calls those places **doors.**

Starting secondary school.
Going somewhere alone for the first time.
Selling something for the first time.
The first exam that actually matters.
The first job.

**These doors open every year.**
And every year **different people** are standing in front of them.

You stood at a door last year,
**and this year someone else is standing there.**

People at a door have three things in common.

**One, it's their first time, so they don't know.** They ask instead of choosing.

**Two, they're in a hurry.** Term already started; the exam is next week.
They don't compare for long.

**Three, they're afraid of getting it wrong.** First time means not knowing
what's risky, so they pick the safe thing.

Put those three together and the competition changes completely.

> **At a door, the better thing doesn't win. The first thing to come to mind wins.**

## Being what comes to mind

Call that position the **default.**

It's an extremely strong place to stand.
It doesn't sell because it's better than the rest.
**It sells because it's the answer to a question.**

This position has an odd property:

**You can't buy it.**

Defaults aren't made by advertising.
They're made by **what the people who already went through that door said.**

The one who went first tells the next one. That piles up, and it becomes default.

So it takes time. **And that's why it's hard to take.**

Someone new can build a better thing.
**They cannot build ten years of what people said on their way through.**

## So why is such a good spot empty

Being honest here.

If this spot is so good, why isn't everyone crowding into it?

**Because it's boring.**

Rotating customers don't spike. This year and next year look the same.
In numbers there's nothing to brag about. Nothing to announce.

People with money to invest want to see growth.
So that kind of money doesn't come here.

> **Competition is thin here not because it's hard to get in
> but because it doesn't look impressive.**

Remember that sentence. **Chapter 16, the last thing this book says,
grows out of it.**

Where big money doesn't come, **whoever stays longest wins.**
And staying costs less than growing does.

## When these customers end

Rotating customers end too. They end differently.

It's rare for a competitor to end them.
**The door closes.**

If people stop hitting that situation, it's over.
If they still hit it but stop solving it with your thing, also over.

The line from the last chapter comes back:

**Needs don't change; methods do.**

Did the door close, or is the door open and something else became the answer?
**Telling those two apart is Part Two's job.**

So the one thing a long seller checks every year is this:

> **Not who the competitors are. Whether that door is still open.**

## The short version

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who's the customer | **whoever hit that door for the first time this year** |
| Why they buy | first time, in a hurry, afraid of being wrong |
| What wins | not the better one — **the one that comes to mind** |
| How you get there | what people who went through piled up |
| Why competition is thin | no growth. **Doesn't look impressive** |
| When it ends | not competition — **when the door closes** |

## Do This Today

Think of **something you did for the first time in the last year.**
It doesn't have to be big. First time is enough.

Write down what you bought. Then write how you found out about it.

- I saw an ad
- I searched
- **I asked someone**

Which of the three?

If you found out by asking, that thing is sitting in the **default** spot.

Then one last question.

> **Is the thing you're making something people would tell each other about
> when they hit something for the first time?**

If not, you haven't found the door yet.
**Finding a door costs time, not money.**
