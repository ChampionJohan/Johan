## 20 · 70 · 10

In the 1980s a large American manufacturer used a method that sorted employees
into three lumps. The top twenty percent were the strong ones, the middle seventy
were the serviceable ones, the bottom ten were the ones to let go, and the proportions were fixed first so that they added to a hundred, and people were fitted into the
proportions.

The last chapter was inside the house. At home the person measuring feels bad.
Here they do not. It is their job, and not doing it means not having done the job.

## You Know on Your First Day

A software company used a similar method for a long time, and in 2012 a magazine
ran a long piece on that company's previous decade. In it is a line from a
developer who had worked there.

> If you're on a team of ten people, you walk in the first day knowing that, no
> matter how good everyone is, two people are going to get a great review, seven
> are going to get mediocre reviews, and one is going to get a terrible review.

The first day. Before anything has been done at all.

## The Boxes Exist Before the Work

What that line shows is the shape of evaluation. It looks like something scored
after the work is seen; it is a procedure in which the boxes come first and people
are put into them. **It is not measuring. It is dividing.**

Which is why the same person's rating changes when they change teams. The work is
identical and depending on who is sitting nearby they land in a lower box or a
higher one. What the rating tells you, then, is not the person but the composition
of the team, and the person receives it as news about themselves.

If one of ten has to go in the bottom box, then on a team where all ten are good,
one still goes to the bottom, which means a seat comes round every year in which
someone who has done nothing wrong becomes the person who did.

## Switched Off in Six Words

In November 2013 that software company dropped the method. An internal email is
reported to have said this.

> No more curve.

And a paragraph later.

> And no more ratings.

No curve and no ratings. A machine that had been turning for close to thirty years
was switched off in six words. Several other large companies followed.

It is good news, and this chapter cannot end there. Ending on an institution being
reformed makes it a business story. The side this chapter wants to look at is the
people who had been inside those boxes, and their several years cannot be returned
by an email.

I have almost never written a rating on a review form. I have received them
several times, and what I received were not numbers but words. Naive. Does not
know how the world works. Kind. They last far longer than any grade does, and
that story belongs to the next chapter.

## What It Means to Be Folded

A review form has about ten boxes on it. Those boxes do not so much cut a person
as fold them. Folded, they fit, and once they fit, the folded side is not visible.
Unlike cutting, nothing is gone — and to the person reading the form it is exactly
the same as gone.

What is on the folded side? Someone who took every question the new arrival had.
Someone who said the thing nobody in the meeting wanted to say. Someone who took
on the work that produces no number and left it tidy for whoever came next. It
fits no box, so it is not written.

It is strange that the side not written down is the side used for seeing people as
people.

## So What Do You Raise and Lower With

From here on I am empty-handed.

Abolish evaluation and what do you raise and lower with? You cannot not raise and
lower. Salaries have to be set and positions have to be set, and for that someone
has to write down who did more. The companies that switched off the curve removed
the ratings and kept on raising and lowering. So it may be that the ruler did not
disappear; it went somewhere you cannot see. Whether an invisible ruler is better
than a visible one, I do not know. A written grade can at least be argued with.

## In Short

| What evaluation looks like it does | What it actually does |
|---|---|
| Measures that person's work | Fits people into fixed proportions |
| The better person goes higher | The box turns on who is sitting nearby |
| What is written is the person | The unwritten side is folded out of sight |

## Today

Think of one person you work with and find one thing **their review form has no
box for**, and tell them. Not praise — you have to pick something unwritten. One
thing with no bearing on the grade they got this year.

The answer that comes back most often is that they did not know they were doing
it. Work that does not get recorded is not being counted by the person doing it
either.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

The developer's line is from Kurt Eichenwald's article in *Vanity Fair*, August
2012, and the two sentences ending ratings are from an internal email sent by that
company's head of HR in November 2013. The 20 · 70 · 10 method is generally
attributed to General Electric in the 1980s.

Both quotations are at the level of public sources cross-read against one another.
I could open neither the magazine original nor the full email. They carry quotation
marks, so the originals should be seen before publication.

Only one date is used. The years in which other companies dropped it differ across
sources, so they are handled as *followed*, and company names appear only where they are necessary, because if this chapter reads as a story about one company, the reader
concludes it is not about theirs.

The next chapter is the same folding with no paper in it. A review form folds
once a year. Folding with the mouth happens every day.
