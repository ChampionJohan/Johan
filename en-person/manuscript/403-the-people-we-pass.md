## Back to the Same Spot a Week Later

In 1972 a psychologist tried this. In the morning rush he went to a commuter rail
platform and photographed the people waiting. A week later he came back to the
same platform at the same hour and handed the photographs to the people standing
there.

He asked two things. Is there a face here you recognize? Have you ever spoken to
any of them?

About eighty-nine percent of the people who received a photograph recognized at
least one person. The average was four. Almost none of them had ever spoken.

## Recognized and Not Known

The psychologist gave this relationship a name. **The familiar stranger.**

Someone you see every day in the same spot, whose face you know, whose absence
yesterday you would have noticed, and to whom you have never once spoken. It runs
both ways. The people on that platform knew about four faces, and those four knew
them in exactly the same way.

That is not being strangers and not being acquainted. So what is it? It is knowing
the role only. That person is the one who takes that car every day, and to them I am the one who stands in that spot every day, and most of the people we meet in a day
are in this relationship.

## Thirty-Two Years Later, Again

In 2004 other researchers used the same method again. This time not on a platform
but in plazas, at bus stops, on walking routes. Seventy-seven point eight percent
recognized at least one person, and the number recognized averaged about three out
of sixty-three.

There is a sentence that wants to be written here. It dropped from 89 to 77.8, so
people recognize each other less than they used to. I will not write it.

The places measured are different. The first is people who stand on the same
platform at the same hour every day; the second is people crossing a plaza, and someone who stands in the same spot daily and someone passing through were always
going to come out differently. Line the two numbers up and blame the intervening
years, and from that point the mismeasuring is ours.

They are at the school and at the security desk and in the restaurant. People who
do their work without comment. I barely know any of their names. There was a day
when one of them came into view as a person. It was not because I spoke to them.
**It was watching them help without a word.** Nobody had asked and nobody was
making anything of it. They did it and went on.

Two things changed afterward. I greet them in passing. And when there is something
small to share, I share it. It is not a dramatic change. Only that I did neither
before.

## You Notice When They Are Not There

This relationship has one property. Present, they are invisible; absent, they are
visible.

When someone who was always in that spot is not there one day, you notice at once.
A different person at the security desk and you know. The one at the shop you
always go to is not there and you know. You do not know their name and you have
barely exchanged words and you know they are gone.

So it is not that we do not know them. We know, and do not know that we know. In
Part Two I wrote that a person shows where usefulness has been taken away; here,
the person shows where the person has been taken away. There are several a day who
only become visible by being missing.

## What the Fare Card Drew

There is one more study, from 2013. Using the transit card records of a city of
about five million, it linked who had been on the same bus at the same time as
whom. Nobody was asked anything; only the records were read.

Three things came out. Repeated encounters were regular, and therefore
predictable. People who met that way did not split into small clusters; the longer
it went, the more densely they linked, and the result is a very large network laid
across the whole city, and not one person inside it could feel that it was there.

People we pass only as roles are not nothing to us. They are an invisible
something. It is already laid down, and we simply do not know it.

## See Everyone as a Person and the Day Does Not Finish

Here is the unresolved part. Try to see every person you pass in a day as a person
and the day does not finish.

There are a hundred people in a subway car. Seeing a hundred people one at a time
cannot be done. So we see them as roles, and seeing them as roles is not rudeness;
without folding them away like that you could not cross the street. Then how far
do you fold and from where do you unfold? Unfold the people you see daily and fold
the ones you see once? Decide it that way and the chapter ends up saying that only
people you see often are people, and that cannot be right. I did not finish this
arithmetic.

## Today

**Say one more thing** to someone you only meet as a role. Not a greeting — one
more thing after it. That it is cold, that the queue was long earlier, that you
wondered whether they are always here at this hour. One is enough.

The answer may be short or may not come. Even so, once that one sentence has
passed, there is a line between you that was not there before. The next time they
are missing, this time you will notice.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

The 1972 work is Stanley Milgram's "The Familiar Stranger: An Aspect of Urban
Anonymity," first published in a division newsletter of the American Psychological
Association and reprinted in his 1977 book. The 2004 work is by Paulos and Goodman
of Intel Research Berkeley, and the 2013 transit-card study is by Sun, Axhausen and
others in *PNAS*.

I could open none of the three originals. This is public sources cross-read
against one another.

Two notes. First, the 1972 figure appears as both 89 and 89.5 percent depending on
the source, so it is written as about eighty-nine percent. Second, **the 1972 and
2004 numbers are not placed side by side,** because the places surveyed differ. The
reason is written into the body of this chapter because I saw them placed side by
side in several other pieces of writing.

the street. Part Five is what happens when one more thing enters those three
places.
