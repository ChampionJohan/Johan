## That Day

My grandmother lay in bed for six months. The first thing she stopped being able
to do was eat by herself. Lifting a spoon is something a person does for a whole
life without once thinking about it. Once that starts to fail, from then on
someone has to be beside you.

Over six months my grandmother handed things back one at a time. And at the end
she left me one sentence.

> Grow up and be a fine person.

Then she died.

I do not remember what I said back. What I remember is only that sentence. **The
last thing a person who could no longer do anything did was to want something for
me.**

## Handed Back One at a Time

Growing old does not arrive all at once. It arrives one thing at a time.

One year the driving stops, a few years later stairs get avoided, after that the
handwriting shakes, and then names come slowly, and putting one thing down can take years or it can finish overnight, and either way what was put down does not come
back. It is handing back, one at a time, what you used to be able to do, and the
place where something was handed back does not refill.

And every time something is handed back, it gets counted.

## This Time the Person Counts Themselves

That is what is different here from the two chapters before. **The person doing
the counting changes.**

A baby does not count itself. It does not know it can do nothing. With someone
laid up ill, mostly the surroundings do the counting; the person is tied up in
getting better and the counting happens outside them.

Growing old is not like that. The person counts. On the day the car keys are
handed over, the one who knows most exactly what has been subtracted is the
person handing them over, and they redo that arithmetic every day. They do it
without anyone counting for them.

Which is why this gets said.

> I am no use now.
>
> I am just a burden.

## The Comfort Uses the Same Ruler

Once that is said, what the person beside them says back is almost fixed.

Don't talk like that. You're still sharp. You're better than I am. This house runs
because of you.

They are good words, and nearly the only words available in that moment. But take
them apart and they are all doing one thing. **They are insisting that some
usefulness is still left.**

Which leaves the ruler in place. The ruler that says there is worth where there is
something you can do and none where there is not, now being held by the comforting
side as well. The person comforted is lighter for a moment, puts one more thing
down next month, and comes back to the same place, and this time there is one less thing to insist on.

Ten times out of ten it is a losing fight. Their side shrinks every year and this
side loses one argument every year.

## So What Do You Say

This is the wall in this chapter.

I understand that you must not answer usefulness with usefulness, and I have not
yet built what to say instead. *It's all right not to be useful* does not work.
Anyone who has said it out loud knows. It does not land as comfort. It lands as a
verdict.

Saying nothing does not seem to be the answer either. No reply to that sentence
sounds like agreement.

What worked least badly, over several tries, was not answering and moving
sideways. Not arguing with the sentence and not letting it slide past either —
just going toward what happened today. That is not because it is the answer. It
is what I do for lack of anything better.

## "What Did You Used to Do?"

This is the question people reach for most often in front of an old person.

There is nothing bad in it. It is on the courteous side. It is offered in order
to stand the person up, and in fact an old person's face does brighten when the
question comes.

But this question uses the same ruler. Because no usefulness is visible in the
person now, it **borrows some from the past.** They used to run something big, and borrowed things have to go back, and when the conversation ends you return to the
present.

And the people who get asked this often know that. They know which time this is.

## Today

Ask one old person something other than what they used to do.

Which means asking about now. What they enjoy most these days. What they ate
today. What annoys them most. What strikes them as strangest about people now.

The last one is good. It is a question they have to answer, there is nothing to
borrow from the past, and the answer that comes could only come from them.

Then listen. That is all of it.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

There are no outside facts in this chapter either. That is true of all four
chapters in Part Two.

One note. *I am no use now* and *Don't talk like that* are written as things
commonly said, not as anyone's particular words. They are not quotations.

And the passage saying that not answering and moving sideways worked least badly
is within what the author has tried; it is not a recommended method, and there is probably something better, and this book did not find it.
