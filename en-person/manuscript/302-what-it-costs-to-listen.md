## The Hard Part Is Not Holding Back

People say the hard part of listening is holding back the urge to cut in. Half
right. Look at the numbers and there is something that catches earlier. **Asking
comes first.** And more gets caught in the asking.

## Eighteen Seconds of What

There is a study from 1984. Seventy-four office visits were recorded and gone
through. Patients completed their opening statement in seventeen of them, which
is twenty-three percent. In fifty-one, sixty-nine percent, the doctor cut in and
turned the questioning toward a specific symptom, and the average time to that
point was eighteen seconds.

The eighteen seconds travelled widely. Something fell off along the way: what
that study counted as an interruption. It counted four things, and the first of
them is a closed-ended question.

> When did that pain start?

That is not a rude thing to say. If anything it is the sound of someone doing their job well, and it stands the patient's story aside and begins asking what the
diagnosis needs, and from there the questions come in a row. So what the study
measured was not rudeness. **It was the point where listening turns into
processing.** Eighteen seconds is not how long until someone was cut off; it is how long until the person in front of them became a case.

## Thirty-Five Years Later

In 2019 another study looked at the same room, and its number is harder. In
thirty-six percent of visits the doctor asked at all what the patient had come for, and where they did ask, the median time to interruption was eleven seconds, and
where they did not interrupt, patients finished in six seconds.

Not asking at all is the bigger number, not interrupting. Two out of three were
not cut off; they never started. And look at the last figure. Left alone, it is
six seconds. Six seconds is the amount of patience required.

I am not going to put the two studies side by side and say things got better or
worse. They counted different things. The 1984 study counted time to
interruption; the 2019 study counted whether anyone asked. Line up two measurements
taken with different rulers and from that point the mismeasuring is ours.

## We Assume People Will Talk a Long Time

Look again at the six seconds. Left alone, people finish what they have to say in
six seconds.

But what is in our heads at the moment we cut in is not those six seconds. It is
an assumption that if this person gets going it will run on. Because of that
assumption we move early. We throw in a question to give the thing direction,
thinking that is better for both of us.

The assumption is wrong, and that is what the number says. Mostly it is six
seconds.

There is a reason we feel it will be long. What we remember are the people who
talked a long time. The one who finished in six seconds does not stay in memory;
the one who went ten minutes stays sharply, and so the average in our heads is longer than the real one. We meet the next person with that average.

This may be the most useful passage in the chapter. **Knowing the estimate is
wrong** is easier than learning to hold back. Holding back takes will. Knowing
does not.

## The Number Survives and What It Counted Falls Off

By the time eighteen seconds reached the newspapers it had become *doctors do not
listen to patients.* The number was not wrong. What fell off was what the number
had counted. Once that is gone, what is left is one sentence blaming people.

Read as a chapter about doctors, this one is useless. Doctors are not the only
ones who make that turn. The person answering the phone, the counselor, the clerk
handling a complaint, the parent listening to a child — all of them make the turn
at some point. They have to. Without it the work does not get done. What this
chapter wants to look at is not abolishing the turn but the fact that it comes
earlier than we think.

Someone was telling me about the abundance of this world. I asked, in the middle,
what the ultimate purpose of a life is.

That is not a rude thing to say. The opposite. It is a bigger and deeper question
than the story he was telling, and I asked it because I thought it mattered more,
and I still think so.

The story ended there. What he had been about to say about abundance I never
heard to the end. Good questions cut better.

## The Work Still Has to Get Done

Here is where I cannot cleanly divide. Listening to the end is not always right.
A doctor has to work after the eighteen seconds. Let every patient say everything
they want to say and the number of people seen in a day falls, and by that much
someone else does not get seen. So that turn is also for someone's sake.

The six seconds narrows the gap a little. Six seconds to listen to the end is not
time nobody can spare. But not every setting finishes in six seconds. Where
listening ends and work begins — I did not draw that line, and I have never seen
it drawn.

## In Short

| Commonly known | What was actually measured |
|---|---|
| Doctors cut patients off in 18 seconds | Closed questions begin at 18 seconds |
| Interrupting is the problem | Not asking at all is the bigger number (36%) |
| Listening to the end takes a long time | Uninterrupted, it ends in 6 seconds |

## Today

In one conversation today, count the places where you wanted to cut in. Not don't
cut in — just count. On your fingers, or afterward from memory.

Counting shows two things. There are more than you thought, and most of them were
not rude remarks but good questions.

If you have room, one more. Once, at a place where you want to cut in, **count to
six in your head.** If the person finishes while you are counting, they were a
six-second case; if not, ask then. All you lose is six seconds.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

The 1984 study is Beckman and Frankel in *Annals of Internal Medicine*, volume
101, number 5, pages 692–696, and the 2019 study is Singh Ospina and others in the
*Journal of General Internal Medicine*, volume 34.

I could open neither original. This is public sources cross-read against one
another, and the figures and citations are confirmed at that level. Someone should
read the originals in a library before publication. This chapter is especially
dependent on its numbers.

One thing is not used. Between the two there is a 1999 study on the same subject;
I found the citation but could not support its figures, so it is out. And lining
all three up turns it into a story about change over time, which, as written
above, is not what this material can carry.
