## Autumn 1785, a Diary

At a college in America the president is examining the graduating class. There
are fifty-eight of them. He calls them up one at a time, asks, listens, and when
it is over he opens his diary and writes down how the day went. What he writes is
four words. Twenty are the best, sixteen are second best, twelve are below, ten
are worse. It is in Latin.

Fifty-eight people have become four lumps. It is said to be nearly the first
recorded grading in American education.

## Only the Bottom Word Is Different

Look at those four words once more. Three of them say what a person is. Best,
second best, below. They may sting depending on how you hear them, but at least
they are statements about the person.

**Only the bottom one is different.** That word is a comparative. It means
*worse.*

So the name attached to those ten does not tell you what sort of people they
were. **It tells you who they are worse than.** It is a name that does not exist
without the forty-eight ahead of them, and if those forty-eight improve, the ten
get worse without moving.

What this book is trying to say is already inside four Latin words from 1785. I
sat looking at it for a long while when I found it: one line of a diary from three centuries ago has the same shape as the review form in use at work right
now.

## We Know That Word

I cannot read the name in that bottom box as a distant old story. The school
system I grew up in has that name in it, unchanged.

There is a box called a grade band. That box is not decided by what I did or how
much of it. It is decided by how many people are ahead of me. So I can study
exactly as I studied last year, get exactly the score I got last year, and drop a
box, because the people around me did better. And I can rise a box having done
nothing at all.

Students know this without being told. After an exam, the first question is
often not what you got. It is what the average was. They already know that their
own score does not tell them their own box.

This is what the Latin word was doing in 1785. The system translated it and has
been using it ever since.

## Fifty-Two Years

In 1837 that same college turned those four words into numbers. Four at the top,
descending from there. There are historians who trace the grade point average now
in use back to it.

It took fifty-two years. What changed in between was not people. It was the way
of writing it down.

Written as words, it is hard to say which of three people is ahead. How far apart
best and second best are is not carried in the words. Written as numbers, it is
carried. A 4.0 and a 3.7 are 0.3 apart, and with that 0.3 you can stand two
people in a line. From the moment subtraction works, people queue.

In my second year of middle school I was number 39. Numbers were handed out in
order of height. There were five people behind me, and those five were big, and
they were the ones who played sports.

So that number had nothing to do with anything I had done. Studying harder would
not make me 38, and doing nothing at all would not make me 40. The number was
given to my height and I came along with it.

And looking at the five behind me I understood one more thing. **If those five
had not been in that class, I would have been last.** My height is the same and
the number is different. My number was not decided by me alone.

## Attach a Number and Comparison Follows by Itself

It is not that the people handing out numbers mean any harm. Numbers exist to get
work done, and someone who has to deal with fifty-eight people needs them. But
there is one thing numbers do that nobody asked them to do. Numbers compare
themselves to each other. Set 3 beside 7 and a distance appears between them, and
once there is a distance, which one is ahead comes out without being asked. That
is the nature of numbers.

So a person who receives a number receives two things. A mark that points at
them, and a distance to the person beside them. Nobody offered the second one. It
arrives anyway.

## Without a Number Nobody Can Find Me

Here is where I could not come down on one side. Is being given a number
necessarily bad?

Think about places with no numbers and it catches immediately. Without a number
at a hospital my turn never comes. Without my name on a list at school nobody
knows I am missing. People who are not counted are also not protected. In every
country, the people nobody counts live hardest.

So numbers are used to stand people in a line and also to find people. The same
device does both. I have not seen the line that separates the two uses, and this book is not the book that finds it; what is different is **knowing** that a
number is attached to you rather than not knowing. That difference is the part
this book can do.

## Today

Write down every number attached to you today. All of them, as they come. Age,
height, weight, salary, employee number, student number, scores, rank, credit
score, step count, how many people follow you, how many unread messages. Write
them out and two things show. There are more than you thought, and **almost none
of them is a number you chose.**

## Where This Chapter Comes From

The 1785 diary is Ezra Stiles's, president of Yale. The line dividing fifty-eight
students into four grades survives, and in 1837 Yale turned those grades into a
four-point scale. It is a passage often cited in the history of education, and
the bibliography that travels with it is Smallwood, *A Historical Study of
Academic Marks in American Colleges* (Harvard University Press, 1935).

I could open neither the diary nor Smallwood's book. This is public sources
cross-read against one another. The four Latin words and the number fifty-eight
come out the same in many places, but things that float around identically are not a source, and before publication someone should sit with the originals in a
library.

No numbers were written into the section about the school system I grew up in.
Which percentage falls in which band changes whenever the system changes, so writing it down would age it immediately, and only the structure was written down:
that your box is set by how many people are ahead of you. That structure does not
age.

One thing was left out. There is a far older system for sorting people into
grades in East Asia, more than a thousand years earlier. Putting it in would turn
this chapter into a history of institutions, and then the name in the bottom box
of those four words gets buried, and that one word is what this chapter is looking at.

The next chapter is about why it went that way. Seeing a person whole is hard, and
when something is hard the easy thing next to it takes its place.
