## A Book of Things to Do Already Exists

Up to here it was looking. From Part Three it is doing. And a book of things to
do already exists. It came out in 1936 and it still sells. It sets out how to
deal with people point by point and the points are mostly right. I am not writing
this chapter to refute it.

The third principle of its second part is this.

> A person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any
> language.

Remember names, use them, do not get them wrong. **This book recommends the same
thing.** That is what this chapter asks you to do as well.

## The Example He Attached to It

What changes the story is the example attached to the principle. The person the
book names ran a political campaign, claimed he could remember fifty thousand
names, and the book records that this ability put a president in office. That is
the evidence for the principle. Remember names and this becomes possible.

**The purpose of remembering was votes.** The book says so itself.

So there is no need to import anyone else's criticism. The book wrote it down,
and writing it down is the honest thing to have done. It says in its own title
that it will teach you to move people. It never hid anything.

Which leaves this. The action is the same and the work it does is different.

## Why a Name, of All Things

Put a different reason under the same action. Why should you say a person's name?
Not because they like it. They do like it, but that is a result, not a reason.

**A name is the only form of address with no connection to what a person does.**
Every other form hangs on something: a title stops working when the post is gone;
a professional form of address gets awkward once the person leaves. Every honorific
attaches to a role or a relation, so when that shifts, the word shakes with it.

It becomes clear if you recall the four people in Part Two. Someone who cannot do
anything yet, someone stopped for a while, someone handing things back one at a
time, someone skilled with nowhere to use it. For all four, a form of address came
off. The name stayed. It is the one thing that did not shrink. That is why you say
it.

## Where the Name Cannot Be Said

Something catches here immediately. In the language I write in, there are a great
many relationships in which a name cannot be said.

You do not say the name of someone older. No child says a parent's name and no
student says a teacher's. At work, people above you are addressed by title.
Saying the name is the moment it becomes rude, and the territory where that is
true is far wider than in English.

So transplanting this chapter looks useless. Some of its use really does shrink.
But what shrinks is replaced by something else becoming visible.

**The less a name can be said, the more is hanging on the form of address.**
Manager, teacher, ma'am, sir. all of these bind a person to what they are right
now, so when that changes there is nothing left to call them. Plenty of people
have stood there hesitating over what to call someone who retired. That
hesitation is what this chapter points at.

There are one or two usable forms. There is a polite particle that attaches to a
given name rather than a role. It is not complete; it is still awkward upward,
and awkwardness means that place is still empty.

So practising this chapter in my language is slightly different. Where a name can
be said, say it. Where it cannot, look once at what the person's form of address
is hanging on. Even that shows you something.

Here is my own case. The only people who call me by my name are family. Outside,
I am *teacher*; almost everyone uses that. It is not a rude form of address. It is
honorific, and I do not mind it.

What is hanging on it, though, becomes visible in the people who have left that post. A person who was *teacher* becomes *boss* afterward — the word applied to
any adult male whose place you cannot locate. Being applicable to anyone means it
points at no one.

## He Changed His Own Name

There is something to add. The man who wrote that book was born with a different
spelling. He later altered one letter to match the spelling of a famous
industrialist of the day, to whom he was neither related nor connected in any
way. It was simply a name people knew.

I have no interest in using this to diminish him. Diminishing is easy, and in
proportion to how easy it is, it leaves nothing. What the fact does tell us is one
thing. He understood better than anyone that a name is a useful object. He
understood it well enough to change his. In that world a name is capital too, and
if it is capital you trade up.

**That world is what this book is trying to leave.**

## Isn't It the Same on the Receiving End

Here is where I cannot tie the knot. Even if the reason differs, doesn't the
receiving side feel the same thing? A person whose name is said does not know what
the sayer was thinking. A name memorized for votes and a name memorized because it
is that person are both pleasant in the moment of being said. Then who is the
sorting of reasons for?

An answer presents itself: it is for the one saying it, because a different reason
makes the next action different: a name memorized for an election is of no use
after the election; a name memorized because of the person keeps getting used.
Plausible, and not entirely convincing. If the receiving side never finds out,
where does the difference live? I stood with that question a long time, and that
is as far as I got.

## Today

Say someone's name once more today. Not just before asking for something — when
there is nothing to ask at all. A name said because there is business is part of
the business; only a name said with no business arrives as a name. Once in
passing, once while greeting, and that is enough.

And try it with someone you only ever address by title. That is much harder, and
what this chapter is trying to say shows most clearly in the difficulty.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

Every fact in this chapter is inside that book. The year 1936, the third principle
of the second part, and the example attached to it. Open the book to the page and
it is confirmed. Only one line is quoted, because pulling in more would make this a
summary of someone else's book, and this chapter is not here to summarize it.

One thing could not be written. It is recorded in several places that a novelist of
the day left a fairly sharp remark about this book, and I could never find where
that remark was published. The words float around identically in many places, and
things that float around identically are not a source. So it is not used at all.
And the year he changed the spelling of his name differs between sources, so no
year is given.
