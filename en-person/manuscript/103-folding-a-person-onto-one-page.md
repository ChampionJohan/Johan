## If You Were Told to Write Yourself on One Page

Say you are handed a single sheet of paper. You have to write yourself onto it.
One sheet, and the boxes are already decided.

What would you write? And once you had written it, what would not be there?

The second question is this chapter.

## 1940, Five Million Sheets

During a war, one country had every foreign national living on its soil fill out
a single form. These were the fields on it.

> Name · date of birth · place of birth · nationality · sex · marital status ·
> race · height · weight · color of hair · color of eyes

More than five million were collected.

I have not found anything that shows better what happens when one person goes
onto one page. Five million people became those eleven boxes.

## Only Things You Cannot Choose Are on It

Look at the fields again and something catches. **Almost all eleven are things
the person could not choose.** Nobody chose their hair color. Nor eye color, nor
place of birth, nor race. Height is mostly not chosen, and weight is a little
different, but what that box asks for is today's figure.

So what is not on that sheet? Nothing the person did. Nothing they knew how to
do. Nothing about who they lived with or what they cared for. Not one thing they
chose. What the form wanted were the marks by which a person could be found
again, and for that, the things that do not change and cannot be chosen are the
most useful. The form did its job extremely well.

There is one thing my own resume has no box for. Purity. It is not that there is
no room to write it. There is no box because writing it down would not make it
believable. That is something you only know after time has passed.

In chapter two I wrote that I could not build a question that showed integrity in
thirty minutes. The same thing shows up here as a missing box. What will not go
onto one page and what will not show in thirty minutes are the same thing. Both of
them mean it takes time.

Paper does not hold time. That is the use of paper and the limit of paper.

## Thirty-Seven Years Later, Two Numbers

In 1977 that country's Supreme Court took a case. In one state, becoming a prison
guard required meeting a height and a weight standard. Five feet two inches, and
a hundred and twenty pounds. A twenty-two-year-old woman wanted to apply; she met
the height and weighed a hundred and ten. She was ten pounds short.

Of women, thirty-three percent were excluded by the height rule and twenty-two
percent by the weight rule before they could even apply, and the Court asked whether
those two numbers were necessary to do the job, found that they were not, and struck the standard down. A person had been folded into two numbers, and the two
numbers had nothing to do with the work. While the standard stood, though, they
worked as if they did.

## Fold It and Something Goes Inward

Between 1940 and 1977 is thirty-seven years. What changed in between was not
people. It was the paper. Folding has one property: fold something and an inside
is created. What goes inside does not disappear. It is still there and it cannot
be seen. In chapter thirteen the same thing gets said about a review form, and
the review form is a descendant of this sheet.

Which is why folding cannot be blamed. To hand along a person without folding
them you would have to show the whole person, and for that the receiving side
would have to live with them. You cannot do that five million times. **Without
folding, nobody gets transmitted at all.**

Where folding goes wrong is somewhere else. It goes wrong when the person holding
the folded sheet takes the sheet for the person. The moment what is not written
on the paper becomes **what is not there.**

## Is There a Way to Hand Someone Along Unfolded

This is where I did not reach the end.

I thought about ways to transmit a person without folding. More boxes? More boxes
means more places that fold. Let people write freely? Then people who write well
do better, and that is a ruler too. Meet and talk? That works up to a certain
number of people.

More plausible than getting rid of the paper is the person receiving the paper
remembering that there is an inside — but that cannot be called a method. It is a
posture. I held onto this for a long time wanting to write down one method, and I
do not think I will have an answer here even by the end of this book.

## In Short

| | The 1940 form | The 1977 standard |
|---|---|---|
| What was written | Eleven boxes, nearly all unchosen | Height and weight, two numbers |
| What was not | What they did, knew, chose | Whether they could do the job |
| The thirty-seven years between | What changed was the paper | |

## Today

Open your resume and write down **three things that are not on it.** By hand, in the blank space on the back, is fine, and one rule for choosing: leave out anything
that has a box and you simply did not fill in. That is just something unwritten.
You are looking for **things with no box to write them in.** Once all three are
down, you can see how much that sheet is saying about you.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

The 1940 form is the United States Alien Registration Form (AR-2), and the 1977
case is *Dothard v. Rawlinson* in the U.S. Supreme Court. The list of fields, the
two standards, and the thirty-three and twenty-two percent were confirmed at the
level of public sources cross-read against one another.

I could open neither the original form nor the opinion; the site hosting the
opinion was blocked from where I was working. The numbers are the spine of this
chapter and should be read in the opinion itself before publication.

Three things were cut. The story that 1950s resumes carried age, weight, parents'
origins and a photograph appears in many pieces of writing, but those pieces may
be copying one another, so it is out. When the resume became standard is sourced
only to company blog posts, so that is out too. And the frequent claim that a
letter of self-recommendation written by one man in 1482 was the world's first
resume is not used, because that letter is a different kind of paper from the one
this chapter is looking at.

Two more were noted and not used: a company's employee records from 1875 to 1946
with race written on them and photographs attached to some, and a newspaper's 1973
help-wanted section split into three columns. Using all four would turn this
chapter into a history of discrimination, and this book cannot be that book.

Part One ends here. We looked at when we began counting people, at why we count
the easy thing, and at what goes inward once the count is written down.

There is one way to see what went inward. Take away what is written on the
outside. Part Two is that, and there is not one piece of outside material in it.
