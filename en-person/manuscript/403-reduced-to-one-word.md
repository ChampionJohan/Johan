## When "Kind" Is Not a Compliment

Kind is a compliment. That is what we were taught and it is mostly true.

There is a place where the word is not used as one. A meeting ends and someone
says it: well, he's kind. The word is doing a job there. **It is deciding not to
give that person anything difficult.**

In the last chapter I wrote that the evaluations I received were words. Three of
them.

> Naive. Does not know how the world works. Kind.

None of the three is an insult. The first two are not compliments either. They
are words you cannot be sure which box to put in, and when you receive one you
learn quickly that the vague word is itself the box.

## The Good Words Hold You Tighter

A word written as a fault can be argued with. Call someone lazy and they can ask
when exactly they failed to do what, and if it is wrong they can say it is wrong.

A good word cannot be argued with. There is no answering *kind* by saying that I
am not kind. Try it and it comes out strange, and the person who said it feels
wronged, because they paid a compliment. A sentence that cannot be answered holds
you more firmly than one that can.

So the person given a good word stays longer where they were put. Work that takes
a long time goes to the one called steady, and the filing nobody wants goes to the
one called careful. The compliment turns into a roster.

## The Word Describes the Past and Decides the Future

*Naive* has the shape of a past tense. It sounds like a statement about how
things have gone so far.

What the word actually does is up ahead. **It decides what will not be given to
that person.** Anything with money on it, anything that requires an argument,
anything that requires a fight. Take those three away and what remains is fixed,
and after some years that person's history has been built in the shape of the
word.

At which point the word becomes true. It was not that the person was that way and
so ended up there; it is that being called that is what gathered them there — and
looking only at the result, the two cannot be told apart.

## There Are Three Things That Fold

In chapter three a single sheet of paper folded a person. In the last chapter it
was a review form. **This is the third, and it is the most common of them.**

| What folds | How often | Written down |
|---|---|---|
| One sheet of paper | Every few years | Yes |
| A review form | Once a year | Yes |
| One word | Nearly every day | No |

The third has no document. With no document there is nowhere to ask for a
correction, and no evidence the person ever heard it. Mostly it happens where
they are not.

In chapter three I wrote that the thing my resume has no box for is purity. The
same thing gets summarized here into one word. On one side there is no box to
write it in, and on this side it travels with no box at all.

I would still like to live purely.

## Some Summaries Are Necessary

Here is the wall in this chapter.

Summarizing is not always bad. The sentence *that person is dangerous, be careful*
has to travel in one word, and if it travels late someone gets hurt. Teaching a
child to tell people apart uses words too. Tell people not to use them and they
cannot speak.

There is a line somewhere between the summary that is needed and the summary that
confines, and all I have done is walk back and forth across it. It looks like the
same place as chapter two, where I could not separate a ruler still worth using
from one that has started eating what it measures, and knowing it is the same
place has not helped me draw it.

## Today

When you talk about someone today, **leave the adjectives out.** Drop kind,
careful, sensitive, and speak only about what the person did.

Try it and the sentences get longer and the whole thing gets uncomfortable. You
want to finish quickly and it will not finish. That discomfort tells you how much
work one word has been doing on your behalf.

And one more. Think of **someone you have reduced to a single word**, then try to
recall one thing they actually did last month. If nothing comes, the word has
taken over the whole space where that person was.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

There are no outside facts in this chapter. Only the three words the author
received and what he thought standing there.

It occurred to me that there is probably research measuring what being summarized
into one word does to a person over time, and the chapter did not need the number
to stand. Anyone who has received three words like those knows the story, and
attaching a figure would turn it into somebody else's story.

The passage saying a compliment turns into a roster is within what the author has
seen. How common it is, I do not know.

The next chapter is about strangers. Reducing someone to one word is still
something done to a person you know; the people we pass in a day do not even get
a word.
