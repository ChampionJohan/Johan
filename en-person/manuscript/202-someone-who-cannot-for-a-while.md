## The First Week, and a Month Later

I was badly hurt in an accident once. This is my own story. **In the first week
friends came.** A lot of them came. They came and said good things. It will work
out, you will be up soon. And see you later.

A month later only a few were left. Those few came and said the same thing. See
you later. The ones who stayed to the end were family. Family did not say much.
They did the nursing. They held the place. Over that time my body recovered and I
left the hospital.

Writing it down afterward, one thing caught. **The people who came in the first
week gave words and the people who stayed to the end did work, and both came out
of the same feeling, and one lasts a week and one lasts to the end.**

## The First Thing to Go

You would think that once you are ill and lying down, being ill is the biggest
thing, but watching from beside it, that is not quite so; the body hurts in its own way, and separately from that, something else drains out. Things to do. A
reason to get up in the morning, a schedule that puts you somewhere by a certain
hour, a place where someone is waiting. All of that is lifted away overnight.

Once it is lifted, something odd happens. The day gets longer, and in that long
day who you are goes dim. The body is the same and the name is the same, and only
now does it become visible that everything that made that person that person was
hanging on the outside.

## In the First Week People Come

**The first week and a month later are different.**

In the first week there is a lot of contact. Startled people ask after you, the
close ones visit, and even people who had drifted away get in touch once. In that
week the sick person is not alone.

A month later it goes quiet.

Calling this coldness is easy, and wrong in proportion to how easy it is. The
people who came did not turn bad. They had a reason to come and the reason ran
out. *How did it go?* has nothing left once it is asked. *Do you need anything?*
gets embarrassing after a couple of rounds. When the errands run out, people are
not sure what to bring next.

And right there the sick person learns something. That nearly all of the talk
around them had been about what they did. That the talk stopped when the doing
stopped.

## The One or Two That Do Not Shrink

Not everything shrinks, though. One or two remain.

Look at the ones who remain and they have one thing in common. They have no
errand. They do not ask, they do not offer to do something, they stop by and
leave. Some of them sit a long while without saying anything. They did not come on
an errand in the first place, so when the errands run out they come anyway.

**A relationship with an errand ends when the errand ends.** That is not the
difference between bad people and good people; it is a difference in what joined you at the start.

This is what a person learns while ill. Which of the things around them was
attached to them and which was attached to their work. Ordinarily you cannot tell,
because the two arrive together. They only separate when the work stops.

## About the Ones Who Stay

Here is where I have no answer.

I cannot blame the ones who thin out. It is true that after a month there is no
reason to come, and manufacturing a reason in order to go is strange in its own way, and the sick person is the first to notice that a visitor who forced themselves
to come is sitting less comfortably than the rest.

So the question turns into this. Why do people not know how to go without an
errand? Everyone managed it in front of a baby.

The stopping I wrote about in the last chapter does not work well here. I could
not find the reason. People who have gone without an errand say it is not hard,
and for people who have not, it is the hardest thing there is, and it looks like
something you could learn and it also looks like something learning does not
reach.

Chapter six is about the ones who can no longer do it at all, not for a while.
There the question gets bigger.

## Today

Get in touch with someone who has been ill a long time, **with no errand.**

Do not ask how they are. Do not ask what they need. Above all do not ask whether
they are getting better. Say you thought of them and got in touch. That is all of
it.

You will feel you cannot do it because you have nothing to say. It is correct
that you have nothing to say. The point where you believe you need something to
say in order to make contact is the thing this chapter is pointing at.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

There are no outside facts in this chapter either. That is true of all four
chapters in Part Two.

One thing should be written down. The sequence — they come in the first week and
not a month later — is something many people describe similarly, but I have not
measured it. I do not know how common it is or how long it takes. This chapter
does not claim that sequence as a general fact. It uses it as one case the author
saw.

I am not even sure it is measurable. Within what I searched, I found no study of
it.
