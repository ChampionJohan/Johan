## What Do You Ask a Stranger First

Picture it. You have ended up seated next to someone you do not know, you have
said hello, and now something has to be said. What do you ask first?

Almost everyone asks the same thing.

> So what do you do?

The question dissolves the awkwardness. That is why everyone uses it. I use it
too.

## Once They Are in a Box, the Rest Is Set

The moment the answer comes, the person goes into a box, and once the box is set
the rest of the conversation flows out of it. Teacher, and you get talk about
kids today. Running a shop, and you get talk about the economy. Taking a break
right now, and the conversation skips a beat. Plenty of people have felt that
skip. Both sides feel it.

**The first question you ask decides the next thirty minutes.** For thirty
minutes we talk about that box. Not about the person.

## The Problem Is That There Is No Bad Intent

There is no bad intent in the question. If anything it is interest. It is trying
to hand the other person a foothold, trying to make something to talk about. And
that interest asks about use first.

In chapter six there was the question of asking an old person what they used to
do. Because no usefulness is visible in them now, I wrote, we borrow some from the
past. *What do you do* is the same question in the present tense. It checks first
what you are currently useful as.

A kind question using the same ruler. That is the third time this shape appears in
Part Three. Remember names, listen to the end, ask what someone does — all of them
come from a good place and the measuring instrument is unchanged.

## What the Question Gives the Asker

Why is this question so comfortable? Because the asker gets something.

Knowing the other person's box settles how I am supposed to talk. How formal to
be, what subjects are allowed, how far a joke can go. Without that information I
cannot choose my next sentence. So I ask first.

Which makes this, before it is a question about them, **a question for my own
comfort.** It is used to switch off the discomfort that arises in front of a
stranger. Once it is off, conversation gets easy.

In my language there is one more thing in front of it. Age.

Not knowing someone's age, I cannot choose my words, because how far to raise the level of politeness is not yet settled, and so in a first meeting people work to find
out the age somehow. What year were you born, what was your entering class, what
animal year are you. Indirect routes to it are quite well developed.

Age and occupation. Both are used to stand a person up vertically. And both have
to be known before speech can come out. To talk with a stranger in my language you
have to decide first where to place them. The language itself is built that way.

This is not something that can be fixed. The language is what it is. But using it
knowingly and using it unknowingly are different.

I have seen someone's face change when I said what I do. It did not change for the
worse. It changed for the better — and whether for better or worse, the fact that
it visibly changes is what this chapter is looking at.

There is the other side too, when I was the one asking. I asked a stranger what
they did. They said they were out of work at the moment.

The one who stopped at that moment was not them. It was me. I could not find the
next sentence. It felt like anything would do and nothing came. The question I
threw got stuck on my own side.

**That person was probably used to that spot.** They will have given that answer
and watched the other person stall many times, and the one stalling stalls freshly every time; the one answering sees the same thing every time.

## Make a List and It Becomes a Technique

So what do you ask? This is the wall in this chapter. I tried to make a list of
replacement questions and stopped, because once it was made it was a technique. It
had turned into an object that reads as *ask these five things and people open up*,
and that returns to the book we parted from in chapter eight. Change the action,
leave the reason as it was.

So no list. Only this, written down.

> **Stop once before you ask.**
> Is what I am curious about this person, or this person's use?

I am not satisfied with that either. *Stop and look* is good for not being a
technique and, for the same reason, gives the hand nothing to hold. If I find
something better I will change it.

## In Short

| Question | What it does |
|---|---|
| "What did you used to do?" (ch. 6) | Borrows usefulness from the past |
| "What do you do?" | Checks present usefulness first |
| What they share | Asks the box before the person |

## Today

With the first person you meet today, **ask something other than their work
first.** Not the weather, not the traffic — something about them: how they came to be here, what they were doing before this, what they are enjoying most lately.

And you do not have to ask about work at all. See whether the conversation holds
without it. Mostly it does. That is what you are checking today.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

There are no outside facts in this chapter.

It is often said that what people ask a stranger first differs from country to
country, so I went looking for material. I could not find anything that actually
surveyed it. It is plausible and unsupported, and unsupported writing gets read as
fact. So it was cut whole.

That space is filled with two scenes the author lived. This is a chapter with no
case material, so if those two were empty the chapter would be generalities.
