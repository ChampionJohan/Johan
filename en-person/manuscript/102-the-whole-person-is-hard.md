## The Conclusion First

When the thing you meant to measure is hard, the easy thing standing next to it
takes its place.

How much a student has grown is hard to measure and a test score is easy. How
well a doctor saw a patient is hard to measure and how many minutes it took is easy, and how well a person does their work is hard to measure and how many cases
they closed is easy. So we measure the easy one. It is not that the hard one gets
abandoned. We measure the easy one while believing we are measuring the hard one.
In chapter one a number got attached to a person; this chapter looks at why it
was that number.

## A Sentence Written in 1976

Someone gave this thing a name. The year was 1976.

> The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making,
> the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be
> to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.

It reads as obvious, and there is one part of it that is not. **The reason an
indicator corrupts is that it became important.** An indicator nobody uses does
not corrupt. It starts corrupting the moment it starts being used. So this is not
a problem you solve by choosing a better indicator, because if the one you chose is good enough to be widely used, the fact of being widely used is what breaks it.

## Four Hours

One country set a target: anyone arriving at an emergency department should be
dealt with inside four hours. It is a good target. If someone waited eight hours
in an emergency department, that needs fixing.

Things happened after the target existed.

People were admitted at three hours fifty-eight minutes. Whether admission was
the right call became the second question. Ambulances were held outside the
building, because a patient who has not yet *arrived* does not start the clock, and during counting periods, surgeries in other departments were postponed and staff
were pulled across to the emergency department.

The numbers improved. What the numbers were meant to measure got worse.

## Counting Erasures

A different country, a different field. In 2009 a local newspaper found
unexplained rises in that state's school test scores. For two schools the odds of
rising that much were smaller than one in a billion. So the state went back to
the answer sheets. What it counted was erasure marks.

It counted, classroom by classroom, how often a wrong answer had been erased and
changed to a right one, and flagged any classroom more than three standard
deviations above the state average for the same grade and subject. Class size was
adjusted for.

This is the middle of the chapter. The score was easy to measure, so the score got
changed. To catch that, measuring the score was no longer enough; **the marks left
by changing the score** had to be measured.

> Measure the easy thing, and to catch what that does you have to measure again.

## Why It Went That Way

In 2011 a special investigation in that state issued a report. It runs to about
eight hundred pages. More than a hundred and seventy people were implicated,
across forty-four schools. The report names one cause. Targets set higher than
anyone could reach, and the humiliation of not reaching them. People who tried to
report it were retaliated against, and the investigators described the atmosphere
as fear and intimidation.

What happened after that is not in this chapter. Write down who ended up where
and the sentence a reader remembers becomes that sentence, and that sentence covers what the chapter is trying to say, so this chapter looks as far as why the
people in that position did what they did.

I once sat on an interview panel. A school was selecting people and I was on the
asking side. What I wanted to see was integrity and diligence. I thought nothing
mattered more than those two in the person who would come, and I still think so.

What I actually looked at that day was ability and intelligence. The questions
went that way and the boxes I was writing in were built that way. Ability and
intelligence are reasonably visible in thirty minutes. I never managed to build a
question that showed me integrity in thirty minutes.

I could not build it, so I did not ask; I did not ask, so I did not measure; and
because I did not measure, integrity in that room may as well not have existed.

## The Two Are Not the Same Thing

Set those two cases side by side and they do different work.

The four-hour case is a target that bent **behavior.** The numbers were recorded
honestly. People moved differently in order to make those numbers true.

The erasure case is a target that bent **the number itself.** There was no room
to change the behavior, so the record was changed.

The second looks like the worse thing, and in this chapter the first is the more
frightening one. Nobody wrote down anything false and the thing being measured
was ruined anyway. There is no one to hold responsible. Everyone followed the
rules.

## Is Measuring the Easy Thing Always Bad

This is where my own argument runs out.

Measuring the easy thing is useful. If fewer people waited eight hours after the
emergency department started timing, that is something the number did. There are
many places where nothing changes unless it is measured, and if you measure
nothing at all in the name of seeing people whole, the weakest person is pushed
out first. It is the same shape as the end of chapter one.

So how far is a ruler still worth using? At what point does the ruler start eating
the thing it was built to measure? I never saw the end of it. I drew the line
several times while writing, and each time a counterexample followed the line
out.

## In Short

| What was meant to be measured | What actually gets measured |
|---|---|
| How much a student grew | Test scores |
| Whether the patient was seen well | How many minutes it took |
| Whether someone is good at the work | How many cases closed |
| Once the number stops being trusted | The marks left by changing the number |

## Today

Write down, separately, **what you measure** and what you meant to measure. Fold
a sheet in half: on the left what you actually count, on the right what you
wanted in the first place.

If the two sides match, you work somewhere good. If they do not, look at how far
apart they are. Far apart is the common case, and a person who knows the distance
trusts the left-hand column less. That alone changes how they work.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

The 1976 sentence is Donald Campbell's and is commonly known by his name; the four-hour emergency target is a British case, and the 2009 scores and 2011 report
concern schools in Atlanta, Georgia, in the United States.

I could not open any of the three originals. This is public sources cross-read
against one another, and for the report I confirmed only that it runs to roughly
eight hundred pages. The erasure criteria — three standard deviations, adjusted
for class size — are the spine of this chapter and should be read in the report
itself before publication.

Two things were left out: the people prosecuted over it and how the trials went,
and who held which post. They are vivid and they stick, which is why they are not
here. It is not that they are untrue. They were cut to keep this chapter from
becoming a book about school systems. This book cannot be that book.

The next chapter is about paper. To put a person on one sheet you have to fold
them, and folding always sends something inward.
