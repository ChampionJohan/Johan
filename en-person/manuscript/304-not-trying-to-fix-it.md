## That Time

Someone brought up something hard. What they said was three short things.

> It hurts. It is hard. I do not know what to do.

I said this.

> This time will pass too. A day will come, far off, when you can tell this story
> and smile.

It is true. I believe it. I still believe it. It is true that it passes and true
that the day comes when you can tell it smiling.

But there is no chance that what that person had to say ended at those three
things. They got three in and then listened to me.

## The Eye Inside the Wish to Fix

The wish to fix is a good impulse. It means spending attention on someone else's
trouble, and spending it costs something. People do not do it for just anyone. But
there is an eye inside that impulse. **An eye that sees a thing as broken.**

A broken thing can be fixed. From the fixing side it becomes a task, and once it
is a task, the person becomes a task. A task that costs effort and attention, but
a task. Which produces something strange: while you are working hard at fixing,
the person in front of you gradually stops being visible, and what is visible is a
problem to be solved.

## The Speed at Which a Solution Arrives

The last chapter had the eighteen seconds — the place where listening turns into
processing arrives earlier than you think. The same thing happens here, and here
it is faster.

When someone opens a hard story, a solution arrives almost immediately. By their
third sentence one is already up. From then on the remaining job is waiting for
them to finish, and while waiting you listen less, because you are polishing what
you will say.

So the moment a solution arrives is the moment listening ends. The words keep
coming in and the listening has stopped. And in most cases the solution is not even
wrong. It would be easier if it were wrong. Because it is right you say it,
because it is right they cannot argue, and because they cannot argue the
conversation is over.

## Why They Are Not Grateful

It is common to feel let down after fixing something. You said a good thing out of
good feeling and their face does not open. It is because what they were looking
for was not an answer.

A person looking for an answer asks for one. They say *what should I do.* If that
did not come, they were not after an answer, and what they were after instead is
often not known to them either — but one thing is clear. **They wanted to finish
telling it.** A story told to the end and a story handed an answer midway become
different stories, and what is left when they remember it later is different.

## Whose Discomfort Gets Switched Off

Here is where this overlaps with the chapter before.

*What do you do* was written as a question that switches off the asker's
discomfort. The solution is the same.

Listening to someone's hard story makes the listener uncomfortable. It feels like
sitting there doing nothing, and it is hard to bear that they are suffering and I am still, and the fastest way to switch off that discomfort is to do something, and the
thing available in that moment is a solution.

So here is what happens the instant a solution comes out. Before their problem is
solved, **my discomfort goes off first.** And once it is off, I am a person who
did something.

That is the third time this shape appears in Part Three. Remembering a name,
asking what someone does, fixing — all of them came from a good place and all of them return something to the giver, and there is nothing wrong with good acts returning something. But looking at **whose discomfort** that return switches off
shows you who the act was aimed at.

That is also why listening alone is hard. Your own discomfort has to stay on.

## So Do You Just Sit There

This is the line I could not draw in this chapter. Where not fixing separates from
leaving someone to it, I do not know.

There really are people who need help. Only listening and doing nothing is
choosing the easy side. Listening is sometimes easier, and you can pick the easy
thing and call it respect. Conversely, not putting out a hand where a hand is
needed is neglect. The line is somewhere between, and it moves from person to
person and day to day. I have one guess: not doing it until asked is wrong less
often. It is a guess, so I will not write it down as a rule.

## Today

If someone tells you something hard today, **go to the end without saying the
solution.** You cannot stop one from arriving, so let it arrive and just keep it
behind your teeth. Only until they have finished.

You do not have to produce it afterward either. If *what should I do* comes, say it
then; if it does not, do not. Try it and your hands will itch, and the itch is
what this chapter is pointing at.

## Where This Chapter Comes From

There are no outside facts in this chapter. Only what the author saw and thought
while standing there.

One thing to note. *The moment a solution arrives, listening ends* has the same
shape as what the study in chapter nine showed, but that study does not support this chapter, because it was measured in an examining room and this is ordinary talk
between people. Same shape is not the same thing.

Part Three ends here. Having read four chapters, one thing will catch. Everything
written here is done toward other people, and the place it fails hardest is in
front of the person closest to you. Part Four is about that.
